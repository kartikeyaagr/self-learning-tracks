## The situation

Instagram launched on the iPhone in October 2010. About 25,000 people signed up on the first day, it passed 1 million users within about two months, and by late 2011 it had roughly 14 million users. Through that period the engineering team was famously tiny: two or three engineers for most of the first year. When Facebook bought the company in April 2012, it had about 13 employees and over 30 million users.

In late 2011 the team published a description of the stack that carried them to roughly 14 million users. Its message was that a tiny team could serve millions of users **because** the technology choices were boring, not in spite of it. This case study looks at what those choices were, why they worked, and the one genuinely hard problem they solved themselves: sharding PostgreSQL and generating IDs.

~~~dg-timeline Instagram's early growth against the size of its engineering team.
Oct 2010 | Launch on iPhone, 25,000 sign-ups on day one
Dec 2010 | 1 million users
Jun 2011 | 5 million users
Late 2011 | *About 14 million users, 3 engineers
Apr 2012 | Android launch; acquired with 30M+ users
~~~

## The principles

The team followed three rules:

1. **Keep it very simple.**
2. **Do not reinvent the wheel.**
3. **Go with proven and solid technologies** when you can.

The reasoning is about people, not machines. With three engineers, every bespoke system is something only one person understands, and that person must fix it at 3 a.m. Off-the-shelf tools come with documentation, community knowledge, known failure modes and other people who have already hit the bugs. Every hour spent building infrastructure was an hour not spent on the product, and the product was growing faster than anyone could hire.

This is the same principle as lesson 01.13 ("when not to add infrastructure"), seen from the other side: Instagram added infrastructure only when a measured problem demanded it, and when it did, it chose the most familiar tool that solved the problem.

## The stack

~~~dg-flow Instagram around 2011: boring parts, each doing one job.
[Web] App -(HTTPS)-> Load balancer -(nginx)-> Django app servers
[Data] Django app servers -(sharded)-> *PostgreSQL + replicas -(cache)-> memcached
[Media] App -(photo upload)-> Amazon S3 -(served via)-> CloudFront CDN
[Async] Django app servers -(tasks)-> Queue workers -(feeds)-> Redis
~~~

- **Hosting.** Everything ran on Amazon EC2 with Ubuntu Linux. No data centre, no hardware purchasing, no racks. When a server died, they started another.
- **Front end.** Amazon's Elastic Load Balancer in front of nginx, then **Django** (Python) app servers running under a WSGI server. The app servers were **stateless**: no user session lived only in one server's memory. Adding capacity meant adding identical machines, and losing one meant nothing.
- **Data.** **PostgreSQL** held users, photo metadata, likes, comments and follows. As it grew, the data was **sharded** across several PostgreSQL servers, each with a **streaming replica** (a second server that continuously applies the primary's write-ahead log) for failover. They used EBS volumes with software RAID for throughput and filesystem snapshots for backups.
- **Photos.** Stored in **Amazon S3**, already terabytes of them, and served through **CloudFront**, Amazon's CDN. The app servers never served or stored image bytes.
- **Redis.** The main **feed** (a list of photo IDs per user, pushed when someone they follow posts) and sessions: data that is small, hot and list-shaped, where Redis's in-memory lists are a natural fit.
- **memcached.** General caching in front of PostgreSQL.
- **Task queue.** A pool of workers took anything slow off the request path: fanning a new photo out to followers' feeds, sharing to Twitter and Facebook, sending notifications.
- **Push notifications** through Apple's push service.
- **Monitoring.** Error tracking (Sentry), system graphs (Munin), external uptime checks (Pingdom) and paging (PagerDuty).

~~~dg-layers The early Instagram stack, from the user's phone down to storage.
iPhone app | uploads photos, reads feed
Load balancer and nginx | spreads traffic, terminates HTTP
*Django app servers | stateless, scale by adding machines
Redis and memcached | feeds, sessions, cached rows
PostgreSQL shards | users, photos, likes, follows
S3 and CloudFront | photo bytes, served from the edge
~~~

## The numbers behind the choices

<div class="ex">
<div class="lab">Worked example</div>

**why S3 and a CDN were non-negotiable**

Suppose 14 million users, 2 million daily actives each viewing 100 photos a day: 200 million photo views a day, about 2,300 per second on average and perhaps 10,000 at peak.

At around 100 KB per photo, that is 1 GB per second at peak, about 8 Gbit/s. Serving that from a handful of EC2 app servers would have needed many more machines and constant attention.

Through CloudFront, those bytes never touch the app servers. The Django tier only returns small JSON documents with photo URLs, perhaps 2 KB per feed page, so the same peak costs the app tier about 20 MB per second instead of 1 GB.

<div class="ex">

**why the feed lived in Redis**

Each user's feed is a list of the latest few hundred photo IDs. At 8 bytes per ID and, say, 300 IDs, that is 2.4 KB per user; 14 million users need about 34 GB, which fits in the memory of one or two large instances in 2011.

Reading a feed page is one `LRANGE` returning 30 IDs in well under a millisecond, followed by a memcached multi-get for the photo rows. The same query in PostgreSQL ("photos by anyone I follow, newest first") would join follows and photos across shards on every app open.

## Sharding and IDs: the one hard problem

Everything above is assembling existing parts. The genuinely hard problem was the database. By 2011 the PostgreSQL data no longer fitted comfortably on one machine, and writes (new photos, likes, comments) were growing fast. They needed to split data across many servers, which raises a question that is easy to underestimate: **how do you generate IDs once data lives on many servers?**

With one database, an auto-increment column hands out 1, 2, 3, and so on. With many, each server's counter would hand out the same numbers. Their requirements for a new scheme:

- IDs **sortable by time**, so a list of photo IDs is already in time order without fetching timestamps. Feeds and profile pages depend on this.
- **64 bits**, so IDs fit efficiently in Redis, in indexes and in every language's integer type.
- **No new moving parts**: no additional service that three engineers would have to keep alive.

~~~dg-compare The ID schemes Instagram considered. Generating IDs inside PostgreSQL won on "no new moving parts".
[UUIDs]
App -> Random 128-bit ID
- Twice the size
- Not time-ordered
[Ticket server]
App -(next ID)-> Central DB
- Extra service to run
- Single point of failure
[Snowflake]
App -> ID service + ZooKeeper
- Two more systems to operate
[*In-shard function]
Insert -> PostgreSQL function
+ Time-sortable, 64 bits
+ Nothing new to run
~~~

They rejected:

- **UUIDs**: 128 bits (twice the size in every index and cache) and, in their random form, not time-ordered, so sorting needs a separate timestamp.
- **A central ticket server** (Flickr's approach, lesson 03.09): a dedicated database whose only job is handing out increasing numbers. One more service to keep up, and if it is down, nobody can post.
- **Twitter's Snowflake** (lesson 04.10): a separate ID service plus ZooKeeper to coordinate machine IDs. Elegant, but more infrastructure to run for a three-person team.

Their answer used PostgreSQL itself.

- **Logical shards.** They created thousands of **logical shards**, each a PostgreSQL **schema** (a namespace with its own copy of the tables), and spread them over a few physical servers. A user's data lives in one logical shard, chosen from the user ID. When a physical server gets too full, some of its schemas are moved to a new server. Moving a schema is a copy of a self-contained set of tables, not a re-split of rows, and the mapping from logical shard to physical server is a small table in configuration.
- **IDs generated inside each shard** by a PL/pgSQL function used as the column default, packing 64 bits as:

| Bits | Contents |
| --- | --- |
| 41 | milliseconds since a custom epoch (2^41 ms is about 69 years) |
| 13 | logical shard ID (up to 8,192 shards) |
| 10 | per-shard sequence, mod 1024 (1,024 IDs per shard per millisecond) |

Any ID tells you **when** it was made and **which shard** holds it, with no lookup table. Lesson 04.08 covers this layout alongside the alternatives.

~~~dg-tree Logical shards are schemas spread over a few physical servers. Moving a shard means moving one schema.
Shard map
  Physical server 1
    Schema shard 0000
    Schema shard 0001
    more schemas
  Physical server 2
    Schema shard 2048
    *Schema shard 1341 | user 31341 lives here
  Physical server 3
~~~

<div class="ex">
<div class="lab">Worked example</div>

**decoding an ID**

A photo is inserted on logical shard 1341 at 12:00:00.123 on 1 December 2011, and the shard's sequence gives 5,000. Taken mod 1024, the sequence part is 5,000 - 4 x 1,024 = 904.

If the custom epoch was 1 January 2011, about 334 days earlier, the timestamp part is 334 x 86,400,000 + 43,200,123 ms = about 28.9 billion ms, which needs 35 bits, well within 41.

The ID is (timestamp shifted left 23 bits) OR (1341 shifted left 10 bits) OR 904. Reading it back, the app shifts right 10 bits and masks 13 bits to get shard 1341, looks up which physical server holds shard 1341, and queries it directly. No directory service, no extra round trip.

</div>

### Limits worth knowing

- 1,024 IDs per shard per millisecond is about 1 million inserts per second per shard, far beyond what one shard needed.
- Clock problems: if a server's clock jumps backwards, IDs may be generated out of order or, in a narrow case, could collide with earlier ones on the same shard. The per-shard sequence reduces the risk, and NTP keeps clocks close (lesson 04.11).
- 8,192 logical shards is the ceiling; choosing thousands up front, far more than the number of physical servers, is what makes later rebalancing cheap.

## Why the boring choices worked

- **Managed infrastructure** (EC2, S3, CloudFront, load balancers) meant no hardware and no storage clusters to run.
- **Stateless app servers** made scaling the web tier a matter of adding instances.
- **One well-understood database**, sharded in a simple, explicit way, instead of a newer distributed database nobody knew how to debug at 3 a.m.
- **Media outside the app** (S3 plus a CDN) kept the heaviest traffic off their servers entirely.
- **Async work in a queue** kept requests fast and made traffic spikes survivable: work piled up in the queue instead of timing out requests.
- **Each tool did one job**: PostgreSQL for durable relational data, Redis for hot list-shaped data, memcached for caching.
- **Monitoring from day one**, so a small team found problems before users told them.

## What it did not do

It is equally instructive what is absent: no microservices, no custom storage engine, no service mesh, no multi-region active-active setup, no Kubernetes (which did not exist yet), no event-sourcing framework. Those solve problems of large organisations and extreme scale. Instagram had neither yet, and adding them would have consumed the team.

## Common misreadings

- "Boring means unscalable." The stack scaled to tens of millions of users and the same core (Django, PostgreSQL, Cassandra later for some data) stayed for years.
- "They never built anything custom." They did, exactly where it mattered: the sharding scheme and ID function. The skill is choosing where to be clever.
- "Use exactly these tools." The lesson is the selection rule, not the list. Today the equivalent might be a managed PostgreSQL, a managed Redis and a serverless queue.

## In an interview

When asked to design something for a small team or an early-stage product, say this out loud: stateless app tier, one relational database with replicas, a cache, object storage and a CDN for media, a queue for slow work, and monitoring. Add sharding only when the numbers say so, and describe how (logical shards, IDs that encode the shard). Then name what you are deliberately not adding.
