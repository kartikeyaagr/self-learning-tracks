It is also often the wrong tool, so the first skill is knowing when to use it.</p>\n<h3>Three ways to change a model&#39;s behaviour</h3>\n<div class=\"tbl\"><table>\n<thead>\n<tr>\n<th>approach</th>\n<th>what changes</th>\n<th>cost</th>\n<th>good for</th>\n</tr>\n</thead>\n<tbody><tr>\n<td><strong>Prompting</strong></td>\n<td>nothing; you change the input</td>\n<td>minutes</td>\n<td>most tasks, especially first attempts</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td><strong>Retrieval (RAG)</strong></td>\n<td>nothing; you add documents to the input</td>\n<td>hours</td>\n<td>facts that change, private documents</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td><strong>Fine-tuning</strong></td>\n<td>the weights</td>\n<td>hours to days</td>\n<td>a consistent format, style or skill; a smaller, cheaper model</td>\n</tr>\n</tbody></table></div>\n<h3>The idea in plain words</h3>\n<p>Pre-training gives a model broad knowledge and skills. Fine-tuning mostly teaches it <strong>how to behave</strong>: which format to use, which tone, which kind of answer, which narrow skill to apply. It is much weaker at adding large amounts of <strong>new knowledge</strong>, and it can make the model forget some of what it knew.</p>\n<h3>Worked example 1: a support bot</h3>\n<p>A company wants answers about its product in a fixed, friendly format.</p>\n<ul>\n<li>The product facts change monthly → <strong>retrieval</strong>, so updates do not need retraining.</li>\n<li>The format and tone are fixed → <strong>prompting</strong> first; <strong>fine-tune</strong> only if prompting is unreliable or too long (and therefore expensive per call).</li>\n</ul>\n<h3>Worked example 2: a cheap specialist</h3>\n<p>You need 10 million classifications a day. A large model does it well with a long prompt, but costs too much. Fine-tuning a 1 B-parameter model on 20,000 examples labelled by the large model can match it on this one task at a fraction of the cost per call. This is <strong>distillation</strong> into a specialist, a very common and sensible use.</p>\n<h3>Worked example 3: how much data?</h3>\n<p>The LIMA paper fine-tuned a 65 B model on only 1,000 carefully chosen examples and got a strong chat assistant. For format and style, hundreds to a few thousand high-quality examples often suffice. For a new skill (say, a new programming language), expect tens of thousands or more, and expect gains to be limited by what the base model already knows.</p>\n<h3>What fine-tuning can cost you</h3>\n<p><strong>Forgetting.</strong> Training on a narrow task can reduce general abilities. Biderman et al. (2024) found that LoRA (next lesson) forgets less than full fine-tuning, and also learns less: a real trade-off. Always re-check a few general benchmarks after fine-tuning.</p>\n<h3>Common mistakes</h3>\n<ul>\n<li><strong>Fine-tuning to add facts</strong> that could simply go in the prompt or a retrieval index.</li>\n<li><strong>Skipping the prompting baseline.</strong> You need it to show fine-tuning helped at all.</li>\n<li><strong>Low-quality data.</strong> The model learns your mistakes as faithfully as your good examples.</li>\n<li><strong>Only measuring the target task.</strong> Measure what you might have broken too.</li>\n</ul>\n<h3>Try it yourself</h3>\n<p>For each, choose prompting, retrieval or fine-tuning, and say why:</p>\n<ol>\n<li>Answer questions about a 500-page internal handbook that changes weekly.</li>\n<li>Always reply in a specific JSON schema, at very high volume, with a small model.</li>\n<li>Write in the style of your company&#39;s brand voice for a few dozen marketing emails.</li>\n</ol>\n<details><summary>Answers</summary><ol>\n<li>Retrieval: large, changing knowledge.</li>\n<li>Fine-tuning: a fixed format, many calls, and a small model to keep costs down.</li>\n<li>Prompting with a few examples first; the volume is small and the style can be shown in the prompt.</li>\n</ol>\n</details>\n</section><details class=\"deeper\"><summary>Go deeper: the original, more advanced notes (1 section)</summary><section class=\"art\"><h2>Should you fine-tune at all?</h2><p class=\"cite\"><span class=\"lab\">Based on</span> Fanout 10.01 · Thinking Machines, LoRA Without Regret · Biderman et al., LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less</p><p>The checkpoint for this lesson is a stakeholder asking you to fine-tune a model on 300 support tickets.
