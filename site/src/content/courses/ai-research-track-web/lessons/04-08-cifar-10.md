CIFAR-10 (60,000 colour images, 32×32, in 10 classes such as cat, ship and truck) is where simple models start failing and techniques like data augmentation, batch normalisation and longer training actually matter. It is still small enough to train on a free Colab GPU in minutes, which makes it the standard sandbox for vision research ideas.</p>\n<h3>The idea in plain words</h3>\n<ul>\n<li>Each image is 32×32×3: 3,072 numbers, red, green and blue for each pixel.</li>\n<li>Objects appear at different positions, sizes, colours and backgrounds. A cat is a cat whether it is on the left or the right, light or dark.</li>\n<li><strong>Data augmentation</strong> creates new training images from old ones by changes that do not change the label: random crops, horizontal flips, small colour shifts. The model sees a slightly different version of each image every epoch, so it cannot memorise them.</li>\n</ul>\n<h3>Worked example 1: how much augmentation adds</h3>\n<p>Hvass-Labs takes a random A misconfigured model usually still trains, just worse, and nothing throws an error. His recipe is a discipline for catching those failures early. Read it twice; this is a condensed version.</p>\n<ol>\n<li><strong>Become one with the data.</strong> Look at hundreds of examples before writing model code. Check label noise, duplicates, class balance, image sizes, outliers. Your eyes catch problems that metrics hide.</li>\n<li><strong>Set up the end-to-end skeleton with a dumb baseline.</strong> Fix the random seed. Verify the initial loss (for <span class=\"katex\"><span class=\"katex-html\" aria-hidden=\"true\"><span class=\"katex-base\"><span class=\"katex-strut\" style=\"height:0.6833em;\"></span><span class=\"mord mathnormal\" style=\"margin-right:0.0715em;\">K</span></span></span></span> balanced classes it should be <span class=\"katex\"><span class=\"katex-html\" aria-hidden=\"true\"><span class=\"katex-base\"><span class=\"katex-strut\" style=\"height:0.6944em;\"></span><span class=\"mop\">ln</span><span class=\"mspace\" style=\"margin-right:0.1667em;\"></span><span class=\"mord mathnormal\" style=\"margin-right:0.0715em;\">K</span></span></span></span>). Initialise the final-layer bias to match the class prior. Add a human or trivial baseline. Overfit <strong>one batch</strong> to near-zero loss; if you cannot, there is a bug. Visualise exactly what goes into the network, right before <code>model(x)</code>.</li>\n<li><strong>Overfit.</strong> Get a model large enough to drive training loss down. Do not be a hero: copy the architecture from the most related paper. Adam at 3e-4 is a safe start.</li>\n<li><strong>Regularise.</strong> Now trade training loss for validation loss: more data (best), augmentation, pretrained weights, smaller input, dropout, weight decay, early stopping.</li>\n<li><strong>Tune.</strong> Random search over hyperparameters beats grid search, because only a few dimensions usually matter.</li>\n<li><strong>Squeeze out the juice.</strong> Ensembles, and simply training longer.</li>\n</ol>\n<p>Goodfellow et al. ch. 11 reach the same recipe from the textbook side. Their process is: decide what metric matters and what level is good enough; build an end-to-end system quickly; instrument it to find which component limits performance; then make incremental changes, each justified by what the instrumentation showed. §11.5 lists debugging tests that are worth memorising because each catches a different class of bug:</p>\n<ul>\n<li><strong>Visualise the model in action.</strong> Look at predictions, not only at accuracy. A detector with 95% &quot;accuracy&quot; might be boxing the wrong objects.</li>\n<li><strong>Visualise the worst mistakes.</strong> Sort validation examples by loss. The top of the list is often mislabelled data or a preprocessing bug.</li>\n<li><strong>Reason from training and test error together</strong> (below).</li>\n<li><strong>Fit a tiny dataset.</strong> If a model cannot memorise 10 examples, it has a bug, not a capacity problem.</li>\n<li><strong>Compare backprop to finite differences</strong> (Lesson 01.08) for any hand-written gradient.</li>\n<li><strong>Monitor histograms of activations and gradients.</strong> Dead ReLUs, saturated tanh units and exploding gradients show up here before they show up in the loss (Lesson 05.01).</li>\n</ul>\n<h3>Reading training curves</h3>\n<p>Goodfellow et al. §5.2 frame generalisation as two quantities to drive down: the training error, and the <strong>gap</strong> between training and test error. A model <strong>underfits</strong> when the training error is too high and <strong>overfits</strong> when the gap is too large. d2l.ai §5.5 adds the warning that the gap depends on data size as well Its useful ideas:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>Separate hyperparameters into <strong>scientific</strong> (the thing you are studying), <strong>nuisance</strong> (must be re-tuned for a fair comparison, such as learning rate) and <strong>fixed</strong>.</li>\n<li>Choose batch size for throughput, then re-tune the learning rate, since the best learning rate depends on batch size.</li>\n<li>Change one thing at a time and keep a log.
