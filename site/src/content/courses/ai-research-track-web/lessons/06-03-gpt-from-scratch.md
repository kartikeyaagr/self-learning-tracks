Tokenization is the step everyone wants to skip and the one that shapes what the model can see.</p>\n<h3>Why bytes, and why merges</h3>\n<p>Text is first encoded as UTF-8 bytes, a sequence over an alphabet of 256 symbols. Training directly on bytes gives very long sequences, and attention cost grows with <span class=\"katex\"><span class=\"katex-html\" aria-hidden=\"true\"><span class=\"katex-base\"><span class=\"katex-strut\" style=\"height:0.8141em;\"></span><span class=\"mord\"><span class=\"mord mathnormal\" style=\"margin-right:0.1389em;\">T</span><span class=\"msupsub\"><span class=\"vlist-t\"><span class=\"vlist-r\"><span class=\"vlist\" style=\"height:0.8141em;\"><span style=\"top:-3.063em;margin-right:0.05em;\"><span class=\"pstrut\" style=\"height:2.7em;\"></span><span class=\"katex-sizing reset-size6 size3 mtight\"><span class=\"mord mtight\">2</span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span>. Character or word vocabularies have other problems: words give an open vocabulary with unknown tokens, characters are still long.</p>\n<p><strong>Byte-pair encoding (BPE)</strong> compresses the byte sequence by repeatedly merging the most frequent adjacent pair into a new token:</p>\n<ol>\n<li>Start with the 256 byte tokens.</li>\n<li>Count every adjacent pair in the training text.</li>\n<li>Merge the most frequent pair into a new token id (256, 257, ...), replacing every occurrence.</li>\n<li>Repeat until the vocabulary reaches the target size.</li>\n</ol>\n<p>A worked example on the string <code>aaabdaaabac</code> (alphabet a, b, c, d):</p>\n<div class=\"tbl\"><table>\n<thead>\n<tr>\n<th>Step</th>\n<th>Most frequent pair</th>\n<th>New token</th>\n<th>Sequence</th>\n</tr>\n</thead>\n<tbody><tr>\n<td>0</td>\n<td></td>\n<td></td>\n<td><code>a a a b d a a a b a c</code> (11 tokens)</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>1</td>\n<td><code>aa</code> (4 times, counting overlaps)</td>\n<td>Z = aa</td>\n<td><code>Z a b d Z a b a c</code> (9)</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>2</td>\n<td><code>ab</code> (2)</td>\n<td>Y = ab</td>\n<td><code>Z Y d Z Y a c</code> (7)</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>3</td>\n<td><code>ZY</code> (2)</td>\n<td>X = ZY</td>\n<td><code>X d X a c</code> (5)</td>\n</tr>\n</tbody></table></div>\n<p>Three merges shrank 11 symbols to 5, and the vocabulary grew from 4 to 7. Real BPE does the same over gigabytes of bytes until it has learned tens of thousands of merges. Sennrich et al. introduced BPE for machine translation for the same reason d2l.ai §15.6 gives: it handles rare and unseen words by breaking them into known pieces, so the vocabulary is closed (everything can be encoded) while common words still get a single token.</p>\n<p>Encoding new text applies the learned merges <strong>in the order they were learned</strong>, always picking the earliest-learned merge available. Decoding maps each token back to its bytes and decodes UTF-8 (with <code>errors=&quot;replace&quot;</code>, because a token sequence can split a multi-byte character).</p>\n<h3>The details that matter</h3>\n<ul>\n<li><strong>Pre-tokenisation.</strong> GPT-2 first splits text with a regex into chunks (words, numbers, punctuation, whitespace runs) and only merges within chunks. Without this, BPE learns tokens like &quot;dog.&quot; and &quot;dog!&quot; separately.</li>\n<li><strong>Special tokens</strong> such as <code>&lt;|endoftext|&gt;</code> are added outside the merge process and handled before BPE runs.</li>\n<li><strong>Vocabulary size trade-off.</strong> Larger vocabularies shorten sequences but enlarge the embedding and output matrices and make each token rarer in training. GPT-2 uses 50,257; Llama 3 uses about 128,000.</li>\n</ul>\n<p>A rough calibration: GPT-2&#39;s tokenizer averages about 4 characters, or 0.75 words, per token on English text, and many more tokens per word for languages that were rare in its training data.
