## What a search system does
A search system takes a short, vague query, usually two or three words, and returns a ranked list of the documents most likely to satisfy it. It does this in tens of milliseconds, over a collection that may hold millions or billions of items, while the collection keeps changing underneath it.

Start with the naive alternative to see why special machinery is needed. Suppose you want the plays by Shakespeare that contain "Brutus" and "Caesar" but not "Calpurnia". You could scan every play line by line, the way `grep` does. Shakespeare's complete works are about 5 MB, and a modern machine scans that in a few milliseconds. So for small data, scanning works.

It stops working for three reasons:
- **Size.** A product catalogue of 50 million items, or a web index of billions of pages, is terabytes. Scanning a terabyte at 2 GB/s takes over 8 minutes per query.
- **Flexible matching.** Users want "running" to match "run", "colour" to match "color", "NYC" to match "New York", misspellings to be forgiven and words near each other to count more. `grep` matches literal strings.
- **Ranking.** A scan answers yes or no. Users want the best ten results first, out of maybe 100,000 that match.

The answer is to do the expensive work once, ahead of time, by building an **index**, a data structure that turns "which documents contain this word?" into a lookup rather than a scan. Every search system is organised around that idea.

## Two halves on two schedules
Every search system has two halves that run at different times and have very different requirements:
- **Indexing** (offline or near-real-time, heavy, can take seconds to hours): collect documents, turn their text into terms, and build data structures that make lookups fast. It's throughput-bound: you care about documents per second and how quickly a change becomes searchable.
- **Querying** (online, must be fast): turn the user's query into terms, look them up, score the matching documents and return the best few. It's latency-bound: you care about p99 milliseconds per query and queries per second.

Separating them is what makes search fast: the query side does as little work as possible because the indexing side has already done the rest.

%%flow Indexing runs ahead of time; querying reuses the same text processing so query terms match index terms.
[Indexing] Documents -(crawl or ingest)-> Parse and tokenize -(normalise, stem)-> *Inverted index
[Querying] Query -(same analyser)-> Query terms -(look up postings)-> Candidates -(score, re-rank)-> Results
%%

## The pipeline, stage by stage
Here is the path from raw document to ranked result. Each stage has its own lesson later in this module.

### Indexing side
1. **Acquire documents.** A **crawler** fetches web pages (lesson 09.12), or, far more often in product work, the application pushes its own records (products, posts, tickets, messages) into the search engine, typically through change data capture from the main database (lesson 09.10).
2. **Parse.** Extract the text and fields from each document: strip HTML tags, pull out the title, body, author, price, date. Decide which fields are searchable text and which are exact values for filtering.
3. **Tokenise and normalise** (together called **analysis**). Split text into **tokens** (roughly, words), lower-case them, fold accents ("café" to "cafe"), optionally remove **stop words** such as "the" (lesson 09.06), and reduce words to a **stem** or base form ("running", "runs" to "run"). The output is **terms**, the units the index stores.
4. **Build the inverted index.** For each term, record which documents contain it, and usually how many times and at which positions (lesson 09.02). Also store per-document data such as length and quality signals.

### Query side
5. **Understand the query.** Apply the same analysis to the query, then correct spelling (lesson 09.15), expand synonyms, recognise entities ("nike" is a brand) and detect intent (lesson 09.07).
6. **Retrieve candidates.** Look up each query term's **postings list** (the list of documents containing that term) and combine them with AND or OR logic (lesson 09.03). Apply filters such as `in_stock = true`.
7. **Score and rank.** Compute a text-relevance score such as TF-IDF or BM25 for each candidate (lessons 09.04, 09.05). Then optionally **re-rank** the top few hundred with a learned model that also uses popularity, freshness, personalisation and click signals (lesson 09.08).
8. **Return results** with titles, **snippets** (short extracts showing the matched words, highlighted) and facets (counts by brand, price band).

The crucial design rule is that **steps 3 and 5 must agree**. If the index stores "run" but the query is processed into "running", nothing matches. Search engines enforce this by attaching one named **analyser** to each field and running it on both documents and queries.

%%layers The ranking funnel: each stage looks at fewer documents with a more expensive scoring method.
All documents | 100 million, never touched one by one
Candidate retrieval | ~100,000 matching docs via postings lists
*First-stage scoring | BM25 on matches, keep top 1,000
Re-ranking | learned model on top 1,000, keep top 50
Final page | 10 results with snippets
%%

## Why the funnel matters
Notice the shape of that diagram. Each stage handles roughly 10 to 100 times fewer documents than the one above, and can afford to spend 10 to 100 times more per document. Postings lookups cost nanoseconds per document. BM25 costs maybe a microsecond. A learned re-ranker with hundreds of features might cost 10 to 100 microseconds per document. Running the re-ranker on all 100,000 matches would take seconds; running it on the top 1,000 takes a few milliseconds. Every large search system, from web search to e-commerce to recommendation, is a funnel like this.

## What a production engine looks like
The most common real implementation is **Elasticsearch** (or its fork **OpenSearch**), a distributed layer on top of **Apache Lucene**, the Java library that does the actual indexing and scoring. Solr is another distributed layer on Lucene. Their vocabulary:

| Concept | What it is |
| Index | A named collection of documents, roughly a table |
| Document | A JSON object, the unit you search and return |
| Mapping | The schema: which fields exist and how each is analysed |
| Shard | A slice of an index; each shard is one complete Lucene index |
| Replica | A copy of a shard, for fault tolerance and read throughput |
| Segment | An immutable piece of a Lucene index; new writes create new segments |

### A search request, step by step
A search request arrives at any node, which acts as the **coordinating node** for that request. It sends the query to one copy (primary or replica) of every shard of the index.
- In the **query phase**, each shard runs the query against its own documents, scores them, and returns only the IDs and scores of its local top results (if the page size is 10, its top 10).
- The coordinator merges these lists into a global top 10.
- In the **fetch phase**, it asks only the shards that own those 10 documents for their full contents (and snippets).

Returning IDs first and fetching bodies second means the network carries 10 full documents rather than 10 times the number of shards.

%%seq Elasticsearch's two-phase search: shards return only IDs and scores first, then the coordinator fetches the winning documents.
Client > Coordinator : search "red shoes", size 10
Coordinator > Shard 1 : query phase
Coordinator > Shard 2 : query phase
Shard 1 -> Coordinator : top 10 IDs + scores
Shard 2 -> Coordinator : top 10 IDs + scores
Coordinator > Coordinator : merge, keep global top 10
Coordinator > Shard 1 : fetch docs 4, 9, 17
Coordinator > Shard 2 : fetch docs 2, 8 ...
Coordinator -> Client : 10 ranked documents
%%

This also shows why **deep pagination** is expensive: asking for results 10,000 to 10,010 forces every shard to return its top 10,010, and the coordinator to sort them all. Elasticsearch caps it by default (`max_result_window` of 10,000) and offers cursor-style `search_after` instead.

### Two practical rules
- **A search engine is not a primary database.** Keep the source of truth in Postgres, MySQL or DynamoDB and copy changes into the search index (lesson 09.10). Search engines are optimised for reading, their durability guarantees and transactional features are weaker, and you will want to rebuild the index from scratch when you change analysers.
- **It's often unnecessary for small data.** Below roughly 100,000 documents with modest query rates, the database's own full-text search (Postgres `tsvector` with a GIN index, MySQL `FULLTEXT`) is usually enough, and saves you running and syncing a second system.

! Example: search for a shop with 2 million products
! Indexing: the product database streams every change through CDC into an Elasticsearch index with fields `title` (analysed text with an English stemmer), `brand` (exact keyword), `price` (number), `in_stock` (boolean). A price change is searchable within about a second.
! Querying: "red runing shoes" arrives. The analyser lower-cases it and splits it into three tokens. The spell corrector changes "runing" to "running", which the stemmer reduces to "run". The engine looks up postings for red, run and shoe, takes documents containing all three (about 4,000), filters to `in_stock = true` (3,100), scores them with BM25 and keeps the top 500. A re-ranker boosts best-sellers and items with good reviews. The page shows 20 results with matched words highlighted and brand facets, in about 50 ms.

! Example: why a scan doesn't work at this size
! The same catalogue holds 2 million products of about 2 KB of text each: 4 GB. Even reading RAM at 5 GB/s, one scan takes about 0.8 s on one core, and you still have to rank the matches. At 500 queries a second you'd need 400 cores scanning flat out. With an inverted index, the same query reads three postings lists of a few thousand entries each, a few hundred kilobytes, and finishes in milliseconds on one core.

## Common mistakes
- Using different analysis at index and query time (stemming one but not the other), so obvious queries return nothing.
- Treating the search engine as the source of truth, then losing data on a bad reindex.
- Running the expensive model on every candidate instead of using a funnel.
- Adding Elasticsearch for a 20,000-row table that the database could search.

## In an interview
Draw the two halves first: an indexing pipeline fed by CDC from the primary database, and a query path through query understanding, retrieval, scoring and re-ranking. Name the funnel sizes. Then dive where the interviewer points: sharding (lesson 09.11), freshness (lesson 09.10), ranking (lessons 09.05 and 09.08) or autocomplete (lesson 09.14).

> Takeaway: search is an offline indexing pipeline (acquire, parse, analyse, build the inverted index) plus an online query pipeline (understand the query, retrieve candidates, score, re-rank, return). Both must analyse text the same way, each ranking stage narrows the candidates so expensive models run on few documents, and the search index is a derived copy of data that lives elsewhere.

## The idea: invert the document
A document naturally lists its words in order: "the cat sat on the mat". That's the **forward** direction: document to words. To answer "which documents contain *cat*?" from forward data, you'd read every document. An **inverted index** flips the relationship: for every term, it lists the documents that contain it. Now the question is one lookup.

Three words to learn now, because the rest of the module uses them:
- The **dictionary** (or vocabulary, or lexicon) is the set of all distinct terms in the collection.
- Each term's list of document IDs is its **postings list**.
- Each entry in a postings list is a **posting**: at minimum a document ID, often with extra data such as how many times the term appears.

A **document ID** (docID) is a small integer the engine assigns to each document, usually in the order documents are added. Integers are cheaper to store, sort and compare than URLs or product SKUs, and the engine keeps a separate mapping from docID back to the real document.

## Building it by hand
Take three tiny documents:

| Doc ID | Text |
| 1 | cat sat |
| 2 | cat ran |
| 3 | dog sat |

Step 1: **tokenise** each document into tokens. Doc 1 gives `cat`, `sat`. Doc 2 gives `cat`, `ran`. Doc 3 gives `dog`, `sat`.

Step 2: **normalise** tokens into terms (lower-case, maybe stem). These are already lower-case single words, so nothing changes.

Step 3: **emit a pair** `(term, docID)` for every token, in document order:
`(cat,1) (sat,1) (cat,2) (ran,2) (dog,3) (sat,3)`

Step 4: **sort** the pairs by term, then by docID:
`(cat,1) (cat,2) (dog,3) (ran,2) (sat,1) (sat,3)`

Step 5: **group** by term, merging duplicates within a document, and record each term's **document frequency** (df), the number of documents in its list:

%%tree The finished inverted index for the three documents: a sorted dictionary of terms, each pointing at a sorted postings list.
Dictionary
  cat | df 2
    *postings: 1, 2
  dog | df 1
    *postings: 3
  ran | df 1
    *postings: 2
  sat | df 2
    *postings: 1, 3
%%

Now "which documents contain sat?" is one dictionary lookup that returns `[1, 3]`. "Which contain cat AND sat?" is the overlap of `[1, 2]` and `[1, 3]`, which is `[1]` (lesson 09.03 covers how to compute that efficiently).

The same steps in code:

```
docs = {1: "cat sat", 2: "cat ran", 3: "dog sat"}
pairs = []
for doc_id, text in docs.items():
    for term in text.lower().split():
        pairs.append((term, doc_id))
pairs.sort()                      # by term, then doc id
index = {}
for term, doc_id in pairs:
    plist = index.setdefault(term, [])
    if not plist or plist[-1] != doc_id:
        plist.append(doc_id)      # sorted, no duplicates
print(index)  # {'cat': [1, 2], 'dog': [3], 'ran': [2], 'sat': [1, 3]}
```

Why sort rather than just append to a hash map as you go? For a few documents either works. For a collection that doesn't fit in memory, sorting is the operation we know how to do efficiently on disk (external merge sort), and it produces postings that are already in docID order, which every later operation depends on.

## What a posting can hold
A bare docID answers "does this document contain the term?". Ranking and phrase search need more:
- **Term frequency** (tf): how many times the term appears in that document. Needed for scoring (lessons 09.04 and 09.05).
- **Positions**: the word offsets of each occurrence. Needed for **phrase queries** ("new york" must have "york" at position p + 1 when "new" is at p) and **proximity** scoring (terms close together count more).
- **Offsets** (character start and end) to highlight matches in snippets without re-analysing the text.

A richer index for "the cat sat on the cat mat" (doc 7) would store `cat → (7, tf 2, positions [1, 5])`. Positions often make the index two to four times bigger, so engines let you turn them off per field.

%%compare How much each posting stores, and what queries that enables.
[DocID only]
cat -> 1, 2, 7
+ Smallest index
- Only yes or no matching
[With tf]
cat -> 1:1, 2:1, 7:2
+ Enables ranking
- A little larger
[*With positions]
cat -> 7:2 at 1, 5
+ Phrases and proximity
- Two to four times larger
%%

## Where the pieces live
An inverted index has two parts of very different size.
- The **dictionary** is small: maybe a few million distinct terms, each with its df and a pointer to its postings. It's kept in memory, or mostly in memory, in a structure that supports fast lookup and prefix search. Lucene uses a **finite state transducer** (FST), a compressed automaton that shares prefixes and suffixes between terms, so tens of millions of terms fit in a few hundred megabytes.
- The **postings** are large, typically most of the index, and live on disk (and in the operating system's page cache when hot).

### Why postings are sorted
Postings are kept **sorted by docID**. That one decision makes:
- Intersecting two lists a single linear merge (lesson 09.03).
- Merging partial indexes cheap.
- Compression very effective, as follows.

### Gap encoding and compression
Because lists are sorted, store the **gaps** between successive IDs instead of the IDs themselves. The list `[1000, 1002, 1005, 1100]` becomes `[1000, 2, 3, 95]`. Common terms have many postings close together, so their gaps are tiny. Small numbers need few bits, so encode each gap with a variable-length code:
- **Variable-byte** (varint): 7 bits of each byte carry data and 1 bit says "more bytes follow". Gaps under 128 take one byte instead of four.
- **Bit packing** in blocks (as Lucene does with blocks of 128): find the largest gap in the block, and store all 128 gaps with exactly that many bits each. Very fast to decode with SIMD instructions.

! Example: how much compression buys
! A term appears in 10 million of a collection's 100 million documents. Stored as 4-byte integers, its postings take 40 MB. On average, the gap between postings is 100 million / 10 million = 10, which fits in 4 bits. With bit packing at about 4 to 5 bits per posting, the list takes about 5 to 6 MB, an eight-fold saving. Smaller lists also mean fewer disk reads and more of the index in the page cache, so compression makes queries faster, not slower.

## Building at real scale
The hand-built method sorts all pairs in memory. A billion-document index produces hundreds of billions of pairs; that doesn't fit. Two approaches:
- **Blocked sort-based indexing**: process documents in blocks that fit in memory, build a sorted partial index for each block, write it to disk, then merge all the partial indexes in one pass (like the merge phase of an external sort).
- **MapReduce-style** distributed indexing: mappers emit `(term, docID)` pairs, the shuffle groups pairs by term across machines, and reducers write postings lists. This is how web-scale indexes were built in batch.

### Lucene's segments
Engines that index continuously, like Lucene (and therefore Elasticsearch, OpenSearch and Solr), use a different trick:
1. New documents go into an **in-memory buffer** and the transaction log.
2. Periodically (Elasticsearch's default **refresh** is every 1 second), the buffer is written out as a new **segment**: a small, complete, immutable inverted index. Once a segment exists, its documents are searchable.
3. A query searches every segment and combines the results.
4. In the background, a **merge policy** combines small segments into bigger ones, so the number of segments stays manageable.
5. **Deletes are soft**: a deleted document is marked in a per-segment bitmap (the "live docs") and filtered out of results. It's physically removed only when its segment is merged. An **update** is a delete plus a re-insert of the whole document.

%%timeline One shard's segments over a few seconds: small segments appear on each refresh and are merged in the background.
[Writes] 0.0 s | docs 1-500 buffered in memory
[Segments] 1.0 s | refresh: segment A (500 docs) searchable
[Writes] 1.4 s | doc 42 deleted, marked in A's bitmap
[Segments] 2.0 s | refresh: segment B (480 docs)
[Segments] 3.0 s | refresh: segment C (510 docs)
[Merge] 3.5 s | *A, B, C merged into D; doc 42 dropped
%%

This is the same idea as the **LSM tree** from Module 7: buffer writes in memory, flush immutable sorted files, compact in the background. Immutability has big benefits: segments never need locks for reading, they cache perfectly in the page cache, and they compress well. The cost is that updates are expensive (rewrite the whole document) and merges use disk I/O.

! Example: an update to a product
! Product 9001's price changes. Elasticsearch doesn't edit the old posting lists; it marks the old version of doc 9001 deleted in segment A's bitmap, adds the new version to the in-memory buffer, and within a second the next refresh writes it into a new segment. Until that refresh, queries still see the old version; after it, they see only the new one, never both, because the delete and the new segment become visible together and the live-docs bitmap filters the old copy. Later, when segment A is merged, the old version's postings disappear for good. If a product is updated 100 times a day, 99 dead copies pile up until merges clean them, which is why frequently updated indexes need tuning of merge settings.

## Common mistakes
- Indexing with positions on every field "just in case", doubling index size for fields nobody phrase-searches.
- Treating updates as cheap. In an inverted index an update rewrites the whole document; high-churn fields (view counts) are better kept outside the text index or in a separate store.
- Forgetting that df and other statistics include deleted-but-not-yet-merged documents until merges happen, which can skew scores slightly.

## In an interview
If asked "how does full-text search work?", build the index for two or three tiny documents on the board in under a minute: tokens, pairs, sort, group, postings with df. Then mention positions for phrases, gap compression, and immutable segments with background merges and soft deletes. That covers what most interviewers want to hear.

> Takeaway: an inverted index maps each term to a sorted postings list of document IDs (plus term frequencies and positions), built by emitting (term, docID) pairs, sorting and grouping. The dictionary stays in memory, compressed postings live on disk, and at scale the index is built as immutable segments that are merged later, with deletes marked in a bitmap.

## Boolean retrieval
The simplest way to use an inverted index is **Boolean retrieval**: a query is a logical expression over terms, and a document either matches or it does not. There is no score, no "better" or "worse" match. A classic example over Shakespeare's plays is `Brutus AND Caesar AND NOT Calpurnia`.

Each Boolean operator becomes an operation on postings lists (the sorted lists of document IDs for each term, lesson 09.02):
- **AND** is the **intersection** of postings lists: documents in both.
- **OR** is the **union**: documents in either.
- **NOT** is the **difference**: documents in one list but not the other.

Boolean search is still everywhere, even in engines that rank. In Elasticsearch, a `bool` query has `must` (AND, and contributes to score), `filter` (AND, no scoring, cacheable), `should` (OR, adds to score) and `must_not` (NOT). Filters such as `in_stock = true AND category = shoes` are pure Boolean, evaluated before or alongside ranking.

## Intersecting two lists
Because postings are sorted by docID, two lists can be intersected in one pass, like the merge step of merge sort. Keep a pointer into each list:
1. If the two current IDs are equal, output the ID and advance both pointers.
2. Otherwise, advance the pointer that holds the smaller ID (that ID can't be in the other list, since everything after the larger ID's position is even larger).
3. Stop when either list runs out.

This takes **O(x + y)** time for lists of length x and y, and needs no extra memory beyond the output.

```
def intersect(p1, p2):
    i = j = 0; out = []
    while i < len(p1) and j < len(p2):
        if p1[i] == p2[j]: out.append(p1[i]); i += 1; j += 1
        elif p1[i] < p2[j]: i += 1
        else: j += 1
    return out
```

%%timeline Tracing the merge of cat [1, 2, 7, 9] and sat [1, 3, 9]: each step compares the two pointers and advances the smaller.
Step 1 | cat 1 vs sat 1: equal, *output 1
Step 2 | cat 2 vs sat 3: advance cat
Step 3 | cat 7 vs sat 3: advance sat
Step 4 | cat 7 vs sat 9: advance cat
Step 5 | cat 9 vs sat 9: equal, *output 9
Step 6 | cat list ends: result [1, 9]
%%

Union works the same way, outputting every ID once. `A AND NOT B` walks both lists and outputs IDs from A that aren't matched in B. Note that a bare `NOT B` would need the list of all documents, which is huge, so engines require NOT to be combined with a positive clause.

## Skip pointers and galloping
When one list is much shorter than the other, the linear merge wastes time stepping through the long list one posting at a time. Two tricks fix this:
- **Skip pointers**: every √n entries or so, the long list stores a pointer that jumps ahead. When the short list's current ID is 50,000 and the long list is at 1,200, the merge can follow skip pointers past whole runs of IDs instead of reading each one. Lucene stores skip data in its postings so it can jump within a block-compressed list.
- **Galloping (exponential) search**: from the current position, probe 1, 2, 4, 8, ... entries ahead until you pass the target, then binary search back. This costs about O(x log(y / x)) for a short list of x and long list of y, close to optimal.

With either, intersecting a list of 300 with a list of 10 million costs a few thousand operations, not 10 million.

## Why the shortest list goes first
For a query with several ANDed terms, **process terms in order of increasing document frequency**, starting with the rarest. The reason is simple: the result of an intersection can never be longer than the shorter of its two inputs. So if you start with the rarest term, the intermediate result is small from the first step, and every later step only checks a few candidates against the longer lists, which skip pointers let you jump through.

The engine knows each term's document frequency (df) from the dictionary for free, before reading any postings, so it can plan the order up front. That's a tiny **query planner**, the same idea as a database choosing the most selective index first.

%%bars Postings scanned for "the AND quick AND zebra" in two evaluation orders. Starting with the rarest term avoids reading the huge list.
the first, linear | 10050300 | ~10 million postings
zebra first, linear | 10050300 | still ~10 million if no skipping
*zebra first, with skips | 6000 | ~6,000 postings checked
%%

! Example: why order matters
! Say "the" appears in 10,000,000 documents, "quick" in 50,000 and "zebra" in 300.
! Bad order: start with "the AND quick". The merge walks 10,050,000 postings to produce up to 50,000 results, then intersects those with "zebra" to keep at most 300. Most of the work was thrown away.
! Good order: start with "zebra AND quick". With galloping through "quick", that takes a few thousand steps and yields at most 300 IDs (say 40). Then check those 40 against "the": with skip pointers, each is a short jump, so maybe 40 × 20 = 800 steps. Total: a few thousand operations instead of ten million, a speed-up of over a thousand times for the same answer.

For OR queries the order doesn't matter for correctness, but the size does: a union is at least as long as the longest list. That's why common words in OR queries are expensive, and why ranked engines use tricks such as **WAND** and **block-max WAND**: they keep track of the score threshold needed to enter the current top 10 and skip over documents that can't possibly beat it, using the maximum possible score contribution of each term.

## The limit of Boolean search
Boolean retrieval has no notion of "better". Every matching document is equally good, and results usually come back in docID order, which means nothing to the user. In practice queries either return far too many results to read ("feast") or none at all ("famine"): `shoes AND red` returns 40,000 products; `shoes AND red AND waterproof AND size-9 AND leather` returns zero.

That is fine for expert users such as patent examiners, librarians and lawyers who write precise queries, want every match and can refine iteratively. Legal research systems still offer Boolean search for exactly this reason. Most users, though, type a few words and want the best results first. Ranking (lessons 09.04 and 09.05) fixes that. But ranking has a cost: scoring every matching document is expensive when there are millions of matches. Tiered indexes are one way to keep it fast.

## Tiered indexes
A **tiered index** avoids scoring every matching document by splitting each term's postings into tiers by importance. For example:
- Tier 1 holds documents where the term appears at least 20 times (or, more usefully, documents with high quality scores).
- Tier 2 holds those where it appears at least 10 times.
- Tier 3 holds the rest.

A query runs on **tier 1 only** first. If tier 1 yields at least K results (K is the number you want to show, say 10), stop and rank those. If not, fall back to tier 2 and merge, and so on. Most popular queries are satisfied by the top tier, which is a small fraction of the index.

%%flow A tiered index: search the high-quality tier first and only fall back when it can't fill the page.
Query -> *Tier 1: best docs -(fewer than K results)-> Tier 2: good docs -(still fewer)-> Tier 3: the rest
%%

Real web engines apply the same idea with **document quality** rather than term frequency. A small tier of high-quality, popular pages (perhaps a few percent of the index, often kept in memory) is searched first. Only rare queries that can't be answered from it go to the large, slower tiers of the long tail. Splitting by quality rather than per term also means one tier is a whole separate index that can live on faster hardware.

%%compare Searching everything versus searching in tiers. Tiers are faster for common queries but can miss a great document that landed in a low tier.
[Single index]
Query -> All postings -> Score all matches
+ Never misses a match
- Scores millions for common queries
[*Tiered index]
Query -> Tier 1 -(enough?)-> Stop or tier 2
+ Most queries touch a small tier
+ Hot tier fits in memory
- A relevant doc in tier 3 can be missed
%%

### Related tricks
All of these trade a little accuracy for speed, and all rely on ordering or pruning postings:
- **Index elimination**: only consider query terms whose idf is above a threshold (rare, informative terms), or only documents that contain most of the query terms. "the best pizza in leeds" is effectively evaluated on "pizza" and "leeds".
- **Champion lists**: for each term, precompute the top r documents by weight and search only those first (lesson 09.06).
- **Impact-ordered postings**: sort each postings list by the term's weight in the document instead of by docID, so the best postings come first and scoring can stop early. You lose cheap linear merges, so this is used with score-at-a-time algorithms.

! Example: a tiered product index
! An e-commerce site has 50 million products, but 2 million account for 95% of sales. It builds tier 1 from those 2 million (in memory, on fast nodes) and tier 2 from everything else. The query "iphone case" finds 3,000 tier-1 matches: plenty to fill a page of 48, so tier 2 is never touched, and the query takes 15 ms. The query "replacement hinge for 2009 dishwasher model x" finds only 2 tier-1 matches, so the engine falls back to tier 2, finds 40 more, and answers in 90 ms. Average latency drops sharply because most traffic is head queries.

## Common mistakes
- Putting common terms first in AND evaluation, or letting user order decide.
- Implementing `NOT` without a positive clause, forcing a scan of all documents.
- Forgetting that filters (Boolean, unscored) can be cached as bitsets and reused across queries, which is one of the cheapest speed-ups available.
- Using tiers without monitoring how often queries fall through to lower tiers. If most do, the tiers are wrong.

## In an interview
When explaining query execution, say: "postings are sorted by docID, so AND is a linear merge; evaluate terms rarest first using df from the dictionary, and use skip pointers so long lists are jumped through, not read." If the interviewer pushes on latency, mention tiers or early termination (WAND) for ranked queries.

> Takeaway: Boolean queries are evaluated by merging sorted postings lists in linear time, starting with the rarest term so intermediate results stay small, and skip pointers let the merge jump through long lists. Boolean search doesn't rank, so tiered indexes put the best documents in a first tier and only fall back to lower tiers when the first can't supply enough results.

@09.04
## From matching to ranking
Boolean search (lesson 09.03) says whether a document matches. Ranked search says **how well**. For a query like "the auto insurance", tens of thousands of documents might contain at least one of those words. You need a number for each, a **relevance score**, so you can show the best ten first.

TF-IDF is the classic way to compute that number from text alone. It's built from two observations about language that are easy to state and surprisingly powerful.
1. **A term that appears often in a document is probably central to it.** A page that says "insurance" twelve times is more likely about insurance than one that says it once. This is **term frequency**, written tf(t, d): the number of times term t occurs in document d.
2. **A term that appears in many documents tells you little.** Almost every English document contains "the", so a match on "the" says nothing about whether a document is relevant. A query for "the auto insurance" should be driven by "insurance" and "auto", not "the". This is captured by **document frequency**, df(t): the number of documents in the collection that contain t.

Term frequency is about one document. Document frequency is about the whole collection. TF-IDF multiplies a local signal by a global one.

## Inverse document frequency
Turn document frequency into a weight that is high for rare terms and low for common ones:

`idf(t) = log(N / df(t))`

where N is the total number of documents. Read it as "how surprising is it to see this term?".
- A term in **every** document: N / df = 1, and log(1) = 0. It contributes nothing.
- A term in **half** the documents: log10(2) ≈ 0.3.
- A term in **1 document of a million**: log10(1,000,000) = 6.

%%bars idf (base 10) in a collection of 1,000,000 documents. The log keeps a term 1,000 times rarer from being 1,000 times heavier.
the: in 1,000,000 docs | 0.001 | 0
of: in 600,000 docs | 0.22 | 0.22
auto: in 20,000 docs | 1.7 | 1.7
insurance: in 1,000 docs | 3 | 3
*subrogation: in 10 docs | 5 | 5
%%

Why the logarithm? Without it, idf would be N / df directly: "subrogation" (in 10 documents) would weigh 100,000 and "insurance" (in 1,000) would weigh 1,000, so the rare word would swamp everything. The log dampens this so a term 1,000 times rarer is about 3 points heavier, not 1,000 times heavier. The base of the log doesn't matter for ranking; it scales every weight by the same constant.

### Why document frequency and not total count
There's another way to measure how common a word is: **collection frequency** (cf), its total number of occurrences across all documents. Why not use that? Compare two words from a news collection of 806,791 Reuters articles:

| Term | cf (total occurrences) | df (documents containing it) |
| try | 10,422 | 8,760 |
| insurance | 10,440 | 3,997 |

Both words occur about 10,400 times in total, so collection frequency treats them as equally informative. But "try" is spread thinly: it appears about once in each of 8,760 articles. "insurance" is concentrated in fewer than 4,000: when an article is about insurance, it says "insurance" several times. Document frequency captures that "insurance" is the better **discriminator**, the word that separates relevant documents from irrelevant ones. That's exactly what a search weight should measure.

## Combining them
The TF-IDF weight of a term in a document is the product:

`tf-idf(t, d) = tf(t, d) × idf(t)`

So the weight is:
- **Highest** when a term occurs many times in a small number of documents (strong local signal, rare globally).
- **Lower** when a term occurs few times in a document, or occurs in many documents.
- **Zero** when a term occurs in every document.

The simplest score for a document against a query adds up the weights of the query terms:

`Score(q, d) = Σ over t in q of tf-idf(t, d)`

Terms in the query that the document doesn't contain contribute 0.

%%flow tf-idf for one term in one document: the count in the document times the term's rarity across the collection.
tf: 3 in the doc -(times)-> idf: log10(1,000,000 / 1,000) = 3 -> *Weight 9
%%

! Example: scoring two documents for "the insurance"
! N = 1,000,000. "the" is in all 1,000,000 documents, so idf = log10(1) = 0. "insurance" is in 1,000 documents, so idf = log10(1,000) = 3.
! Document A says "the" 40 times and "insurance" once: score = 40 × 0 + 1 × 3 = 3.
! Document B says "the" 5 times and "insurance" 6 times: score = 5 × 0 + 6 × 3 = 18.
! B wins by a wide margin, and the 40 uses of "the" in A count for nothing. Without idf (raw tf only), A would score 41 and B 11, and the wrong document would win.

## Sublinear tf
Is a document that says "insurance" 20 times really 20 times as relevant as one that says it once? Almost never. The 20th mention adds much less evidence than the first. A common fix is **sublinear** (log-scaled) term frequency:

`wf(t, d) = 1 + log(tf)` if tf > 0, else 0

With base 10: tf 1 gives 1, tf 10 gives 2, tf 100 gives 3. Repetition still helps, but with sharply diminishing returns. This is the first step towards **saturation**, which BM25 (lesson 09.05) makes explicit and tunable.

## The vector space model
TF-IDF becomes more powerful when you treat documents as **vectors**. Imagine a space with one dimension per term in the dictionary, possibly millions of dimensions. Each document is a point in that space: its coordinate on the "insurance" axis is its tf-idf weight for "insurance", and so on. Most coordinates are zero, since a document contains a tiny fraction of all terms (a **sparse vector**). The query is also a vector in the same space.

Relevance is now geometric: how closely does the document vector point in the same direction as the query vector? The standard measure is **cosine similarity**, the cosine of the angle between them:

`cos(q, d) = (q · d) / (|q| × |d|)`

The dot product `q · d` sums, over shared terms, the product of their weights. Dividing by the vector lengths `|q|` and `|d|` **normalises for length**. Without it, a long document wins just because it contains more words and therefore more matches and higher counts. With it, a short, focused document on the topic can beat a 50-page manual that mentions the topic in passing.

%%compare Scoring with and without length normalisation. Without it, long documents win simply by containing more words.
[Raw tf-idf sum]
Query -> Sum weights -> Long docs favoured
- 50-page manual beats a focused FAQ
- Keyword stuffing raises the score
[*Cosine similarity]
Query -> Dot product -(divide by lengths)-> Direction match
+ Focused short docs can win
+ Score between 0 and 1
- Can over-penalise long relevant docs
%%

! Example: cosine with two terms
! Query "auto insurance". Use idf: auto 1.7, insurance 3. Query vector q = (1.7, 3), length √(1.7² + 3²) = √11.89 ≈ 3.45.
! Doc X: a short page with tf auto 1, insurance 1, so weights (1.7, 3). Its other words also add to its vector length; say its full length is 10. q · X = 1.7 × 1.7 + 3 × 3 = 11.89. Cosine = 11.89 / (3.45 × 10) ≈ 0.34.
! Doc Y: a long manual with tf auto 3, insurance 5, so weights (5.1, 15). With thousands of other words, its full length is 80. q · Y = 1.7 × 5.1 + 3 × 15 = 53.67. Cosine = 53.67 / (3.45 × 80) ≈ 0.19.
! Raw sums would rank Y first (20.1 versus 4.7). Cosine ranks the short, on-topic X first.

### How engines compute this fast
The engine doesn't compare the query against every document vector. It walks the postings lists of the query terms only (a handful of lists), accumulating each document's partial dot product in a table of **accumulators**, one per candidate document. Document lengths are precomputed at indexing time and stored per document, so the division is cheap. Then a heap keeps the top K. This **term-at-a-time** (or document-at-a-time, walking all lists in parallel) scoring is why ranking stays fast: work is proportional to the length of the query terms' postings, not to the collection.

## Weaknesses
Plain TF-IDF has known problems:
- **Unbounded tf**: raw term frequency grows without limit, so stuffing a keyword 500 times keeps raising the score. Spammers exploited this in early web search.
- **Crude length handling**: cosine normalisation can over-penalise long documents that are genuinely relevant, and there's no knob to tune it.
- **Bag of words**: word order and proximity are ignored, so "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" score the same.
- **Vocabulary mismatch**: "car" doesn't match "automobile" at all; TF-IDF only rewards exact term matches. Synonyms (lesson 09.07) and vector search (lesson 09.13) address this.

BM25 (next lesson) fixes the first two with saturation and tunable length normalisation, and is the default in Lucene and Elasticsearch today.

## Common mistakes
- Forgetting idf is computed per shard in distributed engines, so a small shard with unusual content can give different scores. Elasticsearch's `dfs_query_then_fetch` exists to gather global statistics first when this matters.
- Expecting TF-IDF to handle synonyms or meaning. It matches strings.
- Removing length normalisation "because long documents are better" without checking with evaluation metrics (lesson 09.09).

## In an interview
Explain TF-IDF in two sentences: tf rewards terms frequent in the document, idf = log(N/df) discounts terms common across the collection, and the score sums tf × idf over query terms. Then name the weaknesses (unbounded tf, crude length normalisation, no synonyms) to lead naturally into BM25 and semantic retrieval.

> Takeaway: tf-idf rewards a term that appears often in a document (tf) but discounts terms that appear in many documents (idf = log(N/df)), so common words like "the" contribute almost nothing. A document's score sums tf-idf over the query terms, and cosine similarity normalises for document length so long documents don't win by size alone.

?? IDF is log(N / df), where N is the number of documents and df the number containing the term. A word like "the" appears in nearly every document, so df is close to N, the ratio is close to 1, and its logarithm is close to 0. Its tf-idf weight in every document is therefore near zero however many times it appears, so it barely changes any score or ranking. Rare terms that actually distinguish relevant documents get large weights, and the log keeps very rare terms from completely swamping the rest.

@09.05
## What BM25 is
**BM25** ("Best Match 25", the 25th in a series of weighting schemes from the Okapi research system of the 1990s) is the default similarity function in Lucene, and therefore in Elasticsearch, OpenSearch and Solr. If you've used any of them, you've used BM25. It keeps tf-idf's two ideas (lesson 09.04): terms frequent in a document matter more, and terms common across the collection matter less. What it changes is **how term frequency and document length are counted**, fixing tf-idf's two main weaknesses.

The formula looks intimidating, so take it in parts:

`score(D, Q) = Σ IDF(qᵢ) × f(qᵢ, D) × (k1 + 1) / ( f(qᵢ, D) + k1 × (1 − b + b × |D| / avgdl) )`

- The sum runs over each term qᵢ in the query Q. Each term contributes independently, and the document's score is the total.
- `f(qᵢ, D)`: how many times query term qᵢ appears in document D. This is the term frequency, tf.
- `|D|`: the length of document D in terms (for the field being scored). `avgdl`: the average length across all documents in the index.
- `k1` and `b`: two tuning knobs, explained below.
- `IDF(qᵢ)`: Lucene uses `ln(1 + (N − n + 0.5) / (n + 0.5))`, where N is the number of documents and n is the number containing the term. Like tf-idf's log(N/df), it's large for rare terms and close to zero for terms in nearly every document. The `+ 0.5` terms smooth the edge cases, and the `1 +` inside the log keeps the value from ever going negative (the original Okapi formula could give negative IDF for terms in more than half the documents).

So each term's contribution is **IDF × a term-frequency part**, and the term-frequency part is where k1 and b live.

## k1: term frequency saturation
In plain tf-idf, a document that mentions "shoes" 100 times scores 100 times higher on that term than one that mentions it once. That's wrong on two counts. Relevance doesn't really grow that way: the tenth mention adds much less evidence than the first. And it rewards **keyword stuffing**: early web spammers repeated popular words hundreds of times in hidden text to climb rankings.

BM25's term-frequency part has the shape `tf × (k1 + 1) / (tf + k1)` (for an average-length document). Look at what it does:
- At tf = 0 it's 0.
- At tf = 1 it's exactly 1, whatever k1 is.
- As tf grows, the denominator grows almost as fast as the numerator, and the value **flattens towards an asymptote of k1 + 1**. It can never exceed that ceiling.

This is called **saturation**: the first few occurrences raise the score a lot, and later ones add less and less. k1 controls how quickly the curve saturates. The default is **k1 = 1.2**, so the ceiling is 2.2.
- A **higher** k1 (say 2 or 3) lets extra occurrences keep counting for longer; behaviour moves closer to raw tf.
- A **lower** k1 saturates sooner. At **k1 = 0**, term frequency is ignored entirely: only presence matters, and the part is 1 for any tf above 0.

%%bars BM25's term-frequency part for an average-length document with k1 = 1.2. It can never exceed k1 + 1 = 2.2, so stuffing a word stops paying.
tf 1 | 1.0 | 1.00
tf 2 | 1.375 | 1.38
tf 5 | 1.77 | 1.77
tf 10 | 1.96 | 1.96
*tf 100 | 2.17 | 2.17
raw tf-idf at tf 100 | 100 | 100
%%

Going from 1 occurrence to 2 adds 0.38. Going from 10 to 100 adds 0.21. A spammer who repeats a word 100 times gets barely more than an honest page that uses it 10 times.

%%widget bm25 BM25 term-frequency saturation: each extra occurrence adds less, approaching k1 + 1. Longer documents need more occurrences to reach the same score.
%%

## b: length normalisation
Now the second fix. A match in a 50-word product title means more than the same match in a 10,000-word manual that mentions everything. Long documents contain more words, so they match more queries and have higher term counts by sheer size. Some normalisation is needed, but how much? Sometimes long documents are long because they ramble; sometimes because they genuinely cover more.

BM25 compares each document's length with the average using the **length factor**:

`L = 1 − b + b × |D| / avgdl`

and multiplies k1 by it in the denominator. Look at the cases:
- A document of **exactly average** length has |D| / avgdl = 1, so L = 1, and nothing changes.
- A document **twice** the average length, with b = 0.75: L = 0.25 + 0.75 × 2 = 1.75. The effective k1 becomes 1.2 × 1.75 = 2.1, so the document needs more occurrences to earn the same score.
- A document **half** the average: L = 0.25 + 0.375 = 0.625. Effective k1 is 0.75, so it saturates quickly and a couple of mentions count for a lot.

The default is **b = 0.75**. **b = 0** turns length normalisation off entirely (L is always 1). **b = 1** applies it fully, in direct proportion to length.

%%compare Two documents scored for "shoes" with k1 = 1.2, b = 0.75 and an average length of 100. The short, focused one wins despite fewer mentions.
[*Doc A: 50 words]
2 mentions -(L = 0.625)-> tf part 1.60
+ Short and focused
+ Saturates after a few mentions
[Doc B: 400 words]
6 mentions -(L = 3.25)-> tf part 1.33
- Long, mentions spread thin
- Needs many more mentions to compete
%%

! Example: two documents for "shoes", worked in full
! Settings: avgdl = 100, k1 = 1.2, b = 0.75, so k1 + 1 = 2.2. Both documents get the same IDF for "shoes", so compare only the term-frequency parts.
! Doc A: 50 words, "shoes" twice. L = 1 − 0.75 + 0.75 × (50 / 100) = 0.25 + 0.375 = 0.625. Denominator = 2 + 1.2 × 0.625 = 2.75. Numerator = 2 × 2.2 = 4.4. tf part = 4.4 / 2.75 = 1.60.
! Doc B: 400 words, "shoes" 6 times. L = 0.25 + 0.75 × 4 = 3.25. Denominator = 6 + 1.2 × 3.25 = 9.9. Numerator = 6 × 2.2 = 13.2. tf part = 13.2 / 9.9 = 1.33.
! Doc A wins despite three times fewer mentions, because it is short and focused. With b = 0 (no length normalisation), Doc A would score 4.4 / 3.2 = 1.38 and Doc B 13.2 / 7.2 = 1.83, and B would win.

## Tuning the knobs
Defaults work well across many collections, which is why they're defaults. But fields differ, and Elasticsearch lets you set k1 and b per field.

| Knob | Default | Raise it when | Lower it when |
| k1 | 1.2 | Repetition genuinely signals relevance (long articles, books) | Fields are short and repetition is noise (titles, tags) |
| b | 0.75 | Long documents ramble and should be penalised | Long documents are long because they have more to say (detailed specs) |

Typical adjustments:
- **Titles and names**: short, every word counts once. Lower k1 (0.5 or less) so one mention is nearly as good as several, and lower b because title length varies little.
- **Tags or keyword lists**: often b = 0 and low k1, since a tag either applies or it doesn't.
- **Long body text**: defaults, or a slightly higher k1.

Change knobs only with an evaluation set (lesson 09.09): a list of queries with known good results, measured before and after. Tuning by eyeballing a few queries usually makes others worse.

### Multi-field scoring
Real documents have several fields: title, body, brand, description. BM25 scores each field separately, using that field's own lengths and statistics. You then combine them: `best_fields` takes the best field's score (plus a bit of the others with `tie_breaker`), `most_fields` sums them, and `cross_fields` treats several fields as one big field so a query like "john smith" can match "john" in first name and "smith" in last name. Field **boosts** (title × 3) express that a title match is worth more than a body match. Getting field combination right often matters more than tuning k1 and b.

## Where BM25 sits in a real system
BM25 is cheap, needs no training data, is easy to explain, and is surprisingly hard to beat as a first-stage scorer. Production systems usually use it to pick the top few hundred or thousand candidates from the inverted index, then re-rank those with a learned model that adds popularity, freshness, personalisation and click signals (lesson 09.08). Many now also combine it with **vector search** (lesson 09.13), because BM25 only matches exact terms: it can't tell that "sneakers" and "trainers" mean the same thing.

%%flow Two-stage ranking: BM25 cheaply narrows millions of matches to a short list; an expensive model orders that list.
Millions of matches -(BM25 via postings)-> *Top 1,000 candidates -(learned re-ranker)-> Top 10 shown
%%

BM25's per-term contribution has a known maximum (IDF × (k1 + 1)), which lets engines use dynamic pruning such as **block-max WAND**: skip blocks of postings whose best possible score can't beat the current 10th result. That's why Elasticsearch can return the top 10 BM25 results from millions of matches without scoring them all.

! Example: keyword stuffing, before and after
! A spam page repeats "cheap flights" 200 times in 1,000 words. A good travel page uses "cheap flights" 4 times in 300 words. avgdl = 500.
! With raw tf × idf, the spam page's term weight is 50 times higher (200 versus 4). With BM25 (k1 = 1.2, b = 0.75): spam L = 0.25 + 0.75 × 2 = 1.75, tf part = 200 × 2.2 / (200 + 2.1) = 2.18. Good page L = 0.25 + 0.75 × 0.6 = 0.7, tf part = 4 × 2.2 / (4 + 0.84) = 1.82. The spam page's advantage shrinks from 50 times to about 1.2 times, small enough that quality signals in the re-ranker easily outweigh it.

## Variants worth knowing
- **BM25F**: combines fields before saturation (weights each field's tf, sums them, then saturates once), which is more principled than summing per-field scores. Elasticsearch's `combined_fields` query is based on it.
- **BM25+**: adds a small constant so that a very long document containing the term always scores more than one that doesn't contain it at all.

## Common mistakes
- Assuming BM25 scores are comparable across queries or indexes. They aren't probabilities; use them only to rank within one query.
- Tuning k1 and b globally when the real problem is field weighting or query understanding.
- Forgetting that scores use per-shard statistics; on small or skewed shards, IDF can differ between shards.
- Expecting BM25 to handle synonyms or misspellings. It matches analysed terms exactly.

## In an interview
Say "BM25 is tf-idf with two fixes: term frequency saturates (k1, default 1.2) so repetition stops paying, and length is normalised against the average document (b, default 0.75)." Sketch the saturating curve. Then place it: "first-stage retrieval with BM25, re-rank the top few hundred with a learned model, optionally fuse with vector search."

> Takeaway: BM25 is tf-idf with saturation (k1, default 1.2, so repeated words approach a ceiling of k1 + 1 and stop adding much) and length normalisation relative to the average document (b, default 0.75). It is the default first-stage ranker in Lucene-based engines, usually followed by a learned re-ranker.

## Two ways to do less work
A search engine's query cost is dominated by postings: how many entries it reads and how many documents it scores. This lesson covers two classic ways to cut that cost. **Stop words** remove the most expensive terms from the index altogether. **Champion lists** keep every term but score only a small, precomputed shortlist of documents per term. Both trade something away for speed, and knowing exactly what they trade is the point of the lesson.

## Why common words are expensive
Word frequencies in any language follow **Zipf's law**: the most common word appears about twice as often as the second, three times as often as the third, and so on. In English text, "the" alone is around 5 to 7% of all words, and the top 100 words make up roughly half of all text.

For an inverted index, this means a handful of terms have enormous postings lists. In a collection of 100 million documents, "the" appears in nearly all of them: a 100-million-entry list. A query containing "the" would read that list, and if scored, touch nearly every document. That's the cost stop words were invented to avoid.

%%bars Approximate share of all word occurrences in English text covered by the most frequent words. A tiny vocabulary makes up a huge share of postings.
Top 1 word (the) | 6 | ~6%
Top 10 words | 23 | ~23%
Top 100 words | 50 | ~50%
*Top 1,000 words | 70 | ~70%
%%

## Stop words
**Stop words** are extremely common words, such as "the", "a", "of", "to", "and", "in", that carry little meaning on their own. A **stop list** is the set you drop. It's usually built like this: sort terms by **collection frequency** (total occurrences across all documents), take the most frequent ones, and check them by hand for meaning in your domain (in a legal corpus "court" might be extremely common but must stay). At indexing time, stop words are not added to the index; at query time, they're removed from the query.

The benefit is real. Because stop words have the longest postings lists, dropping a few dozen can shrink an uncompressed index by a quarter or more, and queries never read those lists.

### What goes wrong
- **Phrase queries break.** "To be or not to be" consists entirely of stop words. With a typical stop list, the query becomes empty. "President of the United States" loses "of the", and "The Who" (the band) becomes just "who".
- **Meaning changes.** "Flights to London" and "flights from London" become the same query, "flights london". "Vitamin A" loses "a". "Not working" loses "not" in some lists.
- **Names and titles vanish.** "Let It Be", "The The", "IT department", "Take That".
- **Position-based features break**: if stop words are removed before positions are assigned, "new york" might match "new in york".

%%compare Dropping stop words versus keeping them. Modern engines keep them because compression and IDF remove most of the cost.
[Stop list]
"to be or not to be" -(remove stop words)-> Empty query
+ Smaller index
+ Never reads huge lists
- Phrases and titles break
- "to London" equals "from London"
[*Keep stop words]
"to be or not to be" -(idf near 0)-> Phrase match works
+ Exact phrases and names work
+ Common words barely affect score
- Larger postings, needs compression
%%

### Why modern engines don't need them
Stop lists have shrunk over time: from lists of 200 to 300 terms, to very small lists of 7 to 12 terms, to none at all. Web search engines generally don't use stop lists, and Elasticsearch's standard analyser has no stop list by default. Three techniques removed the need:
- **Compression.** Long postings lists of a common word have tiny gaps between docIDs (lesson 09.02), so they compress extremely well, often to a bit or two per posting. Storage cost drops sharply.
- **IDF weighting.** Ranking already gives common words almost no weight (lesson 09.04): "the" gets idf near zero, so keeping it doesn't distort scores.
- **Impact-ordered postings and dynamic pruning.** Query processing can stop reading a common term's list early, or skip it entirely during scoring, once it's clear the term can't change the top results. Algorithms like WAND and block-max WAND do this automatically.

A middle path is also common: index stop words, but treat them specially. For example, use **common-word bigrams** ("the_who", "to_be") so phrases with stop words stay cheap, or treat stop words as optional in the query (`should` clauses) so they help ranking when present but aren't required to match.

! Example: the cost of keeping "the"
! An index of 100 million documents. "the" appears in 98 million. Uncompressed at 4 bytes per docID, its postings would be 392 MB. With gap encoding, the average gap is about 1, which bit-packs to 1 or 2 bits per posting: roughly 15 to 25 MB. Add positions and it's larger, but the index for one word costs tens of megabytes, not hundreds. At query time, block-max WAND notices "the" can contribute almost nothing to any score and reads only the blocks it needs for phrase checks. Keeping "the" costs a few percent of index size and makes "The Who" findable.

## Champion lists
Even with an inverted index and no stop words, a single query term can match hundreds of thousands of documents. The query "insurance" in a large collection might match 400,000 documents, and fully scoring all of them (BM25 plus quality signals) for every query is slow. **Champion lists** (also called **fancy lists** or **top docs**) cut that work.

The idea:
1. **At indexing time**, for each term t, precompute the r documents with the highest weight for t (for example, highest tf, or highest tf-idf). This short list is t's champion list.
2. **At query time**, take the **union** of the champion lists of the query terms and compute full scores only for those documents.
3. Return the top K of those.

With r = 500 and a two-word query, at most 1,000 documents are scored, regardless of how many contain the terms.

%%flow Champion lists: precompute each term's best r documents at index time, then score only their union at query time.
[Index time] Term "insurance" -(keep top r by weight)-> *Champion list: r docs
[Query time] Query terms -(union of champion lists)-> Small candidate set -(full score)-> Top K
%%

### What you trade away
- **Exactness.** A document that is good overall but not in any single term's top r is never considered. Champion lists approximate the true top K; they don't guarantee it.
- **Combination effects.** A document that mentions both terms moderately (say, ranked 600th for each term alone) may be the best match for the pair, but it's in neither list and will be missed.
- **Filling the page.** If r is smaller than K (the number of results you need), or if most champions fail the query's filters (out of stock, wrong language), you may not have enough results.
- **Query-blind choice.** r is chosen at index time, before the query is known. r can vary by term, for example larger for rarer terms or for terms often combined with filters, but it's always a guess.
- **Freshness.** Champion lists must be recomputed as documents change; a new, excellent document may not be a champion until the next rebuild.

%%matrix What each speed-up costs. All of them trade some exactness or flexibility for less work per query.
Technique | Index size | Query speed | Exact top K | Phrases work
Stop list | yes, smaller | yes, faster | partial | no
Keep stop words + compression | partial, a bit larger | partial | yes | yes
Champion lists | partial, extra lists | yes, much faster | no | yes
Tiered index | partial, extra tiers | yes, faster | no | yes
Block-max WAND | yes, small metadata | yes, faster | yes | yes
%%

### Static quality scores
Champion lists get much better when combined with a **static quality score** g(d): a query-independent measure of how good a document is, such as PageRank for web pages, review score and sales for products, or reputation for answers. Two uses:
- Build each champion list from a combination of the term's weight and g(d), so champions are both relevant to the term and good in general.
- Order postings by g(d) descending, so the best documents are read first and a query can stop after finding enough high-scoring candidates.

The final score is then something like `score(q, d) = g(d) + cosine(q, d)`. Because g(d) is the same for every query, high-quality documents get a head start, and the engine can stop once no remaining document could beat the current top K.

A further refinement is **high and low lists**: each term's postings are split into a high list (its champions) and a low list (the rest). The query scores high lists first and only consults low lists if it can't fill K results. This is the per-term version of the tiered index from lesson 09.03.

! Example: champion lists for "cheap flights"
! r = 500. "cheap" has 2,000,000 documents and "flights" has 300,000. Fully scoring the union of their postings could mean scoring over 2 million documents. With champion lists, you take the 500 best for "cheap" and the 500 best for "flights", a union of at most 1,000 (in practice maybe 800, since some overlap), and score those fully: roughly 2,500 times less scoring work.
! What's lost: a travel deals page ranked 600th for "cheap" and 700th for "flights" individually, but excellent for the phrase "cheap flights", is in neither list and is never seen. If many such pages exist, the results are worse. That's the accuracy given up for speed, and why systems that use champion lists check with evaluation metrics (lesson 09.09) how often the true top 10 is missed.

## Where you see these ideas today
- **Elasticsearch / Lucene** keep stop words by default and rely on compression and block-max WAND, which gives champion-list-like speed without losing exactness.
- **Web-scale engines** use static quality ordering and tiers so most queries are answered from a small, high-quality slice of the index.
- **Two-stage ranking** (lesson 09.05) is the modern descendant: a cheap stage picks a shortlist, an expensive stage scores only that.

## Common mistakes
- Applying a large stop list to product or name search, so "The North Face" or "Vitamin A" can't be found.
- Removing stop words at indexing time but not at query time (or the reverse), breaking matches.
- Using champion lists with r smaller than the page size plus the expected filter loss.
- Never measuring how often approximation drops the true best result.

## In an interview
If asked about stop words, explain the trade (smaller index versus broken phrases and meanings) and say modern engines keep them, relying on compression, IDF and dynamic pruning. For champion lists, state the trade precisely: faster queries by scoring only each term's precomputed top r, at the cost of missing documents that are strong for the combined query but not top for any single term.

> Takeaway: stop lists shrink the index but break phrases and meaning, so modern engines keep stop words and rely on compression, IDF and pruning. Champion lists precompute each term's top r documents so queries only score a small candidate set, trading exactness (and sometimes a full page) for speed.

## Why query understanding comes first
**Query understanding** is everything that happens to a query **before** retrieval and ranking: working out what the searcher means from what they typed. It's easy to underrate, because most engineering attention goes to ranking. But ranking can only order documents that retrieval found, and retrieval can only find what the interpreted query asks for. If the engine thinks "apple" means fruit when the user wants a phone charger, no ranking model can fix the result.

Think of search as a communication channel. The searcher has an **intent** in their head ("cheap Nike trainers for running, under $100"). They **encode** it in a few words, quickly, often misspelled ("nike runing shoes under 100"). The engine must **decode** it. Queries are short (two to three words on average in web and e-commerce search), ambiguous, and full of noise. Query understanding is the decoder.

The payoff is large. In e-commerce, a meaningful share of queries (often 10 to 20%) return zero or poor results because of misspellings, vocabulary mismatch or over-specific queries. Fixing interpretation for those queries usually moves revenue more than any ranking change.

## The stack, from characters to meaning
Query understanding is a stack of layers. Each one works on the output of the one below, from raw characters up to the searcher's intent.

%%layers The query understanding stack, from raw characters up to the searcher's intent.
Characters | normalisation (case, accents, Unicode), tokenisation
Words | stemming and lemmatisation, spelling correction
Phrases | segmentation ("new york" is one unit), entity recognition
*Meaning | synonyms, taxonomies, classification of intent
Rewriting | relaxation, expansion, field restriction
Interaction | autocomplete, context, personalisation, conversation
%%

### 1. Character filtering and normalisation
Make equivalent strings identical. Lower-case ("iPhone" to "iphone"). Fold accents ("café" to "cafe", "Zürich" to "zurich"). Apply **Unicode normalisation** so that "é" typed as one code point and "e" plus a combining accent compare equal. Unify punctuation and quotes, full-width and half-width characters (common in Japanese input), and strip invisible control characters.

### 2. Tokenisation
Split the string into **tokens**. It's harder than splitting on spaces:
- "wi-fi", "e-mail": one token or two? Index both forms.
- "C++", "C#", ".NET", "AT&T": naive tokenisers strip the symbols and lose the meaning.
- "iphone15" versus "iphone 15": split letters from digits.
- Chinese, Japanese and Thai don't use spaces between words, so tokenisation needs a dictionary or a statistical segmenter, or the engine indexes overlapping character pairs (bigrams).

### 3. Stemming and lemmatisation
Map word variants to a common form so they match.
- **Stemming** chops suffixes with rules. The Porter stemmer turns "running" and "runs" into "run", and "connection" and "connected" into "connect". Fast, language-specific, and sometimes wrong: "university" and "universe" both become "univers".
- **Lemmatisation** uses a dictionary and part of speech to find the true base form: "ran" to "run", "better" to "good", "mice" to "mouse". More accurate, slower.
Over-stemming merges words that shouldn't match ("news" to "new"); under-stemming misses words that should. E-commerce engines often prefer light stemming (just plurals) because "shoes" to "shoe" is safe but aggressive stemming causes odd matches.

### 4. Spelling correction
Fix typos such as "runing" or "iphnoe" using edit distance plus word frequency and context (lesson 09.15). Decide whether to correct silently, search both forms, or ask "Did you mean...?".

### 5. Query segmentation
Decide which words belong together as a unit. "new york times square" should be "new york" + "times square", not "new" + "york times" + "square". Segmentation uses statistics from documents and query logs: how often words appear together compared with how often you'd expect by chance (measures such as **pointwise mutual information**). Segments then become phrase queries or boosted proximity matches.

### 6. Entity recognition
Tag parts of the query with types: brands ("nike"), places ("leeds"), people, categories ("running shoes"), attributes ("red", "size 9"), and numeric constraints ("under $100", "2 bedroom"). Usually done with a dictionary of known values from your catalogue (all brands, all colours) plus a trained sequence model for ambiguous cases.

### 7. Query classification
Map the whole query to a category or intent:
- **Navigational**: the user wants a specific page or product ("nike login", "iphone 15 pro 256gb blue").
- **Informational**: they want to learn ("how to clean suede").
- **Transactional or browse**: they want to shop a category ("running shoes").
Category classification ("this query belongs to Footwear > Running") lets the engine filter or boost the right department. It's usually a text classifier trained on which categories users clicked or bought from for each query.

### 8. Synonyms and taxonomies
Know that "sofa" and "couch" mean the same, that "tv" equals "television", that "sneakers" and "trainers" are regional synonyms, and that "sneakers" is a kind of "shoes" (a **hypernym** relation in a taxonomy). Synonyms can be applied at index time (store both forms) or at query time (expand the query); query time is easier to change without reindexing. Be careful with one-directional relations: "shoes" should not be expanded to every kind of shoe, but "sneakers" can safely include "trainers".

### 9. Query rewriting
Change the query to retrieve better results:
- **Expansion**: add synonyms or related terms (lesson 09.16) to increase recall.
- **Relaxation**: if nothing matches all the terms, drop the least important one. "nike air max 90 red size 9 waterproof" with zero results might drop "waterproof" and say so.
- **Field restriction**: search "nike" only in the brand field rather than everywhere, so a product description that mentions "unlike nike" doesn't match.
- **Filter extraction**: turn "under $100" into a structured `price < 100` filter instead of text.

### 10. Autocomplete, context, personalisation and conversation
Guide users while they type (lesson 09.14), so many queries are well-formed before they're submitted. Use **context**: the page they're on, their location, the previous query in the session ("cheaper ones"). **Personalise** carefully: a user who always buys women's clothing may want "jeans" interpreted that way. And when the engine is unsure, **ask**: facets, "Did you mean Apple the brand or apples the fruit?", or a conversational follow-up.

## A worked pass
%%flow One query moving through the stack before it ever touches the index.
[Text] "Nike runing shoes under $100" -(normalise, tokenise)-> nike runing shoes under 100 -(spell, stem)-> nike run shoe
[Meaning] nike run shoe -(entities)-> brand nike, price under 100 -(rewrite)-> *Structured query
%%

! Example: "Nike runing shoes under $100"
! Normalise: "nike runing shoes under $100". Tokenise: nike, runing, shoes, under, $100. Spell-correct: "runing" is not in the vocabulary; the closest frequent word at edit distance 1 is "running". Stem lightly: running → run, shoes → shoe.
! Segment: "running shoes" is a known category phrase. Recognise entities: "nike" matches the brand dictionary, "under $100" matches a price pattern. Classify: category Footwear > Running, high confidence.
! Rewrite into a structured query: `brand = nike AND category = running-shoes AND price < 100`, with free text "run shoe" scored by BM25 against title and description, and "trainers" added as a synonym. Only now does the engine look up postings and rank. Without the price and brand extraction, BM25 would have tried to match the literal tokens "under" and "100" in product text, and returned products whose descriptions mention "100% cotton".

## Latency budget and architecture
All of this must happen in a few milliseconds. A typical budget for an e-commerce search request is 100 to 200 ms total, of which query understanding gets perhaps 5 to 20 ms. That means:
- Dictionaries (brands, synonyms, categories) loaded in memory, refreshed periodically.
- Small, fast models (a classifier that runs in under a millisecond) on the hot path; larger models (an LLM rewriting the query) only for the long tail, or precomputed offline for frequent queries.
- **Caching by query string**: the most frequent 10,000 queries make up a large share of traffic, so their interpretations can be precomputed and cached.

%%seq The query understanding service sits in front of the search engine and returns a structured query within a small latency budget.
User > Search API : "nike runing shoes under $100"
Search API > QU service : interpret
QU service > QU service : cache miss: normalise, spell, tag
QU service -> Search API : brand, category, price filter, text
Search API > Search engine : structured query
Search engine -> Search API : top 48 products
Search API -> User : results + "showing results for running"
%%

! Example: when relaxation saves a query
! "red nike air max 90 size 9 waterproof" returns zero results: no product has all those attributes. The rewriter ranks the constraints by importance learned from logs (brand and model matter most, "waterproof" least), drops "waterproof", and finds 14 products. The page says "No exact matches for waterproof. Showing red Nike Air Max 90 in size 9." A zero-result page becomes a sale. Logging which constraint was dropped also tells the merchandising team that customers want a waterproof version.

## How to judge it
The measure of success is **query performance**: whether queries lead to successful outcomes such as clicks, add-to-carts or purchases, and whether zero-result and reformulation rates go down. Evaluate each step by whether it improves those, not by whether it's linguistically correct. An aggressive stemmer that is "more correct" linguistically may hurt conversions. Run changes as A/B tests and track per-query-segment metrics (head, torso and tail queries behave differently).

## Common mistakes
- Different analysis at index and query time, so terms don't line up.
- Treating every token as text, so "under", "$100" and "size 9" are matched as words instead of becoming filters.
- Over-expanding synonyms, so "apple" matches "fruit" for a phone accessories shop.
- Correcting valid brand names or product codes as typos ("Asics" to "basics").
- Shipping changes without measuring zero-result rate and conversion per query segment.

## In an interview
List the steps in order quickly: normalise, tokenise, stem or lemmatise, spell-correct, segment, recognise entities, classify intent, rewrite (expand, restrict to fields, relax). Then show judgement: keep the hot path cheap with in-memory dictionaries and caching, use a model only where it pays, and measure with zero-result rate and conversions.

> Takeaway: before retrieval, a query is normalised, tokenised, stemmed, spell-checked, segmented into phrases, tagged with entities and intent, and rewritten (expanded, restricted to fields, relaxed). Retrieval and ranking can only work with the interpretation this produces, so it's judged by search outcomes, not linguistic purity.

## What feedback is
BM25 (lesson 09.05) ranks by text alone. It knows nothing about which results people actually found useful. Every day, though, your users run millions of queries and react to the results: they click, skip, buy, come back, rephrase. That behaviour is the richest relevance signal you have. **Relevance feedback** is the general name for using people's reactions to improve ranking.

It comes in two kinds:
- **Explicit feedback**: the user says which results were good, for example by marking them relevant, rating them, or answering "Was this helpful?". It's accurate but rare, because few users bother. Human raters hired to judge results are also explicit feedback, and they're expensive.
- **Implicit feedback**: the engine infers relevance from behaviour. A click suggests a result looked relevant. A result skipped while a lower one was clicked suggests it looked worse. A purchase, a long dwell time on the page, or a session that ends after the click (the user found what they wanted) are stronger signals. Implicit feedback is plentiful and free, but noisy and biased, as this lesson explains.

## Rocchio and query modification
The oldest technique is **Rocchio's algorithm**, from the SMART retrieval system of the 1960s. It works in the vector space model (lesson 09.04): documents and queries are vectors of term weights. After the user marks some results relevant and others not, Rocchio moves the query vector **towards** the centroid (average) of the relevant documents and **away from** the centroid of the non-relevant ones:

`q' = α·q + β·mean(relevant) − γ·mean(non-relevant)`

Typical weights are α = 1, β = 0.75, γ = 0.15: the original query matters most, positive evidence a lot, negative evidence a little. The new query picks up terms common in the relevant documents, so a search for "jaguar" where the user marked car pages relevant gains "engine", "sedan" and "dealer", and drifts away from "habitat" and "rainforest".

A variant, **pseudo-relevance feedback** (or blind feedback), skips the user entirely: assume the top 10 results are relevant, extract their most distinctive terms, add them to the query and search again. It often improves recall, but if the top results were wrong, it drifts further off-topic (**query drift**).

Lucene's **MoreLikeThis** query is a modern relative: given a document the user liked, it picks that document's most distinctive terms (high tf-idf) and builds a query from them, so you can show "similar items".

Today the main use of feedback is not re-querying in the moment. It's **training data for ranking models**.

## Learning to rank
**Learning to rank** (LTR) means training a machine-learning model to order results, using examples of good and bad orderings. The standard setup, as in Elasticsearch and OpenSearch:
1. A cheap **first-stage** query (BM25, filters, maybe vector search) retrieves candidates.
2. A trained model **re-scores the top N results** (say 100 to 1,000). It runs as a **rescorer**, not over the whole index, because evaluating hundreds of features per document is expensive.
3. The final order shown to the user comes from the model.

%%flow Learning to rank: logs and judgments train a model offline; the model re-ranks the first stage's top results online.
[Offline] Click logs and raters -> Judgment list -(features per query and doc)-> *Train model
[Online] Query -(BM25)-> Top N candidates -(rescore with model)-> Final order
%%

### The pieces
- **Judgment list**: a set of queries, each with candidate documents and a relevance **grade**, such as 0 (bad) to 4 (perfect). Grades come from human raters, or are derived from user behaviour such as clicks and purchases (after correcting for bias, below). A useful list has thousands of queries, sampled across frequent ("head") and rare ("tail") queries.
- **Features**, of three kinds:
  - **Document features**, independent of the query: popularity, price, recency, rating, number of reviews, return rate.
  - **Query features**, independent of the document: query length, detected category, whether it contains a brand.
  - **Query-document features**: how well this document matches this query, such as the BM25 score of the title field, BM25 of the description, whether the brand matches, the vector similarity, the historical click-through rate of this document for this query.
- **Model**: typically **gradient-boosted decision trees** trained with the **LambdaMART** objective, which directly optimises a ranking metric such as NDCG (lesson 09.09) rather than predicting each grade independently. Trained with XGBoost or LightGBM outside the search engine, then uploaded to it. Neural re-rankers (cross-encoders that read query and document together) are increasingly common for text-heavy search.

%%tree The three families of learning-to-rank features, with typical examples of each.
Features for one query-document pair
  Document only
    popularity, sales last 30 days
    rating, review count
    age, freshness
  Query only
    query length
    detected category
  *Query and document together
    BM25 title, BM25 body
    brand match, category match
    past click rate for this pair
%%

! Example: building judgments from a click log
! For the query "wireless earbuds", the logs over 30 days hold 80,000 searches. For each product shown, compute a label from behaviour: purchased after click = grade 4, added to cart = 3, clicked with long dwell = 2, clicked and bounced back in under 10 seconds = 1, shown but never clicked (after position correction) = 0. Repeat for the top 5,000 queries. For each (query, product) pair, compute 60 features. Train LambdaMART on 80% of queries, test on 20%, and compare NDCG@10 with plain BM25. If it improves from 0.61 to 0.68 offline, run an A/B test before rolling out.

## Why clicks are biased
Clicks are the cheapest implicit signal and the most dangerous. A click depends not only on whether a result is relevant, but also on **whether the user saw it** and **how attractive it looked**. Several biases distort them.

| Bias | What happens | Common correction |
| Position bias | Users click higher results far more often regardless of quality, because they read top-down and often stop early | Estimate how likely each position is to be examined and reweight clicks by its inverse; randomise order slightly for a small share of traffic |
| Presentation bias | Results with images, ratings, badges or bold snippets attract clicks | Include presentation as a feature so the model can separate it |
| Selection bias | You only get feedback on results you showed; anything never shown gets no clicks | Occasional exploration: show new items in some slots |
| Trust bias | Users trust the engine and click its top result even when a lower one is better | Combine with post-click signals |
| Click is not satisfaction | Misleading titles get clicks followed by a quick return | Use dwell time, purchases or no return to results as stronger signals |

%%bars Typical share of users who even look at each result position on a list page. Clicks at lower positions are rare largely because few people get that far.
Position 1 | 95 | ~95% examine
Position 2 | 70 | ~70%
Position 3 | 50 | ~50%
Position 5 | 30 | ~30%
*Position 10 | 12 | ~12%
%%

### The feedback loop
The danger of ignoring bias is a **self-reinforcing loop**: the model learns that whatever was on top is good, ranks it on top again, it collects more clicks because it's on top, and the model becomes more certain. New or better items never get shown high enough to collect the clicks they'd need to rise. Rankings freeze.

%%flow The position-bias loop: training on raw clicks teaches the model to keep whatever it already ranked first.
Result shown first -(gets most clicks)-> Clicks used as labels -(model learns)-> *Ranked first again
%%

### Correcting it
- **Inverse propensity weighting**: estimate the **propensity** (probability of being examined) for each position, then weight each click by 1 / propensity. A click at position 8, where only 15% of users look, counts about six times as much as a click at position 1. Propensities are estimated with small randomisation experiments: swap results between positions for a tiny fraction of traffic and see how click rates change.
- **Click models**: probabilistic models of how users scan a page (the **cascade model** assumes users read top-down and stop at the first satisfying result) that separate "examined" from "attractive" from "satisfying".
- **Pairwise preferences**: treat "skipped result 2, clicked result 3" as evidence that 3 beats 2 for this query, which is less sensitive to absolute position effects.
- **Exploration**: reserve a small share of slots or traffic for items the model is unsure about, so new items get a chance (a bandit approach).

! Example: reading a click log
! For "wireless earbuds", result 1 gets 40% of clicks and result 3 gets 12%. Raw clicks say result 1 is more than three times as attractive. But randomisation experiments on your page (where tall product cards push result 3 below the fold, so it is seen less than the typical curve above suggests) show position 1 is examined by 95% of users and position 3 by 30%. Divide: result 1's click rate among users who looked is 40 / 95 ≈ 42%; result 3's is 12 / 30 = 40%. They are almost equally attractive. The raw numbers mostly measured position. Training on raw clicks would have taught the model that result 1 is far better, keeping it on top forever.

## Online evaluation
Offline metrics on judgment lists (lesson 09.09) guide development, but the final test is live behaviour. Two common methods:
- **A/B tests**: split users into groups, give each a different ranker, and compare business metrics (conversion, revenue per search, zero-result rate, reformulation rate) over a week or two.
- **Interleaving**: merge results from rankers A and B into one list for the same user, and see whose results get clicked more. It needs far less traffic than an A/B test to detect a difference, because each user compares both directly.

## Common mistakes
- Training on raw clicks without correcting for position, creating a feedback loop.
- Using clicks alone when purchases or dwell time are available; clickbait wins.
- Running the learned model over the whole index instead of re-scoring the top N.
- Features that leak the label, such as "was clicked in the same visit", so offline metrics look great and online results don't move.
- Never exploring, so new products can't earn their way up.

## In an interview
When asked how to use clicks to improve search, say: "log impressions, clicks and conversions with positions; turn them into graded labels with position-bias correction; train a LambdaMART re-ranker on document, query and query-document features; re-score BM25's top few hundred; validate with NDCG offline and an A/B test or interleaving online." Then name position bias as the main trap.

> Takeaway: feedback can be explicit (rare, accurate) or implicit (clicks, skips, purchases). Today it mostly trains learning-to-rank models that re-score the first stage's top results using document, query and query-document features. Raw clicks are biased, above all by position, and must be corrected before being used as labels, or the ranker locks in its own past choices.

?? Clicks depend on where and how a result was shown, not only on how relevant it is. The main example is position bias: people click higher results much more often simply because they look at them first and often stop early, so a mediocre top result can collect more clicks than an excellent one at position 5. Other examples are presentation bias (images and ratings attract clicks), selection bias (results never shown get no clicks) and clickbait (a click followed by a quick return). @09.09
## Why you need numbers
Every change to a search system (a new analyser, a synonym list, BM25 settings, a re-ranker) makes some queries better and some worse. Looking at a handful of queries by eye is how teams fool themselves: the queries you think of are the ones you just fixed. To know whether a change helps overall, you need **metrics** computed over many queries, and to choose the right metric you need to know what users lose when the system is wrong.

Evaluation starts with a **test collection**: a set of documents, a set of queries (ideally sampled from real logs, covering frequent and rare ones), and **relevance judgments** saying which documents are relevant to each query. Judgments come from trained human raters, or are derived from user behaviour such as clicks and purchases (lesson 09.08).

## Why accuracy is the wrong measure
The obvious measure, **accuracy** (the fraction of documents classified correctly as relevant or not), is useless for search. For a typical query, well over 99.9% of documents are irrelevant. A system that returns **nothing at all** labels every document "not relevant" and is 99.9% accurate. Search needs measures that look only at the relevant documents and at what was retrieved, ignoring the vast pool of correctly ignored documents.

## Precision and recall
For one query, split all documents by two questions: was it retrieved, and is it relevant?

%%matrix The four outcomes for one query. Precision and recall ignore the huge true-negative cell.
Outcome | Relevant | Not relevant
Retrieved | true positive (tp) | false positive (fp)
Not retrieved | false negative (fn) | true negative (tn)
%%

- **Precision** = tp / (tp + fp): of what I returned, how much was relevant?
- **Recall** = tp / (tp + fn): of everything relevant, how much did I return?

Precision is about **noise** in the results. Recall is about **gaps**: things that should have been found and weren't.

! Example: computing both
! A collection holds 30 documents relevant to "contract termination clause". The system returns 20 results, of which 12 are relevant. Precision = 12 / 20 = 60%: 8 of the 20 results are noise. Recall = 12 / 30 = 40%: 18 relevant documents were missed. Accuracy over a million documents would be (1,000,000 − 8 − 18) / 1,000,000 = 99.997%, which tells you nothing.

### They pull against each other
Recall **never decreases** as you return more documents: returning the whole collection gives 100% recall and terrible precision. Returning one sure-fire result can give 100% precision and terrible recall. Most tuning moves along that trade-off: looser matching (OR instead of AND, synonyms, spelling correction, stemming, vector search) raises recall and usually lowers precision; stricter matching does the opposite.

%%bars How one query's precision and recall change as the system returns more results. Recall only rises; precision usually falls.
P at 5 results | 80 | precision 80%
R at 5 results | 13 | recall 13%
P at 20 results | 60 | precision 60%
R at 20 results | 40 | recall 40%
P at 100 results | 25 | precision 25%
*R at 100 results | 83 | recall 83%
%%

## When recall matters more
Which measure matters depends on the cost of each kind of mistake for this user.
- **Precision matters** for web search and shopping. Users look at the first page, maybe the first few results, and don't care whether the 5,000th relevant page was found. Irrelevant results on page one cost attention and trust.
- **Recall matters** when missing a relevant document is costly and reading extra results is cheap by comparison:
  - **Legal discovery**: paralegals must find every relevant email or contract in a lawsuit. Missing one can lose the case or bring sanctions; reading a few hundred extra documents is just work.
  - **Intelligence analysis**: an analyst must find every message mentioning a threat.
  - **Patent prior-art search**: one missed earlier patent can invalidate a new one.
  - **Medical systematic reviews**: a review must include every relevant clinical trial, or its conclusions are biased.
  - **Compliance and safety**: finding every transaction matching a sanctions rule, every product affected by a recall.
  - **The candidate-retrieval stage** of any two-stage ranker (lesson 09.05): whatever isn't retrieved can never be re-ranked up, so the first stage should favour recall and leave precision to the re-ranker.
  - **Search over your own data**: "find my email from the plumber" has exactly one relevant result, and failing to return it is total failure.

Professional searchers in recall-heavy fields will read many results, write long Boolean queries and iterate. That's why legal and patent search tools still offer Boolean operators (lesson 09.03).

## The F measure
To compare systems with one number, use the **F measure**, a weighted **harmonic mean** of precision and recall. The balanced version is

`F1 = 2PR / (P + R)`

Why harmonic rather than a plain average? Because the harmonic mean punishes imbalance. A system that returns one relevant document out of 50 has P = 1.0 and R = 0.02. The arithmetic mean says 0.51, which looks respectable. F1 says about 0.04, which is honest. You can't game F1 by maximising one side.

The general form `Fβ = (1 + β²)PR / (β²P + R)` lets you weight the trade: **β > 1 favours recall** (F2 counts recall as twice as important), **β < 1 favours precision** (F0.5).

! Example: two systems for one query
! There are 10 relevant documents. System A returns 5 results, 4 relevant: P = 0.8, R = 0.4, F1 = 2 × 0.8 × 0.4 / 1.2 = 0.53. System B returns 20 results, 8 relevant: P = 0.4, R = 0.8, F1 = 0.53.
! Same F1, different products. A suits a shopper who wants a short, clean list. B suits a paralegal who must not miss anything. With F2 (favouring recall), A scores 0.44 and B scores 0.67; with F0.5, A scores 0.67 and B 0.44. Choosing β is choosing your user.

## Measures for ranked results
Precision and recall treat results as an unordered **set**. Users see a **ranked list**, and a relevant document at position 1 is worth far more than one at position 40. Measures that respect order:

### Precision at k
**P@k** is precision among the top k only. P@10 matches what a user sees on one page. It's easy to explain but ignores order within the top k and ignores how many relevant documents exist.

### Recall at k
**R@k** is the share of all relevant documents found in the top k. It's the standard metric for first-stage retrieval: "does the right answer appear anywhere in the top 1,000 candidates?"

### Mean reciprocal rank
**MRR** looks at the rank of the **first** relevant result: score 1/rank (1 for position 1, 0.5 for position 2, 0.33 for 3), averaged over queries. Good for navigational queries and question answering, where one right answer is all the user needs.

### Mean average precision
**MAP**: for each query, compute precision at each rank where a relevant document appears, average those values (counting relevant documents never retrieved as zero), then average over all queries. It rewards putting relevant results early and finding all of them.

! Example: average precision for one query
! There are 4 relevant documents. The system places relevant ones at ranks 1, 3 and 6, and misses the fourth. Precision at those ranks: 1/1 = 1.0, 2/3 = 0.67, 3/6 = 0.5. Average precision = (1.0 + 0.67 + 0.5 + 0) / 4 = 0.54. Swapping the rank-6 document with the irrelevant one at rank 2 puts relevant documents at ranks 1, 2 and 3: (1.0 + 1.0 + 1.0 + 0) / 4 = 0.75.

### NDCG
**NDCG** (normalised discounted cumulative gain) is the standard metric for graded relevance and the one most ranking teams report.
1. Give each result a **gain** from its grade, for example perfect 3, good 2, fair 1, bad 0.
2. **Discount** each gain by its position: divide by log2(rank + 1). Rank 1 divides by 1, rank 2 by 1.58, rank 4 by 2.32, rank 10 by 3.46.
3. Sum to get **DCG**.
4. Divide by the DCG of the **ideal** ordering (all results sorted best first) to get NDCG between 0 and 1.

%%flow DCG discounts each result's gain by its rank, so a great result at rank 1 counts more than at rank 4.
Rank 1: gain 3 / 1 -> Rank 2: gain 2 / 1.58 -> Rank 3: gain 0 -> Rank 4: gain 3 / 2.32 -> *DCG = 5.55
%%

For that list (grades 3, 2, 0, 3), DCG = 3 + 1.26 + 0 + 1.29 = 5.55. The ideal order is 3, 3, 2, 0: DCG = 3 + 1.89 + 1.0 + 0 = 5.89. NDCG = 5.55 / 5.89 = 0.94. Moving the perfect result from rank 4 up to rank 2 (pushing the others down one place) gives the ideal order and NDCG 1.0.

%%matrix Which ranked metric suits which situation.
Metric | Uses grades | Respects order | Best for
P@k | no | partial, only the cut-off | first page quality
R@k | no | no | candidate retrieval
MRR | no | yes, first hit only | one right answer
MAP | no | yes | binary judgments, full lists
NDCG | yes | yes | graded relevance, ranking teams
%%

## Online metrics
Offline metrics need judgments, which are expensive and can go stale. Online, teams also run A/B tests and interleaving (lesson 09.08) and track behaviour:
- **Click-through rate** on the results page, and **time to first click**.
- **Zero-result rate**: share of searches returning nothing. A direct recall failure.
- **Abandonment** and **reformulation rate**: searches with no click, or immediately followed by a rephrased query. Signs the results missed.
- **Conversion** and **revenue per search** for shopping.
Offline and online metrics should move together. When they don't, the judgments are usually the problem.

## Common mistakes
- Reporting accuracy for retrieval.
- Optimising average precision over all queries when a few high-traffic queries dominate business value; weight by traffic, or report head and tail separately.
- Tuning to the same judgment set you evaluate on, until you overfit it. Hold some out.
- Using P@10 to judge the first stage of a two-stage system, where R@1000 is what matters.

## In an interview
When asked "how do you know search is good?", name precision and recall, explain the trade-off, then choose: "P@10 and NDCG@10 for the shopper-facing ranking, recall@1000 for candidate retrieval, and online zero-result rate, reformulation rate and conversion through A/B tests." Then give one recall-first domain to show you know the cost of a miss differs by user.

> Takeaway: precision is the share of returned results that are relevant; recall is the share of relevant documents that were returned, and they trade against each other. F1 combines them, and ranked measures (P@k, MRR, MAP, NDCG) reward putting relevant results first. Choose the measure that matches the user's cost of missing something versus the cost of reading noise.

## The search index is a copy
A search engine such as Elasticsearch should not be your primary store. The **source of truth** is a database such as Postgres, MySQL or DynamoDB, with transactions, constraints and backups. The search index is a **derived copy** of some of that data, reshaped for search: denormalised (a product document includes its brand name, category path and stock status from other tables), analysed into terms, and missing anything search doesn't need.

Keeping a derived copy in sync is the whole problem of this lesson. Every change in the database (a new product, a price change, a deletion, a stock update) must reach the index, **exactly as it happened, in the right order, eventually, without anything slipping through**. If it doesn't, users see products that no longer exist, miss products that do, or see a price that's wrong. Search drift is a quiet failure: nothing errors, the results are just wrong.

There are three broad ways to sync: write to both from the application (dual writes), poll the database for changes, or stream the database's own change log (change data capture). Only the last is safe and efficient at scale.

## Why dual writes go wrong
The tempting approach is for the application to write to both: update the database, then update the index.

```
db.execute("UPDATE products SET price = 80 WHERE id = 123")
es.update(index="products", id=123, doc={"price": 80})
```

This **dual-write** pattern looks fine in testing, but the two writes are **not atomic**, and nothing coordinates them.

%%seq A dual write that fails halfway: the database has the new price, the index still shows the old one, and nothing will fix it.
App > Database : UPDATE price = 80
Database -> App : committed
App > Search index : update price = 80
Search index -> App : timeout (write lost)
App > App : crash before retry
%%

### The failure cases
- **Partial failure.** One write succeeds and the other fails: a timeout, a crash, a deploy, a full disk on the search cluster. The index is now permanently wrong until something happens to rewrite that document, which may be never. Retrying in-process helps only if the process survives.
- **Race conditions.** Two requests update the same row. They commit to the database in order A then B, but their index writes arrive in order B then A (different threads, different network paths, a GC pause). The database ends with B's value; the index ends with A's stale value. No error is raised anywhere.
- **Every writer must remember.** Any code path, batch job, migration script or admin tool that changes the database without also updating the index silently creates drift. In a large codebase, someone always forgets.
- **No shared transaction.** You can't wrap both writes in one transaction, because the search engine doesn't take part in the database's transactions. Two-phase commit isn't available.
- **Writing the index first** is worse: if the database transaction then rolls back, the index shows a change that never happened.

! Example: the race, with timestamps
! At 10:00:00.000 a merchant sets price 90 (request A). At 10:00:00.010 a bulk discount job sets price 80 (request B). Postgres commits A then B; the row ends at 80. A's index update is delayed 40 ms by a slow connection; B's goes straight through. The index receives 80 at 10:00:00.015, then 90 at 10:00:00.045. The index shows 90; the database says 80. Customers searching "under $85" don't find the product, and it stays that way until the next edit.

## Polling
A step better: write only to the database, and run a job that periodically queries for changes: `SELECT * FROM products WHERE updated_at > :last_seen ORDER BY updated_at`. It's simple, and every writer is covered automatically. Its problems:
- **Deletes are invisible**: a deleted row isn't returned by any query. You need soft deletes (a `deleted_at` column) or a separate tombstone table.
- **Needs an `updated_at` column** that every writer maintains correctly, ideally by trigger.
- **Clock and commit-order issues**: a long transaction that started earlier can commit with an older `updated_at` after the poller has moved past it, so the change is skipped.
- **Latency versus load**: polling every second adds steady query load; polling every minute makes the index a minute stale.

Polling is acceptable for small systems with relaxed freshness. For anything serious, read the log.

## Change data capture
The safer pattern is to write **only** to the database, and let a separate process read the **database's own change log** and apply each change to the index. This is **change data capture** (CDC).

Every serious database already keeps an ordered log of committed changes for crash recovery and replication: the **write-ahead log** (WAL) in Postgres, the **binlog** in MySQL, **change streams** in MongoDB, **DynamoDB Streams**. A CDC connector reads that log like a replica would and turns each committed change into an event. **Debezium** is the common open-source tool, usually running on Kafka Connect and publishing to Kafka topics.

What log-based CDC gives you:
- **All changes are captured, including deletes**, because they're all in the log. No polling queries, no `updated_at` column, no soft deletes.
- **Low delay** (typically milliseconds to a second) and **low overhead** on the database compared with polling queries.
- **Commit order**: events come out in the order transactions committed, so the race above disappears.
- **No change to the data model or to application code**: every writer, including scripts and admin tools, is covered automatically.
- **Rich events**: each event includes the row's **old state** and **new state**, the operation (create, update, delete), and metadata such as the transaction ID and log position.
- **Initial snapshots**: to start, the connector reads a consistent snapshot of existing rows, then switches to streaming from the log position the snapshot corresponds to. Incremental snapshots let you re-snapshot tables without pausing the stream.
- **Filtering and transformation**: include only some tables or columns, mask sensitive fields, reshape events with single message transformations.

%%flow CDC: the application writes once; the database's log becomes the ordered stream that keeps the index in sync.
[Write] App -(single write)-> Database -(WAL or binlog)-> *Debezium
[Sync] Debezium -(change events, keyed by id)-> Kafka -(indexer consumer)-> Search index
%%

Because the change events are published to Kafka **keyed by primary key**, all changes to one row go to one partition and stay in order (lesson 08.02). If the indexer crashes, it restarts from its last committed offset, so nothing is lost. A brand-new index can be built by running a fresh snapshot plus the stream. And other consumers (a cache invalidator, an analytics pipeline) can read the same change stream for free.

%%compare Three ways to keep a search index in sync with the primary database.
[Dual write]
App -> Database
App -> Search index
- Partial failures leave drift
- Races reorder updates
- Every writer must remember
[Polling]
Database -(updated_at query)-> Poller -> Index
+ Single write path
- Misses deletes and late commits
- Load versus staleness trade
[*Log-based CDC]
Database -(WAL)-> Debezium -> Kafka -> Indexer
+ All changes incl. deletes, in order
+ Replayable, sub-second lag
- More infrastructure to run
%%

### The outbox variant
CDC on raw tables makes your table schema the event schema. If the search document needs data from several tables, or you want deliberate, stable event shapes, use the **transactional outbox** (lesson 08.03): write a `ProductChanged` row to an outbox table in the same transaction, and stream only the outbox with CDC.

## What you still have to handle
### Lag and read-your-writes
The index is **eventually consistent**: behind the database by the CDC delay plus the indexer's processing plus the search engine's refresh interval (1 second by default in Elasticsearch). Typically one to a few seconds. A merchant who saves a product and immediately searches for it may not see it. Common fixes:
- On the item's own page, read from the database, not the index.
- After a user's edit, show their change directly in the UI (optimistic display) or overlay their recent edits on search results.
- For the rare request that truly needs it, ask the engine to refresh (`refresh=wait_for`) and accept the latency cost.

### Idempotent, versioned writes
Replays and redeliveries can apply an event twice, and after a re-snapshot old events can arrive after new ones. The indexer should **upsert by document ID** and use **external versioning**: pass the log position (Postgres LSN) or a row version as the document's version, and have the engine reject writes with a lower version than the stored one. Elasticsearch supports this with `version_type=external`. Then applying an event twice, or an old event late, is harmless.

### Denormalisation
A product search document joins several tables: product, brand, category, inventory, reviews summary. A change to the brand's name must update every product document of that brand. The indexer must react to changes in **any** source table, look up the affected documents, and rebuild them. Keep the fan-out bounded: a brand rename touching 2 million products is a bulk reindex job, not a per-event action.

### Reindexing
When the mapping changes (a new analyser, a new field), you can't change existing documents in place. Build a **new index** (`products_v8`) from a snapshot while the stream continues, let it catch up, then atomically switch an **alias** (`products`) from v7 to v8. Queries always use the alias, so the switch is invisible to users.

%%timeline A zero-downtime reindex: build the new index beside the old one, catch it up from the stream, then flip the alias.
[v7] 09:00 | Alias products serves v7, CDC keeps it current
[v8] 09:00 | Create products_v8 with new mapping
[v8] 09:05 | Snapshot copy starts at log position 88,100
[v8] 11:40 | Snapshot done; replay stream from 88,100
[v8] 11:52 | *Caught up: lag under 1 s
[Alias] 11:53 | Flip alias products to v8
[v7] 12:30 | Delete v7 after checks
%%

! Example: a price change, end to end
! A merchant sets a shoe's price to 80. The app commits one `UPDATE` to Postgres. Debezium reads the WAL entry and publishes `{op: "u", before: {price: 95}, after: {price: 80}, source: {lsn: 4411}}` to the Kafka topic `db.products`, keyed by product 123. The indexer consumes it, rebuilds document 123 and writes it with `version = 4411, version_type = external`. About 1.2 seconds after the commit, the next refresh makes it searchable.
! The indexer crashes after writing but before committing its Kafka offset. On restart it re-reads the event and writes version 4411 again; with external versioning a write must carry a strictly higher version, so Elasticsearch answers with a version conflict, which the indexer treats as "already applied" and moves on. Later, a re-snapshot replays an older event with LSN 4300 for the same product; Elasticsearch rejects it as an older version. The index ends correct.

## Monitoring
- **End-to-end lag**: time from database commit to searchable. Alert if it exceeds, say, 30 seconds.
- **Connector health and replication slot size**: in Postgres, a stuck CDC connector keeps its replication slot open, and the database must retain WAL until it catches up, which can fill the disk. Alert on retained WAL size.
- **Periodic reconciliation**: sample documents and compare index against database, or compare counts per category, to catch drift from bugs.

## Common mistakes
- Dual writes "with retries" in application code.
- Polling without handling deletes.
- Indexer writes without versioning, so replays or re-snapshots overwrite newer data.
- Forgetting the Postgres replication slot, which can fill the primary's disk when the connector stops.
- Changing mappings in place instead of reindexing behind an alias.

## In an interview
Say: "The database is the source of truth. I won't dual-write: it's not atomic and updates can arrive out of order. I'll use log-based CDC (Debezium on the WAL) into Kafka keyed by ID, and an indexer that upserts with external versions so replays are harmless. The index lags by a second or two, so item pages read from the database. Mapping changes go through a new index and an alias flip."

> Takeaway: dual writes to a database and a search index drift apart on partial failures and races, and polling misses deletes. Write only to the database and let log-based change data capture stream ordered changes, including deletes, to an idempotent, versioned indexer, and reindex behind an alias.

The safer pattern is log-based change data capture: the app writes only to the database, a tool such as Debezium reads the write-ahead log or binlog and publishes ordered change events (including deletes) to Kafka keyed by ID, and an idempotent indexer upserts each document using the log position as an external version, resuming from its offset after failures. ## Why split an index at all
A single machine can hold a surprisingly large inverted index: hundreds of gigabytes, tens or hundreds of millions of documents. But eventually one of three limits is hit:
- **Size**: the index no longer fits on one machine's disks, or the hot part no longer fits in its memory (page cache), so queries slow down sharply.
- **Query throughput**: one machine can't serve the required queries per second.
- **Indexing throughput and recovery**: rebuilding or copying one giant index takes too long when a machine fails.

The answer is to split the index across machines, called **partitioning** or **sharding**. Each piece is a **shard**. For throughput, each shard is also copied to several machines as **replicas**. There are two fundamentally different ways to split an inverted index, and the choice shapes every query.

## Two ways to split
Recall that an inverted index is a dictionary of terms, each with a postings list of documents (lesson 09.02). You can cut that structure along either axis.

**Term partitioning** (a **global** index): each machine holds the **complete postings lists for a subset of terms**. Machine 1 has every document's postings for terms in some range or hash bucket (say, "a" to "f"), machine 2 the next range, and so on. A term lives on exactly one machine.

**Document partitioning** (a **local** index): each machine holds a **complete index**, with every term, but only **for a subset of the documents**. Shard 1 indexes documents 1 to 10 million, shard 2 the next 10 million, or documents are assigned by hashing their ID.

%%tree The same index cut two ways. Term partitioning splits the dictionary; document partitioning splits the collection.
Inverted index for 30M docs
  By term (global)
    Node 1 | terms a-f, all 30M docs
    Node 2 | terms g-r, all 30M docs
    Node 3 | terms s-z, all 30M docs
  *By document (local)
    Shard 1 | all terms, docs 1-10M
    Shard 2 | all terms, docs 10-20M
    Shard 3 | all terms, docs 20-30M
%%

### How a query runs in each
- **Term-partitioned**: the query "cat sat" goes only to the machine owning "cat" and the machine owning "sat". But to intersect or score, the two postings lists must meet in one place: one machine ships its whole "cat" list (possibly millions of entries) to the other, or both ship to a coordinator.
- **Document-partitioned**: the query goes to **every** shard (**scatter**). Each shard intersects and scores locally, using only its own documents, and returns its local top K. A coordinator merges them into a global top K (**gather**). Only small result lists cross the network.

%%flow The two query paths: by term, postings lists travel to meet; by document, every shard works locally and only top K lists travel.
[By term] Query "cat sat" -(cat)-> Node 1 -(ship full cat list)-> *Node 3 intersects with sat
[By document] Query "cat sat" -(scatter to all)-> Shards 1, 2, 3 -(local top K)-> *Coordinator merges
%%

## Comparing them
| Property | Term-partitioned | Document-partitioned |
| Machines per query | Only those holding the query's terms | All shards |
| Multi-term queries | Long postings lists shipped between machines | Each shard intersects locally |
| Network per query | Large (postings lists) | Small (top K IDs and scores) |
| Load balance | Hot terms make hot machines | Even, if documents spread evenly |
| Adding a document | Touches many machines (one per term) | Touches one shard |
| Losing a machine | Whole terms become unsearchable | Some documents missing from results |
| Scoring statistics | Global df for free | Each shard sees local df only |

## Why document partitioning wins
Document partitioning is much more common: Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Solr and web search engines all use it. The reasons follow from the table:
- **Each shard answers queries on its own.** All the expensive work (intersection, scoring, top-K selection) happens locally, close to the data. Only tiny result lists travel.
- **Writes touch one place.** A new document with 500 distinct terms goes to one shard. Under term partitioning, it would update 500 postings lists spread across most of the cluster.
- **Load spreads evenly.** With documents assigned by hash, each shard gets a similar slice of every term. Under term partitioning, the machine holding "iphone" during a launch week, or "the" always, becomes a hotspot.
- **Failures degrade gracefully.** Losing a document-partitioned shard (with no replica) loses some documents from results; users may not notice. Losing a term partition makes every query containing those terms fail.
- **Operations are simpler.** Shards are self-contained units that can be moved, replicated, snapshotted and recovered independently.

Term partitioning's apparent advantage, fewer machines per query, is undone by the cost of moving long postings lists across the network and by hot spots. It has niche uses (some systems with very high query rates and short queries, or term-sharded auxiliary structures), but it's rarely the default.

### The costs of document partitioning
- **Every query hits every shard.** With 100 shards, each query costs 100 shard-level searches. Query latency is set by the **slowest** shard (tail latency, lesson 02.12), so one slow node slows every query. Mitigations: replicas and adaptive replica selection (send to the fastest copy), hedged requests, and routing (below).
- **Statistics are local.** Each shard computes idf (lesson 09.04) from its own documents. A term rare in one shard but common overall gets over-weighted there. When documents are spread randomly and shards are large, local statistics are close to global and it doesn't matter. When it does matter (small shards, skewed data), engines can gather global statistics first: Elasticsearch's `dfs_query_then_fetch` adds a round trip to collect term frequencies from all shards before scoring. Large systems also compute collection statistics in background jobs and distribute them to shards periodically.
- **Deep pagination is expensive.** Asking for results 1,000 to 1,010 makes every shard return its top 1,010 (lesson 09.01).

### Routing: not every query needs every shard
If queries are always scoped to one tenant or user (a mailbox search, a SaaS customer's documents), route documents by that key: all of tenant 42's documents go to the shard `hash(42) % shards`. Then a tenant's query hits **one** shard instead of all. Elasticsearch supports this with a `routing` value. The risk is uneven shards when one tenant is huge; give large tenants their own index or spread them over a few shards.

## Sizing shards in Elasticsearch
Practical rules for the document-partitioned design in Elasticsearch:
- Aim for shards between **10 GB and 50 GB**, and fewer than about **200 million documents** per shard. Very large shards are slow to recover and rebalance (moving a 200 GB shard to a new node takes a long time); very small shards waste overhead.
- **Each shard is traditionally searched by one thread per search request** (recent versions can also search a shard's segments in parallel), so spreading data over more shards adds parallelism, but each shard also adds fixed overhead: memory for segment metadata, file handles, and coordination work on every query. Thousands of tiny shards make a cluster slow and unstable.
- Keep the shard count per node bounded. Elasticsearch's default limit is **1,000 shards per non-frozen data node**, and real clusters usually stay well below it.
- Shard count is **fixed at index creation**. To change it, use the split or shrink APIs (which multiply or divide by a factor) or reindex into a new index behind an alias (lesson 09.10). So size with growth in mind.
- **Replicas** add read throughput and fault tolerance, not write capacity: every write goes to the primary and all its replicas.

%%bars Rough cost and benefit of shard sizes. Mid-sized shards balance per-shard overhead against slow recovery.
1 GB shards | 300 | 300 shards for 300 GB: heavy overhead
*30 GB shards | 10 | 10 shards: balanced
300 GB shard | 1 | 1 shard: hours to recover
%%

### Time-based data
For logs, metrics and events, data arrives in time order, queries mostly target recent data, and old data is deleted. Don't use one giant index. Use **rollover** with index lifecycle management: write to the newest index (`logs-000042`), and when its primary shard reaches 50 GB or the index is a day old, create `logs-000043` and switch writes there. Old indices can move to cheaper hardware (hot, warm, cold tiers) and are deleted **whole** when they expire. Deleting individual documents is expensive in Lucene, because deleted documents are only cleaned up when segments merge (lesson 09.02); dropping a whole index is instant.

%%flow Time-based indices with rollover: write to the newest index, and drop whole old indices instead of deleting documents.
Writes -> *logs-000042 -(rollover at 50 GB primary shard)-> logs-000043 -(after 30 days)-> Delete old index
%%

! Example: sizing a product search index
! 300 million products at about 2 KB each of indexed data is 600 GB. At about 30 GB per shard, that's 20 primary shards; with one replica each, 40 shards. Spread across 8 data nodes, that's 5 shards per node, about 75 GB of index per node, much of which can sit in the page cache on 128 GB machines.
! A query fans out to 20 shards (one copy of each) in parallel. Each takes about 15 ms to return its local top 10. The coordinator merges 200 hits into the final 10 and fetches their documents. Total about 30 ms at the median; the p99 depends on the slowest of 20 shard calls, so replica selection and hedging matter. If query load doubles, add replicas (and nodes), not primaries. If the catalogue grows to 900 million products, shards would grow to about 90 GB, so the team plans a reindex into 60 primaries before then.

! Example: why term partitioning hurts here
! The same catalogue term-partitioned over 8 nodes. The query "black running shoes" touches 3 terms on up to 3 nodes. "black" matches 30 million products: its postings list, even compressed at about 1 byte per posting, is about 30 MB. To intersect it with "shoes" (20 million) on another node, one of those lists must cross the network: tens of megabytes per query. At 2,000 queries a second, that's tens of gigabytes a second of internal traffic. Document partitioning ships only 20 small top-10 lists per query.

## Common mistakes
- Thousands of tiny shards ("one index per customer per day"), which overwhelm the cluster's metadata and memory.
- One huge shard that takes hours to recover after a node failure.
- Ignoring routing when every query is tenant-scoped, so each query pays the cost of every shard.
- Adding primaries to increase query throughput, when replicas are what scale reads.
- Forgetting that local idf can skew scores on small or skewed shards.

## In an interview
Say "document-partitioned, like Elasticsearch: each shard is a full index of a subset of documents, queries scatter to all shards and gather local top K." Then mention the costs (every query hits every shard, tail latency, local idf) and the fixes (replicas, routing by tenant, global statistics). Size shards around 10 to 50 GB with arithmetic.

> Takeaway: indexes can be split by term or by document. Document partitioning is far more common because each shard intersects and scores locally, writes touch one shard, load is even and failures degrade gracefully; its costs are a scatter-gather to every shard, tail latency from the slowest shard, and the need for global statistics. ## What a crawler does
A **web crawler** (or spider) is the acquisition stage of web search (lesson 09.01). It starts from a set of **seed URLs**, downloads each page, extracts its links, and adds new links to a queue, repeating until it has covered as much of the web as it should. Then it keeps going, revisiting pages to catch changes. The loop is simple to describe and very hard to run at scale, because the web is enormous, constantly changing, full of traps, and made of servers that belong to other people.

The requirements split into must and should:
- **Must be robust.** Survive **spider traps**: sites that generate infinite URLs (a calendar with a "next month" link forever, session IDs in every URL, endlessly nested paths), malformed HTML, huge files, servers that never finish responding.
- **Must be polite.** Respect each site's rules (robots.txt) and never overload a server. A crawler that sends hundreds of requests a second to a small site is indistinguishable from a denial-of-service attack, and will be blocked.
- **Should be distributed and scalable**, so adding machines adds throughput.
- **Should be efficient**, using bandwidth and CPU well.
- **Should prioritise quality**, fetching important pages first.
- **Should keep pages fresh**, revisiting pages in proportion to how often they change and how much they matter.
- **Should be extensible** to new formats and protocols.

### The scale
To see why each module exists, size it. Suppose the goal is 1 billion pages a week, average 100 KB each.
- 1,000,000,000 / 604,800 seconds ≈ **1,650 pages a second**.
- Bandwidth: 1,650 × 100 KB ≈ 165 MB/s, about **1.3 Gbit/s**.
- Storage: 100 TB of raw pages a week, before compression (HTML compresses about 5 to 10 times).
- If each fetch takes about 1 second end to end (DNS, connect, download), you need about **1,650 fetches in flight** at once, which means thousands of concurrent connections, so asynchronous I/O across several machines.
- And politeness: if you can hit each host at most once every few seconds, you need **tens of thousands of distinct hosts** in rotation at any moment.

## The modules
A standard architecture, based on the Mercator crawler design, has these parts.

%%flow The crawl loop: take a URL from the frontier, fetch, parse, drop duplicates, and feed new links back.
[Fetch] *URL frontier -(next URL)-> DNS resolve -> Fetch page -> Parse
[Store] Parse -(content seen?)-> Store for indexing
[Links] Parse -(links)-> Filter and robots.txt -> Normalise -(seen URL?)-> URL frontier
%%

1. **URL frontier**: the queue of URLs to fetch next. It decides both what is important and when each host may be contacted (detailed below).
2. **DNS resolution**: turning hostnames into IP addresses. DNS is a classic bottleneck: lookups can take hundreds of milliseconds or seconds, and standard library resolvers block the thread. Crawlers run their own asynchronous resolvers and cache results aggressively (respecting TTLs), since many URLs share a host.
3. **Fetch**: download the page over HTTP, with timeouts, a maximum size, and conditional requests (`If-Modified-Since`, `ETag`) on revisits so unchanged pages cost almost nothing.
4. **Parse**: extract text, metadata and links from HTML (and PDFs, other formats). Some pages need JavaScript rendering, which is far more expensive, so it's reserved for important sites.
5. **Content seen?**: skip pages whose content was already seen at another URL. Mirrors, copies and parameter variations make a large share of the web duplicate. Exact duplicates are found with a **fingerprint** (a 64-bit hash of the content). **Near-duplicates** (same article with different ads or dates) are found with **shingles**: the set of overlapping k-word sequences in the page, compared by Jaccard similarity, made affordable with MinHash or SimHash sketches.
6. **URL filter**: drop URLs outside the crawl's scope (by domain, file type, or a blocklist of known traps).
7. **robots.txt**: a site's rules on which paths crawlers may fetch, and sometimes a `Crawl-delay`. Fetch it once per host, **cache** it (typically for a day), and **check it just before fetching** each URL, because rules change and a URL may wait in the frontier for days.
8. **Normalisation**: turn relative links into absolute URLs and **canonicalise** them: lower-case the host, remove default ports and fragments (`#section`), sort or strip tracking parameters, resolve `../`. Respect `rel="canonical"` hints. Without this, the same page enters the frontier under dozens of URLs.
9. **Duplicate URL elimination**: don't enqueue a URL already in the frontier or already crawled. With billions of URLs, the "seen" set is too big for memory: store fingerprints of URLs on disk, sorted, keep a cache of recent ones, and batch lookups. A Bloom filter in memory can cheaply say "definitely not seen" for most new URLs.
10. **Checkpointing**: periodically save the frontier and state so a crash doesn't restart the crawl from the seeds.

%%state A URL's life in the crawler. Most new links are dropped as already seen or out of scope; fetched pages are scheduled for revisits.
[*] -> Discovered
Discovered -> Dropped : filtered or already seen
Discovered -> Queued : enters frontier
Queued -> Fetching : host's turn arrives
Fetching -> Fetched : 200 OK, new content
Fetching -> Queued : timeout, retry later
Fetching -> Dropped : robots.txt disallows
Fetched -> *Queued : revisit scheduled
%%

## The URL frontier: priority and politeness
The question that matters most for the web's goodwill is how a crawler avoids hammering a single site. A naive frontier is one FIFO queue. But links are highly local: a page on example.com links mostly to other pages on example.com. A FIFO queue fills with runs of URLs from the same host, and many fetcher threads pull them at once, sending a burst of parallel requests to one server.

The Mercator frontier solves this with **two sets of queues** that separate two concerns: **what is important** and **when a host may be contacted**.

%%flow The Mercator frontier: front queues decide what is important; back queues, one host each, decide when a host may be contacted again.
[Priority] New URL -> Prioritiser -(by importance)-> Front queues 1..F
[Politeness] Front queues 1..F -(biased pick)-> Back queue router -(one host per queue)-> *Back queues 1..B
[Fetch] Back queues 1..B -(heap of next allowed times)-> Fetcher thread
%%

### Front queues: priority
There are F front queues, numbered by priority. A **prioritiser** gives each URL a priority, based on page quality (link-based importance such as PageRank, the site's reputation) and how often it changes (news home pages change every few minutes; an archived paper never changes). It places the URL in the matching front queue. When the back side needs more URLs, it picks a front queue with a **bias towards higher priorities** (high queues are chosen more often, but low ones aren't starved) and takes from it.

### Back queues: politeness
There are B back queues, with two invariants:
- Each back queue holds URLs from **exactly one host**.
- Each host maps to **only one** back queue at a time (a table records which).

So two threads can never fetch from the same host at once, because a host's URLs are all in one queue and only one thread takes from a queue at a time.

### The heap of next-allowed times
A **min-heap** holds one entry per back queue: the earliest time that queue's host may be contacted again. A fetcher thread:
1. Pops the top of the heap: the back queue whose host is allowed soonest.
2. Waits until that time if it hasn't arrived yet.
3. Takes **one** URL from that queue and fetches it.
4. Computes the host's next allowed time and pushes the queue back into the heap.
5. If the queue is now empty, it refills it from the front queues: it takes URLs until it finds one for a host that has no back queue, and assigns that host to this queue (URLs for hosts that already have a queue are routed there).

### The gap
How long should a host wait between requests? A fixed delay (1 second for everyone) treats a tiny shared server the same as a huge CDN. The Mercator rule sets the gap **relative to how long the last fetch took**: about **10 times** the last fetch duration. A fast server that responded in 0.1 s can be hit every second; a slow server that took 3 s (perhaps because it's struggling) waits 30 s. The crawler automatically backs off from servers under stress. A `Crawl-delay` in robots.txt, or HTTP 429 and 503 responses with `Retry-After`, override it with a longer wait.

The Mercator recommendation is about **three times as many back queues as fetcher threads**, so that whenever a thread is free, some host is allowed to be contacted and the thread doesn't sit idle.

%%timeline Politeness in action for two hosts: the fast host is fetched every few seconds, the slow one waits ten times its last fetch time.
[Host A] 0 s | fetch, took 0.2 s; next allowed at 2.2 s
[Host B] 0 s | fetch, took 3 s; next allowed at 33 s
[Host A] 2.2 s | fetch, took 0.2 s; next at 4.4 s
[Host A] 4.4 s | fetch, took 0.3 s; next at 7.7 s
[Host B] 33 s | *fetch again, took 1 s; next at 44 s
%%

! Example: two hosts, one slow
! Host A's last page took 0.2 s, so its next allowed time is now + 2 s. Host B's took 3 s, so B waits 30 s. A fetcher pops the heap, gets A's queue (earliest time), fetches one page, and reinserts A at now + 2 s. B's 50 queued URLs sit untouched until its 30 seconds are up. Meanwhile, other threads are busy with thousands of other hosts. A struggling site is never hit by two threads in parallel and sees at most one request every 30 seconds while it's slow; when it speeds up to 1 s responses, its gap shrinks to 10 s.

! Example: how many back queues
! A crawler runs 600 fetcher threads per machine. With B = 3 × 600 = 1,800 back queues, there are 1,800 distinct hosts in rotation per machine. If the average gap is 5 seconds, each host allows 0.2 fetches a second, so 1,800 hosts allow 360 fetches a second, enough to keep a machine at its target of about 300 pages a second. With only 600 back queues, the same machine could do at most 120 a second politely, and most threads would wait on the heap.

## Freshness: when to revisit
After the first crawl, most of the work is **recrawling**. Pages change at very different rates. Revisit too rarely and the index is stale; revisit too often and you waste bandwidth and the site's resources. Crawlers estimate each page's change rate from history (how often content changed at past visits) and schedule revisits accordingly, weighted by importance. Conditional requests (304 Not Modified) make unchanged revisits cheap. Sitemaps with `lastmod` dates and push protocols where sites notify search engines of changes help too.

## Scaling out
Distribute the crawler by **host**: each crawler node owns a set of hosts, for example by hashing the hostname (consistent hashing so nodes can be added). Politeness for a host is then enforced entirely on one node, with no coordination. When a node extracts a link to a host it doesn't own, it sends the URL to the owning node, batched to reduce messages. Content fingerprints and seen-URL sets are partitioned the same way, and seen-URL checks are batched because the set is too large for memory. Hosting providers with many sites on one IP address need care: politeness may need to apply per IP as well as per host name.

## Common mistakes
- A single FIFO frontier, which bursts requests at one host.
- Fixed per-host delays that ignore server speed and `Retry-After`.
- Checking robots.txt only when a URL is discovered rather than just before fetching.
- No URL canonicalisation or trap detection, so the frontier fills with infinite calendar pages.
- Synchronous DNS lookups that stall every thread.

## In an interview
Draw the loop (frontier, DNS, fetch, parse, content dedupe, filter, robots, normalise, URL dedupe, back to frontier), then focus on the frontier: front queues for priority, back queues with one host each for politeness, a heap of next-allowed times, a gap of about ten times the last fetch, and partition by host across machines. Do the throughput arithmetic to show why you need thousands of hosts in rotation.

> Takeaway: a crawler loops frontier, DNS, fetch, parse, dedupe, filter and normalise, then back to the frontier. Politeness comes from the Mercator frontier: back queues hold one host each, a heap enforces each host's earliest next contact time, and the gap scales with the host's response time (about ten times the last fetch), with robots.txt checked just before fetching.

?? It never lets two fetches to the same host happen at once and spaces requests out per host. In the Mercator frontier, each back queue holds URLs for one host only (and each host has only one back queue), and a min-heap records the earliest time each host may be contacted again; a thread takes one URL from the queue whose time has come, fetches it, and the host must then wait a gap of roughly ten times that fetch's duration, so slow or struggling servers are automatically hit less often. ## Why vectors
Keyword search matches words. A query for "cheap flights to the coast" won't find a page that only says "budget airfare to the seaside", because no analysed terms overlap. Synonym lists (lesson 09.07) patch some of this by hand, but language has endless ways to say the same thing, and users increasingly type whole questions ("what shoes are good for flat feet") rather than keywords. This is the **vocabulary mismatch** problem.

**Vector search** (also called semantic or dense retrieval) addresses it by representing both queries and documents as **embeddings**: arrays of numbers produced by a trained model so that texts with similar meanings land close together in a high-dimensional space. "Budget airfare" and "cheap flights" end up near each other even though they share no letters. Typical embeddings have 128 to 1,536 dimensions; some models produce 3,072. Search becomes: **find the stored vectors nearest to the query vector**.

### Where embeddings come from
An embedding model (usually a transformer encoder) is trained on pairs of texts that should match (a question and its answer, a query and the product bought) and pairs that shouldn't, adjusting its weights so matching pairs get nearby vectors. At indexing time, you run every document (or every chunk of a long document) through the model and store the vector. At query time, you embed the query with the same model, which takes a few milliseconds on a GPU or tens of milliseconds on a CPU. Changing the model means re-embedding the whole collection, because vectors from different models aren't comparable.

### Distance measures
- **Euclidean (L2)** distance: straight-line distance; considers direction and magnitude.
- **Cosine similarity**: the cosine of the angle between vectors, ignoring length; 1 means same direction, 0 unrelated. The usual choice for text embeddings.
- **Dot product**: cosine without normalising; slightly cheaper. If vectors are normalised to length 1, dot product equals cosine, so many systems normalise once at indexing time and use dot product.
- **Hamming** distance: the count of differing bits between binary vectors; extremely fast, used with binary-quantised embeddings.

## Approximate nearest neighbours
Comparing the query with every stored vector (**exact** or brute-force search) is simple and perfectly accurate, but slow at scale.

! Example: the cost of brute force
! 100 million documents with 768-dimension float32 embeddings take 100,000,000 × 768 × 4 bytes ≈ 307 GB. A brute-force query computes 100 million dot products of 768 multiply-adds each, about 77 billion operations, and must stream all 307 GB out of RAM. Even at 50 GB/s memory bandwidth, that's about 6 seconds per query on one machine. At 1,000 queries a second, impossible. Under about 100,000 vectors, though, brute force takes a few milliseconds and is a perfectly good choice.

So systems use **approximate nearest neighbour** (ANN) indexes that examine a small fraction of the vectors and *usually* find the true nearest ones. How often they do is measured as **recall@k**: the share of the true top k that the index returned. A recall of 95% means on average 9.5 of the true 10 nearest are found. Note this is recall of the *index* compared with exact search, not relevance recall (lesson 09.09).

| Index | Idea | Trade-off |
| HNSW | Multi-layer graph; sparse top layers act as express lanes | 95%+ recall at low latency; memory heavy (vectors plus links); slow to build; inserts costly |
| IVF | k-means clusters; search only the nprobe nearest clusters | Faster to build, lighter, handles inserts better; lower recall at the same latency |
| LSH | Hash functions that make similar vectors collide | Simple, fast to build, good for streams; usually lower recall than HNSW |
| Annoy | Forest of random-projection trees, memory-mapped | Loads instantly, shareable between processes; immutable, must rebuild to change |

**Quantisation** is often combined with these to save memory: **product quantisation** (PQ) splits each vector into sub-vectors and replaces each with the ID of its nearest centroid, compressing a 3 KB vector to perhaps 64 bytes. **Scalar quantisation** stores each dimension as int8 instead of float32 (4 times smaller). Search runs on compressed vectors, then the top candidates are **re-scored** with full-precision vectors to recover accuracy.

## How HNSW works
**HNSW** (Hierarchical Navigable Small World) is the most widely used ANN index: it's in Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, pgvector, Lucene, Weaviate, Qdrant and many others. It combines two ideas.
- A **skip list** is a sorted linked list with extra, sparser layers on top. A search starts in the sparse top layer, takes long jumps, then drops down a layer for shorter jumps, finishing in the full bottom list. Search takes O(log n) steps.
- A **navigable small world** (NSW) graph links each vector to some of its near neighbours plus a few longer links. **Greedy routing** (always step to the neighbour closest to the target) reaches the target region in few hops, like the "six degrees of separation" effect.

HNSW stacks NSW graphs into layers like a skip list.

%%tree HNSW layers for 1 million vectors with M = 16: each layer up holds about 1/16 of the one below, so the top layers are tiny and every vector is in layer 0. Search enters at the top and descends.
Layer 4 | ~15 nodes, longest jumps
  Layer 3 | ~240 nodes
    Layer 2 | ~3,900 nodes
      Layer 1 | ~62,000 nodes, medium jumps
        *Layer 0 | all 1M vectors, short local links
%%

### Building
- Each new vector is assigned a random **top layer**. The probability of reaching higher layers falls off exponentially (controlled by a level multiplier mL = 1/ln(M)), so most vectors live only in layer 0, and very few reach the top.
- To insert, search from the top down to find the vector's nearest existing neighbours at each of its layers, and link it to the best **M** of them (M is the number of neighbours per node, typically 16 to 64; layer 0 often allows 2M).
- **efConstruction** controls how many candidates are explored when finding those neighbours: higher means a better graph and a slower build.

### Searching
1. Start at the entry point in the top layer.
2. Greedily hop to whichever neighbour is closest to the query until no neighbour is closer.
3. Drop to the next layer, starting from that node, and repeat.
4. In layer 0, run a wider search that keeps a candidate list of size **efSearch**, and return the best k.

%%flow HNSW search: start at the sparse top layer, greedily move toward the query, then drop a layer and repeat.
Entry point, top layer -(greedy hops)-> Closest in top layer -(descend)-> Layer below -(greedy hops)-> Layer 0 -(efSearch candidates)-> *Nearest neighbours
%%

### The knobs and their costs
- **M**: more links means higher recall and more memory. On a standard 1-million-vector benchmark (SIFT1M, 128 dimensions), index memory rises from about 0.5 GB at M = 2 to about 5 GB at M = 512.
- **efSearch**: more candidates means higher recall and higher latency. Typical curves run from around 80% recall at about 1 ms per query to close to 100% at tens of milliseconds.
- Updates are a delete plus an insert, and deletes are soft (marked, skipped during search) until a rebuild or merge. Building HNSW over hundreds of millions of vectors can take hours. Teams often keep a small **hot index** for recent inserts beside a large **cold index**, search both, and rebuild the cold one in the background and swap.
- The whole graph needs to be in memory for good latency. That's the main cost driver.

%%bars Typical recall of an HNSW index against exact search as efSearch grows. Each step up costs latency.
efSearch 16 | 82 | ~82% recall, ~1 ms
efSearch 64 | 94 | ~94% recall, ~2 ms
efSearch 256 | 98.5 | ~98.5% recall, ~6 ms
*efSearch 1024 | 99.7 | ~99.7% recall, ~20 ms
%%

## Filtering
Real queries add structured filters: "trail shoes under $100 in stock, size 9". The options:
- **Post-filter**: get the top N vectors (N much bigger than K), then drop those failing the filter. Risk: a strict filter (only 1% of products match) leaves fewer than K results, or none.
- **Pre-filter**: apply the filter first, then search only the matching vectors. With brute force over the filtered set, this is exact and fine when the filtered set is small. But an ANN graph built over all vectors can't be used efficiently on an arbitrary subset.
- **Filter during traversal**: walk the HNSW graph as usual but only accept results that pass the filter, still using non-matching nodes as stepping stones. Elasticsearch's kNN `filter`, and most dedicated vector databases, do this. When the filter is very selective, engines automatically switch to brute force over the filtered set.

## Hybrid retrieval and reciprocal rank fusion
Neither method wins everywhere.
- **BM25 is precise for exact terms**: product codes ("WH-1000XM5"), names, rare jargon, error messages ("ERR_SSL_PROTOCOL_ERROR"), quoted phrases. Embeddings tend to blur these: the vector for one model number lands near other model numbers, so the exact match may not rank first.
- **Vector search handles meaning**: paraphrases, synonyms, natural-language questions, cross-lingual matches, where keyword search finds nothing.

Evaluations across many datasets consistently find that combining them beats either alone. **Hybrid search** runs both retrievals in parallel and merges the results.

%%compare Keyword retrieval versus vector retrieval. Each catches what the other misses, which is why hybrid search combines them.
[BM25 keyword]
Query -(analysed terms)-> Inverted index -> Exact matches
+ Exact codes, names, rare terms
+ Cheap, explainable, no model
- Misses paraphrases and synonyms
[Vector kNN]
Query -(embedding model)-> HNSW index -> Similar meaning
+ Paraphrases, questions, synonyms
- Blurs exact identifiers
- Memory heavy, needs a model
[*Hybrid with RRF]
Both in parallel -(fuse by rank)-> One list
+ Best of both
- Two retrievals per query
%%

### Why not just add the scores
BM25 scores are unbounded and depend on the query (a long query gives larger scores), while cosine similarity lies between −1 and 1 and clusters in a narrow band (often 0.7 to 0.9). Adding them, or even a weighted sum, needs normalisation that is fragile and query-dependent. **Reciprocal rank fusion** (RRF) sidesteps this by using only **ranks**:

`RRF score(d) = Σ over result lists of 1 / (k + rank of d in that list)`

A document missing from a list contributes nothing from it. Elasticsearch's default `rank_constant` k is **60**, and `rank_window_size` (how many results from each list are considered) defaults to the requested size. The constant k dampens the advantage of the very top ranks, so being 1st versus 2nd matters little, but appearing in both lists matters a lot. RRF needs no training and no score normalisation, which is why it's the default.

%%flow Hybrid search with RRF: run keyword and vector retrieval in parallel, then merge by rank alone.
[Keyword] Query -(BM25)-> Ranked list A -(1 / (60 + rank))-> *RRF sum per document
[Vector] Query -(embed, kNN)-> Ranked list B -(1 / (60 + rank))-> *RRF sum per document
[Merge] *RRF sum per document -(sort by sum)-> Final ranking
%%

! Example: fusing two lists with k = 60
! Doc A is 1st in BM25 and 5th in kNN: 1/61 + 1/65 = 0.0164 + 0.0154 = 0.0318. Doc B is 2nd in BM25 and absent from kNN: 1/62 = 0.0161. Doc C is 1st in kNN only: 1/61 = 0.0164. Doc D is 10th in both: 2 × 1/70 = 0.0286.
! Order: A (0.0318), D (0.0286), C (0.0164), B (0.0161). A document both methods like beats one that only one method ranks first, and even D, which neither ranks highly, beats the single-list winners. If you need more control, use weighted RRF or a learned re-ranker (lesson 09.08) over the fused list.

After fusion, many systems apply a **cross-encoder** re-ranker to the top 50 to 100: a model that reads the query and each document together and scores relevance directly. It's far more accurate than comparing separate embeddings but too slow to run on more than a short list.

## Common mistakes
- Replacing BM25 entirely with vectors, then finding exact product codes and names no longer work.
- Embedding long documents as one vector; the meaning blurs. Split into passages (chunks) of a few hundred words.
- Forgetting the memory bill: HNSW wants vectors and graph in RAM.
- Post-filtering with a strict filter and returning empty pages.
- Adding BM25 and cosine scores directly.
- Changing the embedding model without re-embedding everything.

## In an interview
Say: "BM25 for exact terms, an HNSW kNN index on embeddings for meaning, run both in parallel, fuse with RRF, and optionally re-rank the top 50 with a cross-encoder." Mention ANN recall versus latency (efSearch), memory sizing, filtering during traversal, and that below about 100,000 vectors brute force is fine.

> Takeaway: vector search finds meaning through nearest neighbours in embedding space, using ANN indexes such as HNSW (high recall, memory-hungry) or IVF (lighter, lower recall). BM25 catches exact terms that embeddings blur. Hybrid search runs both and merges with reciprocal rank fusion, which uses ranks (1/(60 + rank)) so incompatible scores don't need normalising.

BM25 is precise for exact tokens such as product codes, names, error messages and rare terms, but misses documents that express the same idea in different words. Vector search over embeddings catches synonyms, paraphrases and natural-language questions, but blurs exact identifiers and can rank near-misses highly. Running both in parallel and merging with reciprocal rank fusion, which only uses each document's rank in each list (summing 1/(60 + rank)), rewards documents both methods find and gets the strengths of each without having to make their incompatible scores comparable. Evaluations consistently show the hybrid beats either alone.

## What autocomplete is for
**Autocomplete** (also called typeahead or query suggestion) shows a short list of likely queries under the search box as the user types. It isn't just about saving keystrokes. Its bigger job is to **guide searchers to queries that work**: correctly spelled, phrased the way the catalogue phrases things, and specific enough to give good results. Users now expect it everywhere, and on mobile, where typing is slow, the suggestion list is often the whole search experience: a large share of mobile searches are completed by tapping a suggestion.

It's also a demanding system. A suggestion request fires on (almost) every keystroke, so request rates are several times the search rate, and the results must come back faster than the user types the next character: the end-to-end budget is about 100 ms, leaving the server perhaps 10 ms.

This lesson covers both halves: **which** completions to offer (a modelling question) and **how** to serve them fast (a data-structure and system question).

## Modelling: which completions to offer
Treat autocomplete as **conditional probability**: given the typed prefix, offer the full queries that are most probable. Estimate the probabilities from a query log.
- If 1,000 of 1,000,000 logged queries are "pants", then P(pants) = 0.001.
- If 50,000 logged queries start with "pa", then P(pants | pa) = 1,000 / 50,000 = 0.02.
- P(pants | pat) would be 0 by pure prefix matching, unless "pat" is a typo for "pant". That's why autocomplete must work together with spelling correction (lesson 09.15): it should tolerate an edit or two in the prefix.

### Complications
- **Recency and seasonality.** Raw frequency over a year suggests "halloween costume" in April. Weight recent counts more, for example with exponential decay (a count from 7 days ago weighs half a count from today), and boost seasonal queries in season.
- **Context.** Location ("pizza" suggests nearby cities), the current page or category, and the session can improve estimates, but too much context fragments the counts until the estimates become noisy (overfitting).
- **Non-prefix matches.** The typed text may not be a prefix of the best suggestion: "pa" should be able to suggest "mens pants" (infix match on a word boundary). This needs an index of word starts, not just query starts.
- **The feedback loop.** If you count queries that were produced by clicking a suggestion, suggestions become popular *because* they were suggested. Exclude or down-weight suggestion-clicked queries when counting.

### Popularity is not enough: query performance
A suggestion is only good if the search it leads to succeeds. So choose suggestions by the probability of a **successful** search (a click or purchase), roughly:

`value(q | prefix) = P(q | prefix) × P(success | q)`

! Example: popularity versus performance
! For prefix "pa": "pants" has P = 0.02 and a click-through rate of 0.1, so value = 0.002. "pant cuffs" is ten times rarer (P = 0.002) but has click-through 0.5, value = 0.001. "pa" itself as a query has P = 0.01 but click-through 0.01, value 0.0001.
! Ranking purely by success probability would put "pant cuffs" first, but that's a niche product most users don't want, so the combined value still favours the popular query, correctly. What performance data does well is **remove** bad suggestions: set a minimum performance threshold so queries that usually lead to zero results or no clicks ("pa") are never suggested.

## The data structure: a trie with cached top-k
The standard serving structure stores queries in a **trie** (pronounced "try", from re*trie*val), also called a **prefix tree**. Each node represents a prefix; each edge adds one character; a path from the root to a marked node spells a complete query. All queries sharing a prefix share the path for it, so "tree", "trie" and "try" share the nodes for "t" and "tr".

%%tree A trie for "tree", "trie", "try" and "toy". Each node caches its top completions, so a lookup is one walk down the prefix.
root
  t | top: tree, try, trie, toy
    *r | top: tree, try, trie
      e | top: tree
        e | tree (end)
      i | top: trie
        e | trie (end)
      y | try (end)
    o | top: toy
      y | toy (end)
%%

### The naive lookup and why it's too slow
To answer "top 5 completions for prefix p" without help:
1. Walk from the root to p's node: O(length of p) steps.
2. Visit **every** node below it, collecting each complete query and its count.
3. Sort the collected queries by count and take the top 5.

Step 2 is the problem. For a short prefix like "s", the subtree holds every logged query starting with "s", possibly millions of nodes. Doing that on every keystroke, for thousands of users a second, is impossible within a few milliseconds.

### The fix: cache top k at every node
Store at **every node** the list of its top k completions (with scores), computed once when the trie is built. A lookup becomes:
1. Walk down the prefix: a handful of steps, since people type short prefixes before choosing.
2. Read the stored list.

That's effectively **constant time** for the bounded prefix lengths people type.

%%compare Answering a prefix with and without cached top-k lists at each node.
[Naive trie]
Prefix "s" -(walk 1 node)-> Visit whole subtree -(sort millions)-> Top 5
- Millions of nodes per keystroke
- Latency grows with popularity
[*Cached top-k trie]
Prefix "s" -(walk 1 node)-> Read stored list -> Top 5
+ Constant-time lookup
- k entries stored at every node
- Updates touch every ancestor
%%

The costs:
- **Memory**: every node stores k entries. That's acceptable because the trie only holds queries above a popularity threshold (rare one-off queries are dropped), k is small (5 to 10), and entries can be pointers or IDs rather than full strings.
- **Updates are expensive**: when one query's count changes, the top-k lists of every ancestor node may change. Doing that per search is too costly, so the trie is usually **rebuilt offline** (hourly or daily) from aggregated logs, and the new version replaces the old atomically.

! Example: sizing the trie
! Keep the 10 million most frequent queries, average 20 characters. A trie shares prefixes, so suppose about 50 million nodes. Each node stores a character, child pointers and a top-5 list of 4-byte query IDs plus 4-byte scores: about 40 bytes for the list and maybe 40 more for structure, so about 80 bytes per node, or 4 GB in total. That fits in memory on one server, so the service can be replicated for throughput without sharding. At 20,000 suggestion requests a second at peak and about 0.2 ms of CPU per lookup, four replicas give ample headroom.

## The surrounding system
%%flow Autocomplete splits into a slow offline path that builds the trie and a fast read path that serves it.
[Offline] Query logs -(aggregate daily counts)-> Filter and score -> *Trie builder -(snapshot)-> Trie store
[Online] Keystroke -(debounced)-> CDN cache -(miss)-> Suggestion service -(in-memory trie)-> Suggestions
%%

### Data collection and building
- **Log** every submitted query with timestamp, whether it came from a suggestion, and its outcome (clicks, purchases).
- **Aggregate** counts per query per day in a batch job (Spark, or a stream processor for near-real-time).
- **Filter**: drop queries below a minimum count (also a privacy protection: a query typed by only one person must never be suggested to others), and drop offensive, unsafe or legally sensitive completions using a block list and a classifier. Autocomplete suggestions are seen as the platform speaking, so this step matters for trust.
- **Score** each query (decayed frequency × performance), then **build** the trie with top-k lists bottom-up: each node's list is the best k among its own query and its children's lists, so building is one pass.

### Serving
- The trie lives **in memory** in a suggestion service. If too large for one machine, **shard by prefix** (the first one or two characters), being careful that common first letters ("s", "c") get more shards.
- Replicate for read throughput and availability.
- Swap in new tries by loading the new snapshot alongside and switching a pointer, so there's no downtime.

### Client and edge
- **Debounce** keystrokes: send a request only after a short pause (say 50 to 100 ms) so a fast typist doesn't send a request per character.
- **Cache** recent responses in the browser, so backspacing is instant.
- **Cache popular prefixes at the CDN**: "a", "b", "ip", "sh" are requested constantly and are the same for everyone (in a region). One- and two-character prefixes cached at the edge remove a large share of origin traffic.
- **Cancel** in-flight requests when a newer keystroke arrives, and discard responses that come back out of order.

%%seq A user types "be". Debouncing skips the request for "b", and the response for "be" comes from the in-memory trie.
User > Browser : types "b"
Browser > Browser : debounce timer starts
User > Browser : types "e" within 80 ms
Browser > Browser : timer resets, then fires
Browser > CDN : GET /suggest?q=be
CDN > Suggestion service : cache miss
Suggestion service > Suggestion service : walk b, e; read top 5
Suggestion service -> CDN : 5 suggestions, cache 5 min
CDN -> Browser : best buy, bed bath, ...
%%

### Freshness
A daily rebuild misses breaking news and trending topics. Add a small **real-time layer**: a stream job counts queries over the last hour, detects ones rising fast, and keeps them in a small separate structure. The service merges its results with the main trie's at query time.

! Example: prefix "be"
! The node for "be" stores [best buy, bed bath, bestbuy hours, beats headphones, bee movie] with scores. Typing "e" sends `GET /suggest?q=be`; the CDN misses (it's late at night in this region), and the service walks b → e and returns the five cached entries in under a millisecond of server time. The full subtree below "be" (perhaps 400,000 queries) is never visited at query time; that work was done when the trie was built. The response is cached at the CDN for 5 minutes, so the next thousand users typing "be" never reach the service.

## Alternatives
- **Elasticsearch completion suggester**: an in-memory FST (a compressed relative of the trie) with weights, supporting fuzzy prefixes. Good when you already run Elasticsearch.
- **Edge n-grams in a normal index**: index "p", "pa", "pan", "pant" for each query and search them like terms. Flexible, slower, larger.
- **Redis sorted sets** per prefix: simple for small vocabularies.

## Common mistakes
- Computing completions at request time by scanning the subtree or querying the main search index with a prefix wildcard.
- Suggesting by raw frequency only, including queries that lead nowhere.
- Counting suggestion-clicked queries, creating a self-reinforcing loop.
- No safety filter or minimum count, exposing offensive or private queries.
- Firing a request on every keystroke with no debounce or caching.

Give the memory arithmetic.

## Why search needs spelling correction
Around 10 to 15% of search queries contain a spelling mistake, and on mobile keyboards the share is higher. A misspelled query usually matches nothing, or matches the wrong things: "iphnoe case" returns zero products, "speling" returns pages that contain the same typo. Each of those is a lost search. A spelling corrector turns them back into successful searches, and it's one of the highest-value pieces of query understanding (lesson 09.07).

The problem looks like it needs a dictionary and some clever string matching. The cleaner way to think about it is **probability**: of all the words the user could have meant, which is most likely, given what they typed?

## The model behind spelling correction
Given a typed word w, choose the candidate correction c that is most probable:

`correction(w) = argmax over c of P(c | w)`

By Bayes' rule, P(c | w) = P(c) × P(w | c) / P(w). P(w) is the same for every candidate, so it drops out:

`correction(w) = argmax over c of P(c) × P(w | c)`

This splits the problem into three parts:
- **Candidate model**: which corrections c to consider at all. Usually, known words within a small edit distance of w.
- **Language model**, P(c): how common the word c is, regardless of the typo. Estimate it by counting words in a large text corpus; around a million words is enough for a decent start. In English text, "the" makes up about 7% of all words.
- **Error model**, P(w | c): how likely it is that someone meaning c would type w. A real one is learned from data about actual typos; a simple one just prefers fewer edits.

A working corrector built on this idea fits in about 20 lines of Python, and its reasoning answers this lesson's question directly.

## Generating candidates
An **edit** is one of four operations on a string:
- **Deletion**: remove one letter ("spelling" to "speling").
- **Transposition**: swap two adjacent letters ("the" to "teh").
- **Replacement**: change one letter to another.
- **Insertion**: add one letter.

The **edit distance** between two strings is the smallest number of edits that turns one into the other. (Counting transpositions as one edit gives the Damerau-Levenshtein distance; plain Levenshtein counts a swap as two replacements.)

For a word of length n, there are n deletions, n − 1 transpositions, 26n replacements and 26(n + 1) insertions: **54n + 25** strings at edit distance 1. For "speling" (n = 7) that's 403 strings, a few of them duplicates, and almost none are real words. Applying edits twice gives edit distance 2: about 403² ≈ 160,000 strings. That sounds like a lot, but generating them takes milliseconds, and filtering to **known words** (those in the vocabulary) leaves only a handful.

%%tree Candidates for "speling", grouped by edit distance. Only known words survive, and frequency picks among the nearest group.
speling (not a known word)
  Edit distance 1
    *spelling | insert l, very common
    spewing | replace l with w, rare
    spieling | insert i, rare
  Edit distance 2
    peeling | delete s, insert e
    sapling | insert a, delete e
%%

### A simple error model: priority by distance
The simplest error model doesn't compute real probabilities. It uses a priority order:
1. The original word, if it's a known word.
2. Otherwise, known words at edit distance 1.
3. Otherwise, known words at edit distance 2.
4. Otherwise, the original word unchanged (don't invent a correction).

Within the first non-empty group, pick the word with the highest P(c). In other words: **choose the nearest correction, and when several are equally near, choose the most common**.

```
def correction(word):
    return max(candidates(word), key=P)

def candidates(word):
    return (known([word]) or known(edits1(word))
            or known(edits2(word)) or [word])
```

%%flow Candidate order: prefer the word itself if known, then known words one edit away, then two, then give up.
"speling" -(known word?)-> No -(edit distance 1)-> spelling, spewing -(pick most frequent)-> *spelling
%%

## Why you need both parts
Now the question: why both edit distance and frequency?
- **Edit distance alone gives ties.** "speling" is one edit from "spelling" (insert l) and also one edit from "spewing" (replace l with w). Edit distance can't choose between them. For short words it's worse: "teh" is one edit from "the", "ten", "tea", "tech", "eh" and more.
- **Frequency alone is absurd.** "the" is the most common word in English. A frequency-only corrector would "correct" every unknown word to "the". Frequency says nothing about whether c resembles what was typed.
- **Together** they work: edit distance limits the candidates to plausible typos, and frequency picks the likeliest among them. "spelling" wins because it's both one edit away and far more common than "spewing". Similarly, "grnt" could be "grant" or "grunt" (both one insertion away), and the more frequent "grant" wins.

! Example: scoring candidates for "speling"
! Suppose in a 1-million-word corpus, "spelling" occurs 130 times and "spewing" 4 times. P(spelling) = 0.00013, P(spewing) = 0.000004. Both are at distance 1, so the simple error model treats them equally, and the corrector picks "spelling", about 30 times more probable.
! Now a real error model. From typo data, dropping a doubled letter ("ll" to "l") is a very common mistake, say probability 0.01 for that position; typing "l" when you meant "w" (keys far apart) is rare, say 0.0001. Then P(c) × P(w | c) gives spelling: 0.00013 × 0.01 = 1.3 × 10⁻⁶, spewing: 0.000004 × 0.0001 = 4 × 10⁻¹⁰. The real error model makes the decision even clearer.

## How well the simple version works
A corrector built exactly this way, with word counts from about a million words of text, scored **75% of 270** test words correct at 41 words per second on a development set, and **68% of 400** at 35 words per second on a held-out test set. Not bad for 20 lines, and the failures point to every improvement real systems make:
- **Better language model**: more and more representative text. And **context**: bigram or trigram probabilities, so "their" versus "there" depends on the neighbouring words.
- **A learned error model**: some mistakes (adjacent keys on the keyboard, doubled letters, vowel swaps, phonetic spellings like "fone") are much more likely than others. Learn P(w | c) per edit type from pairs of typo and correction.
- **Unknown words in the dictionary**: if the language model doesn't contain the intended word, no correction can find it.
- **Larger distances** in some cases, and words that should be **split** ("runningshoes") or **joined** ("ear buds").

Distance 2 | 15 | ~15%

## Spelling correction in search
Search engines adapt the model in several ways.

### Correct against your own vocabulary and logs
Don't use a generic dictionary. Product names, brands, slang and model numbers aren't in it, and generic word frequency is wrong for your domain. Build the language model from the **index vocabulary** (terms in your documents, weighted by document frequency) and from **query logs** (what other searchers typed and then didn't correct). The right correction is what other users meant. Query logs are also where the best error model comes from: a query followed seconds later by a similar query that succeeds is a free (typo, correction) pair.

### Find candidates fast with a k-gram index
Generating 160,000 strings per word is fine for one word but wasteful at thousands of queries a second, and edits2 gets expensive for long words. A **k-gram index** narrows candidates first:
1. Break every vocabulary term into overlapping character k-grams (usually bigrams or trigrams, with boundary markers: "$bo", "bor", "ord", "rd$").
2. Build an inverted index from each k-gram to the terms containing it.
3. For a misspelled word, break it into k-grams, look up their postings, and find terms that share many k-grams (by Jaccard overlap).
4. Compute exact edit distance only for those few terms.

%%flow A k-gram index narrows candidates before computing edit distance.
"bord" -(bigrams: bo, or, rd)-> Postings for each bigram -(most shared)-> *board, border, lord -(edit distance)-> board
%%

Another fast approach is **symmetric delete** (used by the SymSpell library): precompute all deletions of every vocabulary word up to distance 2 and index them. At query time, generate only deletions of the input (n strings, not 54n) and look them up. It's very fast at the cost of a large precomputed table.

### Context-sensitive correction
Some errors are real words in the wrong place: "flew form Heathrow" has no misspelled word, but "form" should be "from". Only phrase context reveals it. Use the query log and a bigram language model: P("flew from heathrow") is much higher than P("flew form heathrow"), so suggest the change. Treat each word as having candidate alternatives and score whole query variants.

### How to present it
- **Auto-correct silently** when you're very confident and the original query returns nothing ("iphnoe" to "iphone").
- **Show results for the correction with a link back**: "Showing results for *iphone*. Search instead for *iphnoe*."
- **Suggest only**: "Did you mean *carrot*?" when the original returns reasonable results and confidence is moderate.
- **Never correct** exact identifiers, quoted phrases, or known brand names that look like typos ("Asics" is not "basics").

%%matrix How to act on a correction, by confidence and whether the original query found results.
Situation | Auto-correct | Show both | Suggest only
High confidence, 0 results | yes | partial | no
High confidence, some results | partial | yes | partial
Low confidence, 0 results | no | partial | yes
Low confidence, good results | no | no | yes
%%

! Example: "acress"
! Candidates at distance 1 include "actress" (insert t), "across" (replace e with o), "access" (replace r with c), "caress" (swap a and c), "cress" (delete a) and "acres" (delete s). A pure frequency choice picks "across", the most common of them. A learned error model says omitting a "t" after "c" is a much more common typo than replacing "o" with "e", which pushes "actress" up. Context settles it: "a famous acress" makes "actress" far more likely under a bigram model, while "acress the river" makes "across" likely. This is why production systems learn the language model, the error model and context from data together.

## Common mistakes
- Correcting with edit distance alone, giving arbitrary picks among ties.
- Using a generic dictionary, so brands and product names are "corrected" into common words.
- Auto-correcting confidently when the original query had good results.
- Ignoring context, so real-word errors ("form" for "from") are never caught.
- Running edits2 over a huge vocabulary per request without a k-gram or symmetric-delete index.

Then mention a k-gram index for speed, context for real-word errors, and confidence-based presentation.

## What related searches are
**Related searches** are the suggestions shown at the bottom or side of a results page ("People also search for", "Related searches"): other queries the user might want to run next. They help in three situations:
- The first query was **too broad**: "jaguar" could mean the car, the animal or the American football team. Suggestions like "jaguar f-type price" and "jaguar habitat" let the user say which.
- The first query was **too narrow** or phrased oddly, and a more common phrasing works better: "inexpensive airfare" to "cheap flights".
- The user wants to **explore**: after "tents", "camping stoves", "sleeping bags".

Related searches are close cousins of **query expansion**, which silently changes the query rather than offering alternatives. They use the same raw material, so this lesson covers expansion first, then how to mine related searches from logs, then how to score, clean and serve them.

## Query expansion
**Expansion** adds terms so a query matches documents that use different words for the same thing. For example, "vp marketing" becomes `(vp OR "vice president") marketing`, so job ads that spell out the title are found.

Sources of expansions:
- **Synonyms** ("couch" for "sofa", "trainers" for "sneakers") and **abbreviations** ("vp", "nyc", "tv").
- **Stemming done as expansion**: instead of stemming the index, expand "shoe" to "shoe OR shoes" at query time. This keeps the index exact (so phrase and exact searches still work) and lets you control which variants are added.
- **Mined equivalences** from logs: queries that lead to the same clicks (below) can be treated as near-synonyms.
- **Embedding neighbours**: terms whose vectors are close (lesson 09.13).

Expansion is a **precision-versus-recall trade-off** (lesson 09.09). It increases recall (more documents match) but risks precision when the added term changes the meaning. "apple" expanded to "fruit" is great on a grocery site and terrible on an electronics site. "Java" expanded to "coffee" ruins a programming search.

How much should an expanded term count? Three options:
- **Treat it equally** with the original terms. Simple, but expansions that are only roughly equivalent pull in noise.
- **Discount** it: give expanded terms a lower weight (for example 0.5), so documents matching the user's exact words still rank first.
- **Feed it to the ranking model** as features ("matched an original term", "matched only an expansion") and let a learned re-ranker decide (lesson 09.08).

Related searches avoid much of this risk by **showing** the alternatives and letting the user choose instead of silently changing the query.

%%compare Expansion silently broadens the query; related searches offer alternatives and leave the choice to the user.
[Query expansion]
"vp marketing" -(add synonyms)-> One broader query -> More results
+ Recall improves invisibly
- Wrong synonyms hurt precision
- User can't see or undo it
[*Related searches]
"vp marketing" -(look up mined list)-> Suggestions shown -> User picks
+ User stays in control
+ Helps disambiguate broad queries
- Needs a click to help
%%

## Mining related searches from query logs
The strongest signal is **what other users did next**. Query logs record, for each search, the user (or anonymous session ID), the time, the query, which results were shown and which were clicked. Three common sources of related pairs come from them.

### 1. Session co-occurrence
Group each user's searches into **sessions**: runs of searches with no gap longer than, say, 30 minutes. Queries in the same session are likely related, because people search in bursts around one task. For every pair (A, B) where B follows A in a session, increment a count. Over millions of sessions, strong pairs emerge: "tent" is often followed by "sleeping bag".

### 2. Reformulations
A **reformulation** is a special, stronger case: a user searches A, clicks nothing (or clicks and comes straight back), and within a few seconds searches B. That suggests A didn't work and B was the user's better attempt. Reformulations that **lead to clicks or purchases** are especially valuable: they're a log of users fixing their own queries. "inexpensive airfare" → no clicks → "cheap flights" → click is a strong signal to suggest "cheap flights" for "inexpensive airfare". Many reformulations are small edits (adding a word, fixing a typo), which also feed spelling correction (lesson 09.15).

### 3. The click graph
Build a **bipartite graph**: one set of nodes for queries, another for documents (or URLs, or products), and an edge from a query to each document clicked for it, weighted by click count. Two queries that lead to clicks on the **same documents** are related even if they share no words. "cheap flights" and "budget airfare" both lead to clicks on the same flight comparison sites. Similarity can be measured by the overlap of their clicked-document sets (cosine or Jaccard over click vectors), or by **random walks** on the graph: start at a query, step to a clicked document, step back to another query that clicked it, and count where you land.

%%tree The click graph connects queries that share no words but lead to clicks on the same documents.
Clicked documents
  *flight comparison site A
    "cheap flights" | 9,000 clicks
    "budget airfare" | 1,200 clicks
  flight comparison site B
    "cheap flights" | 6,000 clicks
    "budget airfare" | 800 clicks
    "last minute flights" | 2,500 clicks
%%

## Scoring and cleaning
Raw co-occurrence counts are dominated by queries that are popular everywhere. "facebook", "weather" and "youtube" follow almost every query in some session, simply because people search for them constantly. Suggesting "weather" after "jaguar" helps nobody.

### Normalise by popularity
Compare how often B follows A with how often you'd expect B by chance:

`lift(A → B) = P(B follows A) / P(B)`

or the closely related **pointwise mutual information**, PMI = log of that ratio. B is suggested for A only if it's **unusually** likely after A. A lift of 1 means no association; a lift of 50 means B is 50 times more likely after A than in general.

! Example: lift for "jaguar"
! Suppose 1% of all sessions contain "weather" and 0.01% contain "jaguar f-type". Among 100,000 sessions containing "jaguar", "weather" follows in 2,000 (2%) and "jaguar f-type" in 1,200 (1.2%).
! Lift for weather = 2% / 1% = 2: barely above chance. Lift for jaguar f-type = 1.2% / 0.01% = 120: very strongly associated. Raw counts would rank "weather" first; lift correctly drops it and keeps "jaguar f-type".

Lift has the opposite failure for very rare B: a query seen twice, both times after A, gets an enormous lift from tiny counts. So require a **minimum count** for each pair, or use a smoothed score that shrinks small-count estimates towards no association.

%%bars Raw follow counts versus lift for queries after "jaguar". Lift removes queries that are popular everywhere.
weather (raw) | 2000 | 2,000 follows, lift 2
jaguar f-type (raw) | 1200 | 1,200 follows, lift 120
*jaguar f-type (lift) | 120 | lift 120, kept
weather (lift) | 2 | lift 2, dropped
%%

### Further cleaning
- **Remove near-duplicates** of the original and of each other ("jaguar cars" versus "jaguar car", "jaguar f type" versus "jaguar f-type"), so the suggestions are actually different. Normalise and compare analysed forms.
- **Diversify**: for an ambiguous query, make sure the final list covers several meanings (car, animal, team), not eight variants of one.
- **Apply query performance**: only suggest queries that themselves lead to successful searches (clicks, purchases), as with autocomplete (lesson 09.14).
- **Safety**: filter offensive or sensitive suggestions with a block list and a classifier. A suggestion on your page reads as your platform's recommendation.
- **Privacy**: require that each pair was seen from a minimum number of **distinct users** (say 20 or more), so one person's unusual searches can never be shown to others. Don't let a user's own private sessions shape suggestions shown to others in identifiable ways.
- **Fallback for rare queries**: many queries are too rare to have log data. Fall back to content: queries whose top results overlap with this query's results, queries sharing rare terms, or the embedding nearest neighbours among known good queries (lesson 09.13).

## Building and serving
%%flow Mining related searches: sessions and the click graph give candidate pairs; scoring and filtering turn them into a lookup table.
[Mine] Query logs -(split into sessions)-> Follow pairs and reformulations -(lift, min users)-> *Candidate pairs
[Graph] Query logs -(query to clicked doc)-> Click graph -(shared clicks)-> *Candidate pairs
[Serve] *Candidate pairs -(dedupe, diversify, filter)-> Key-value table -(cache)-> Results page
%%

The pairs are computed **offline** in a batch job (daily is typical) over the last few weeks of logs:
1. Sessionise the logs and extract follow pairs and reformulations.
2. Build the click graph and compute query-query similarity for queries above a minimum traffic.
3. Merge the candidate sources with weights, compute scores, and apply the cleaning steps.
4. Keep the top 8 or so per query and write them to a **key-value table**: normalised query → list of related queries.

At query time, the search service normalises the query, looks it up in the table (an in-memory cache or Redis in front of a key-value store), and renders the list. It's one lookup, well under a millisecond, and can run in parallel with the main search so it adds no latency.

! Example: sizing the table
! Related searches are stored for the 20 million most common normalised queries. Each entry holds 8 related queries of about 25 bytes each, plus the key: roughly 250 bytes per query, so about 5 GB in total. That fits in a replicated in-memory store. The daily batch job processes 30 days of logs (say 3 billion searches); with sessionisation and pair counting in Spark, it runs in an hour or two on a modest cluster. Queries outside the 20 million fall back to content-based suggestions or show none.

## Measuring success
Track the **click-through rate on related searches**, the **success rate** of searches started from a suggestion (do they lead to clicks or purchases?), and whether sessions that use them end successfully more often. A/B test changes to the mining or scoring.

## Common mistakes
- Using raw co-occurrence counts, so generic queries dominate every list.
- Using lift without a minimum count, so one-off queries with tiny counts appear.
- Showing near-duplicates of the original query.
- No privacy threshold, so a rare personal query from one user appears for others.
- Expanding queries silently with risky synonyms when showing them as suggestions would be safer.

## What the feature needs
"Recent searches" shows a user their last few queries when they tap or click into the search box, before they type anything. It's small, but it's one of the most used parts of search: people repeat searches a lot (checking an order, re-finding a product, a colleague's name in a chat app), and a tap on a recent search saves typing and lands on a query that already worked.

Write the requirements down precisely, because each one drives a design choice:
- **Per user**: every user has their own list.
- **Ordered newest first.**
- **A fixed maximum**, say 10 entries. Older ones fall off.
- **No duplicates**: searching "running shoes" again moves it to the top rather than adding a second copy.
- **Fast reads**: the list appears the instant the search box gets focus, so the read must take a millisecond or two on the server.
- **Cheap writes**: one write per search, which can be a very high rate across all users.
- **User control**: delete one entry, or clear all.
- **Privacy**: search history is personal data, with retention limits and legal obligations.

The question "where, with what eviction, and what about privacy?" maps directly onto these: a store suited to small per-user ordered lists, eviction by recency with a fixed cap, and a set of privacy controls.

## Redis lists fit exactly
A **Redis list** is an ordered sequence of strings stored under one key. Internally it's a **quicklist**: a doubly linked list of compact, contiguous blocks (listpacks). That gives:
- **O(1)** push and pop at either end (head or tail).
- O(N) access by index in general, which doesn't matter at length 10.
- Up to 2³² − 1 elements per list, far more than needed.

Redis lists support a well-known pattern called the **capped list**: push the newest item to the head, then trim the list to a fixed length so the oldest item falls off the tail. That is exactly "keep the 10 most recent".

```
LREM   recent:u42 0 "running shoes"   # remove any older copy
LPUSH  recent:u42 "running shoes"     # newest at the head, O(1)
LTRIM  recent:u42 0 9                 # keep only the 10 newest
EXPIRE recent:u42 7776000             # whole list expires after 90 idle days
LRANGE recent:u42 0 9                 # read newest to oldest
```

What each command does and costs:
- `LREM key 0 value` removes **all** copies of a value. It's O(N) in the list's length, trivial at 10. It provides de-duplication, and it also implements "delete this one entry" when the user taps the x.
- `LPUSH` adds to the head in O(1).
- `LTRIM 0 9` keeps indexes 0 to 9 and drops the rest. Its cost is O(N) in the number of elements **removed**, which here is at most one per push.
- `EXPIRE` sets a time-to-live on the whole key. Each new search resets it, so the list only disappears after 90 days of no searching.
- `LRANGE 0 9` reads the list; its cost is proportional to the offset plus the number of elements returned, tiny for 10.

%%flow Recording a search: remove the older copy, push to the head, trim to 10, and refresh the expiry.
New search -(LREM duplicate)-> LPUSH to head -(LTRIM 0 9)-> *10 newest kept -(EXPIRE)-> Idle lists vanish
%%

### Atomicity
Two searches from the same user can arrive at once (two tabs, a retry). If their commands interleave (`LREM` from one, `LREM` from the other, then both `LPUSH`es), you can end up with a duplicate. Wrap the sequence in `MULTI` / `EXEC` (a transaction that runs the commands back to back with nothing interleaved) or in a short **Lua script**, which Redis runs atomically. Pipelining the commands also cuts the round trips from four to one.

### Eviction by recency
**Eviction** is the rule for what to throw away when the list is full. Here it's **recency**: the oldest entry is removed when an eleventh is pushed. That matches what users expect from "recent". Frequency-based eviction (keep the most-searched) would be a different feature, "frequent searches", and would need counts, which a sorted set provides instead.

%%timeline One user's list over a day. Repeats move to the top; the oldest entry falls off when an eleventh distinct search arrives.
09:00 | Search "running shoes": list has 1 entry
09:05 | Search "trail shoes": 2 entries, newest first
12:30 | Search "running shoes" again: *moved to top, still 2
14:00 | Eight more searches: list is full at 10
14:10 | Eleventh distinct search: oldest entry trimmed
18:00 | User taps x on "running shoes": LREM removes it
%%

### Alternatives you could use
- A **sorted set** keyed by query with the timestamp as score: `ZADD` naturally de-duplicates (updating the score moves it to the top), and `ZREMRANGEBYRANK key 0 -11` trims to the newest 10. Equally good; slightly more memory.
- A **database table** `(user_id, query, searched_at)` with an index on `(user_id, searched_at desc)`: works, but every search becomes an insert plus a delete of old rows, and reads hit the database on every search-box focus. Fine at small scale, wasteful at large.
- **Inside the main search logs**: no, those are analytical, batch-oriented and shouldn't be read per user in real time.

### Sizing
Memory is small. 10 entries of about 30 bytes each, plus Redis's per-key and list overhead (roughly 100 to 200 bytes), is under 1 KB per user.

! Example: sizing for 10 million active users
! 10,000,000 users × about 800 bytes ≈ 8 GB, call it 10 GB with overhead and headroom. A Redis cluster of 3 primaries with 4 to 8 GB each, plus replicas, holds it comfortably, sharded by the user key (`recent:{user_id}` hashes to a slot). If those users run 50 million searches a day with peaks of 2,000 searches a second, that's 2,000 pipelined write transactions a second, and maybe 5,000 reads a second from search-box focus events. A single Redis primary handles over 100,000 simple operations a second, so the load is trivial; the cluster is sized for memory and availability, not throughput.

### Durability choices
- If losing the history on a cache failure is acceptable (it usually is: it's a convenience, and it rebuilds itself as the user searches), **Redis alone** is enough. Enable persistence (AOF or snapshots) and replicas so routine restarts and failovers don't lose it.
- If the history must **sync across devices** reliably or be kept as part of the user's account (some products show it on a "your activity" page), write it to a **durable key-value store** too, for example DynamoDB keyed by user, and treat Redis as a cache in front. Write asynchronously so search latency isn't affected.
- **Logged-out users** can keep recent searches in the browser's **local storage**, which never leaves the device. Optionally merge it into the account history when they sign in.

%%seq A search writes the history asynchronously; focusing the search box later reads it from Redis in about a millisecond.
User > Search API : search "running shoes"
Search API > Search engine : run query
Search API > Redis : MULTI LREM, LPUSH, LTRIM, EXPIRE (async)
Search engine -> Search API : results
Search API -> User : results page
User > Search API : focus search box later
Search API > Redis : LRANGE recent:u42 0 9
Redis -> Search API : 10 recent queries
Search API -> User : show recent searches
%%

Note the write is **off the critical path**: the search results don't wait for the history write. If Redis is briefly unavailable, the search still works and that one history entry is simply missed.

## Privacy
Search history is **personal data**, and often sensitive: it can reveal health conditions, finances, sexuality, religion, politics, job hunting or relationship trouble. Treat it accordingly.
- **User control.** A visible "Clear history" (delete the key, and any durable copy) and a per-item remove (`LREM`). A setting to **turn history off**, after which nothing is recorded. Honour these immediately, not in tomorrow's batch.
- **Retention limits.** Keep only what the feature needs. The `EXPIRE` above deletes idle history automatically; you might also cap the age of individual entries. Data you don't keep can't leak.
- **Separation from analytics.** Aggregate query logs used for autocomplete, related searches and ranking (lessons 09.14, 09.16, 09.08) should be pseudonymised, aggregated and **thresholded** (a query must come from many distinct users before it can be suggested), and must never surface one user's searches to another.
- **Account deletion and legal requests.** Deleting an account must delete this key and any durable copy. Under laws such as the GDPR, users can ask for a copy of their data or its erasure, so you must be able to find and remove every copy, including backups within their retention window.
- **Shared and private contexts.** Show history only to the signed-in user. Don't record searches made in private or incognito modes. On shared devices, consider not showing history at all until the user signs in.
- **Security.** Restrict who can read the Redis cluster; don't log full history in application logs; encrypt durable copies at rest.

! Example: one user's day, with deletion
! u42 searches "running shoes", then "trail shoes", then "running shoes" again. On the third search the transaction runs LREM (removing the old "running shoes"), LPUSH (adding it at the head), LTRIM (no-op, only 2 entries) and EXPIRE (90 days from now). The list reads ["running shoes", "trail shoes"].
! That evening, u42 searches for a medical symptom, then decides they don't want it in their history and taps the x: `LREM recent:u42 0 "<that query>"`. It's gone from Redis at once and from the durable copy within seconds. Because autocomplete only suggests queries seen from at least 50 distinct users, the query was never eligible to appear for anyone else. If u42 doesn't search for 90 days, the whole key expires and the history disappears without anyone running a cleanup job.

## Common mistakes
- Appending without de-duplication, so the list shows "running shoes" five times.
- Doing the history write synchronously in the search request path.
- Storing history forever with no expiry or user controls.
- Feeding raw individual histories into shared suggestions, leaking one user's searches to others.
- Forgetting the durable copy and backups when handling deletion requests.
