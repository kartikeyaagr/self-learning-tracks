@01.01
## Why this step comes first
A system design prompt is deliberately vague. "Design Twitter" could mean a weekend prototype for a hackathon or a platform for 500 million people, and those are completely different systems. The prototype is one server and one database. The platform needs precomputed feeds, sharded storage, a media pipeline and a CDN. If you start drawing before you know which one you're building, you will almost certainly build the wrong one, and every minute spent on it is wasted.

The same is true at work. A product manager who says "we need notifications" might mean a nightly email digest or sub-second push alerts on a trading app. The first is a cron job; the second is a fleet of persistent connections. Requirements clarification is the cheap step that prevents the expensive mistake.

So the first few minutes go on turning a vague prompt into a short, written, agreed list of what you are building and how well it must work. In a 45-minute interview that's roughly 3 to 8 minutes. On a real project it might be a one-page design brief. Either way, the output is the same: a list you can point back to when you make every later decision.

%%timeline How the first ten minutes of a 45-minute design session typically go.
0 min | Restate the prompt in your own words
1 min | List candidate features, ask which matter most
3 min | *Agree the top 3 functional requirements and what's out of scope
5 min | Ask about scale, latency, consistency and durability
7 min | *Write 3 to 5 measurable non-functional requirements
9 min | Note headline numbers, then start the design
%%

## Functional requirements: what the system does
Functional requirements are the features, written as things a user (or another system) can do. A good habit is to phrase each one as "Users should be able to...". That phrasing forces you to think about who acts and what they achieve, rather than about implementation.

Narrow them to the **top three**. Real products have hundreds of features, but a design can only go deep on a few, and the core few usually decide the architecture. For a Twitter-like product:
- Users can post short text posts (tweets), optionally with an image.
- Users can follow other users.
- Users can see a home feed of recent posts from people they follow.

Everything else is explicitly declared out of scope: direct messages, search, trends, ads, notifications. Saying "I'll leave search out for now, but the design should allow adding it later" is a sign of control, not a gap. It shows you know the feature exists and that you're making a conscious choice to focus.

Why only three? Because each extra feature drags in its own storage, APIs and edge cases. Search alone needs an inverted index, a separate ingestion pipeline and ranking. Including it means spending your limited time on a second system instead of going deep on the first.

### Turning a feature into something precise
Even a simple feature hides choices. "Users can see a feed" raises questions:
- Is it ordered by time, or ranked by relevance?
- How far back does it go? The last 24 hours, or everything?
- Is it the same for everyone who follows the same people, or personalised?
- Does a new post have to appear within one second, or is a minute fine?

Each answer changes the design. A chronological feed can be precomputed per user. A ranked feed needs a scoring step at read time. A one-minute delay allows asynchronous fan-out through a queue; a one-second guarantee might not.

%%compare A vague feature versus a precise one: the precise version is something you can design and test against.
[Vague]
Users see a feed
- Ordering not defined
- Freshness not defined
- No idea how big the feed is
[*Precise]
Users see the 50 newest posts from people they follow, newest first
+ Order: reverse chronological
+ New posts visible within 60 seconds
+ Paged, 50 at a time, back 30 days
%%

## Non-functional requirements: how well it does it
Non-functional requirements (NFRs) describe qualities: speed, scale, reliability, correctness, cost. Write them as "The system should...". The key is to make them **specific to this system** and, where possible, **measurable**.

"Low latency" is weak, because every system wants it and it can't be checked. "The home feed renders in under 200 ms at the 99th percentile" is useful. It names the operation (feed render), the measure (p99) and the threshold (200 ms). Later it will justify specific choices, like a cache in front of the database or precomputing feeds instead of building them on every request.

A checklist to pick 3 to 5 from:
- **Scale**: users, requests per second, data size, read/write ratio, traffic bursts (a live sports final, Black Friday).
- **Latency**: which operations must be fast, and how fast. Not every operation needs to be: posting can take 500 ms; scrolling the feed can't.
- **Availability vs consistency**: is it worse to show stale data or to show an error? For a feed, stale is fine. For a bank balance, it isn't.
- **Durability**: can any acknowledged data ever be lost? A chat message, a payment, a medical record: never. A typing indicator: who cares.
- **Security and compliance**: authentication, privacy laws (GDPR), data residency (data must stay in the EU).
- **Environment**: mobile clients on flaky networks, a small budget, a team that only knows Java, an existing Postgres you must reuse.

%%matrix How strict each quality needs to be depends on the product. The same checklist gives very different answers.
Product | Latency target | Stale reads OK? | Data loss OK? | Scale
Social feed | 200 ms p99 | yes, seconds | partial, likes can drift | 100M+ users
Bank transfer | 1 s | no | no | thousands per second
Chat app | 500 ms delivery | partial, typing status | no, messages | 50M users
Analytics dashboard | 5 s | yes, minutes | partial, sampled | billions of events
%%

### Rank them, because they conflict
NFRs fight each other. Strong consistency costs latency (you must coordinate replicas) and availability (you may have to refuse requests during a network problem). Very high availability costs money (redundant everything). Low latency worldwide means data in many regions, which makes consistency harder.

So after listing them, rank them. "Correctness of payments beats latency; latency of the feed beats freshness." Agreeing on that order early means many later decisions make themselves.

## Questions worth asking
Good questions each remove a branch of the design tree. For a news feed:
- Mobile, web, or both? (Mobile means flaky networks, battery, push notifications.)
- Which features matter most? (Tells you where to go deep.)
- Chronological or ranked? (Precompute vs score at read time.)
- How many people can a user follow? (5,000 caps fan-out; "unlimited" means celebrities with 100 million followers.)
- How much traffic? (10 million daily active users is one region with caching; 1 billion is a global system.)
- Text only, or images and video too? (Video brings transcoding, object storage, CDN.)
- How fresh must the feed be? (Seconds vs minutes.)

%%tree Each answer prunes the design space. Here, two questions already decide most of the feed architecture.
Design a news feed
  Ranked by relevance? | yes
    Score candidates at read time
    *Precompute candidates, rank on read
  Chronological? | yes
    Follows capped at 5,000?
      *Fan out on write: push post IDs to followers
    Unlimited followers?
      Hybrid: push for most, pull for celebrities
%%

If the interviewer won't answer, **state an assumption out loud and write it down**: "I'll assume 100 million daily active users and that a feed can be up to a minute stale." You'll lean on those assumptions later, and writing them down means nobody can say you changed the problem halfway through.

## Should you estimate capacity now?
Some people run detailed back-of-the-envelope numbers at the start; others only calculate when a number will change a decision. A good middle path is to note the **headline numbers** now (daily active users, rough requests per second, data growth per day) and save detailed maths for the moment it decides something, such as whether the data fits on one machine or whether one database can take the write rate.

For example, "100 million daily users, each loading the feed 10 times a day" is 1 billion feed loads a day, which is about 12,000 per second on average and maybe 30,000 at peak. You don't need more precision than that to know you'll need caching and many stateless servers.

## Common mistakes
- **Designing before agreeing.** Drawing boxes in minute one. You'll get the scale or the core feature wrong.
- **Feature sprawl.** Listing ten features and going deep on none.
- **Unmeasurable NFRs.** "Scalable, fast and reliable" says nothing.
- **Silent assumptions.** Assuming a number without saying it, then contradicting it later.
- **Forgetting to write it down.** If it isn't on the board, it'll be forgotten by minute 30.

! Example: a URL shortener in about two minutes
! Functional: create a short link from a long URL; visiting the short link redirects to the original; optional custom alias; optional expiry date.
! Out of scope: analytics dashboards, user accounts, link editing.
! Non-functional: redirects are fast (p99 under 100 ms) and highly available (99.99%), since a broken link is very visible; reads outnumber writes about 100 to 1; a short link must never point to the wrong URL (correctness over availability for creation); 100 million new links per month.
! Headline numbers: 100M links a month is about 40 writes per second on average; at 100:1, about 4,000 redirects per second. Over 5 years that's 6 billion links, so a 7-character code from 62 symbols (62^7 ≈ 3.5 trillion) is plenty.

! Example: what changes if the answers change
! If the interviewer says "links expire after 24 hours and we expect 10 billion a month", storage becomes a rolling window instead of growing forever, and the write rate (about 4,000 per second) now needs a distributed ID generator rather than a single counter. Same feature list, very different design: that is why you ask.

@01.02
## From requirements to boxes
Once the requirements are agreed, the next job is a **high-level design**: the main components and how data flows between them. Think of it as the map you will navigate for the rest of the session. It should be simple enough to fit on one whiteboard, complete enough that every functional requirement has a path through it, and agreed with the interviewer (or your team) before you go deeper.

Jumping straight to boxes is still a mistake. Two small steps first make the drawing almost mechanical.

### Step 1: core entities
List the **nouns** the system stores and passes around. For a Twitter-like product: User, Tweet, Follow, and maybe Media. For a ride-hailing app: Rider, Driver, Ride, Location, Fare. Don't design full schemas yet; just name the entities and the one or two fields that matter for the design (a Tweet has an author and a timestamp; a Follow links two users).

Entities matter because they tell you what has to be stored, and how big it is. A Tweet is small text; Media is large binary data. That alone predicts two different kinds of storage.

### Step 2: the API
Write **one endpoint per functional requirement**. This forces you to think about inputs and outputs, and it becomes the contract every component must fulfil.

| Requirement | Endpoint | Returns |
| Post a tweet | `POST /v1/tweets {text, mediaId?}` | the new tweet's ID |
| Follow a user | `POST /v1/follows {followeeId}` | 204 No Content |
| Read the feed | `GET /v1/feed?cursor=...` | 50 tweets and a next cursor |

Two habits worth building now:
- **The current user comes from the auth token, never from the request body.** If `POST /v1/tweets` accepted `{authorId}`, anyone could post as anyone.
- **Paginate every list** with a cursor. "Return the whole feed" doesn't survive a user with 10,000 posts in it.

With entities and endpoints written down, the design becomes a sequence of small questions: for each endpoint, which components does a request touch, and what state changes?

## Draw one request at a time
Take each endpoint and trace it. For a URL shortener's redirect:

%%seq Tracing one endpoint end to end: a redirect touches the load balancer, the service, the cache and, on a miss, the database.
Browser > Load balancer : GET /abc123
Load balancer > Link service : forward
Link service > Cache : get abc123
Cache -> Link service : miss
Link service > Database : SELECT url WHERE code = abc123
Database -> Link service : https://example.com/long/path
Link service > Cache : set abc123 (TTL 1 day)
Link service -> Browser : 301 Location: https://example.com/long/path
%%

Then the create path. Then any background work. When every endpoint has a path, the high-level design is done, and every box on the board is justified by at least one requirement. If a box isn't on any path, delete it.

## Logical before physical
A **logical** design names roles, not products: client, load balancer, API service, feed service, relational database, key-value cache, message queue, object store. A **physical** design picks the products: NGINX, Postgres, Redis, Kafka, S3.

Name roles first. Naming a product too early ("we'll use Kafka") invites questions you can't yet justify ("why not SQS?") and pulls the conversation into product trivia. Choose products at the end, when a requirement makes the choice obvious: "we need replayable, ordered event streams consumed by three teams, so Kafka" is a justified choice; "Kafka because it's popular" is not.

%%compare The same design at two levels. Agree the logical roles first; pick products only when a requirement forces the choice.
[*Logical]
Client -(HTTPS)-> API service -(key lookup)-> Link store
+ Easy to discuss and change
+ Focuses on what each part must do
- Hides cost and operational details
[Physical]
Mobile app -(HTTPS)-> Go service on Kubernetes -(GetItem)-> DynamoDB + Redis
+ Concrete enough to build and cost
- Invites product debates too early
%%

### Good habits while drawing
- **Build up one endpoint at a time** instead of drawing everything at once.
- **Label every arrow** with what flows over it, and whether it is **synchronous** (request and wait for a response) or **asynchronous** (fire an event onto a queue and move on). That one distinction decides a lot about latency and failure handling.
- **Write key fields next to each data store**, not full schemas: "links: code (PK), url, created_at".
- **Mark where state lives.** Stateless services can be copied freely; stateful boxes (databases, caches) are where the hard problems are.
- **Defer optimisations.** When you think "we'll need a cache here", say it, note it, and move on. Add it once the simple path works and you can say which requirement needs it.

## The C4 model: zoom levels for diagrams
Diagrams fail when they mix levels: a box for "the whole payments platform" next to a box for "the retry helper function". The **C4 model** gives a vocabulary for drawing at a consistent level of detail. It defines four zoom levels, like a map that goes from country to street:

%%tree The C4 model's zoom levels. Each level zooms into one box of the level above.
System context: your system as one box
  Containers: apps and data stores
    API service
      Components: controllers, feed builder, auth client
        Code: classes and functions
    *Feed worker
    Postgres database
    Redis cache
%%

- **System context**: your system as a single box, with the people who use it and the external systems it talks to (payment provider, email service). Good for non-technical audiences.
- **Container**: zoom into your system. A container is anything that runs or stores data separately: a web app, a single-page app, a mobile app, a backend service, a database, an S3 bucket. The diagram shows how responsibilities are split, the main technology choices, and how containers communicate. Every team should have one.
- **Component**: zoom into one container to show its major internal parts (modules, controllers, a repository layer).
- **Code**: classes and functions. Rarely worth drawing by hand; IDEs generate these.

C4 deliberately leaves **deployment details** (clusters, load balancers, replication, failover, regions) off the container diagram, because they differ between production and staging. They go on separate deployment diagrams. In an interview, your high-level design is roughly a container diagram with a few deployment notes (a load balancer, replicas) added once they matter.

## Then go deep where it matters
After the high-level design comes a **deep dive** on two or three components, chosen because they are the hardest or most important part of this specific system: feed generation for Twitter, the ID generator for a URL shortener, matching for ride-hailing. Then come scaling concerns (load balancing, caching, sharding, replication) and failure modes.

The logical design is what makes that possible. You can only discuss bottlenecks once everyone can trace a request end to end, and you can only choose where to go deep once you can see all the parts.

## Worked example: URL shortener, from requirements to boxes
! Entities
! Link: code (7 characters, primary key), long_url, created_at, expires_at. That's under 1 KB per row.

! API
! POST /v1/links {url, alias?, expiresAt?} → 201 {code}. GET /{code} → 301 or 302 redirect, or 404 if unknown or expired.

! Create path
! Client → load balancer → link service. The service gets a unique number from an ID generator (so two creates never collide), encodes it in base 62 (0-9, a-z, A-Z) to get a 7-character code, stores (code → long URL) in the database, and returns the short link.

! Redirect path
! Client → load balancer → link service → cache. On a hit, return the redirect immediately. On a miss, read from the database, fill the cache, return the redirect. Reads outnumber writes 100 to 1, so the cache absorbs most of the load.

%%flow The URL shortener's high-level design: two paths, five roles, every box justified by a requirement.
[Create] Client -(POST /links)-> Link service -(next id)-> ID generator
[Create] Link service -(insert code, url)-> *Database
[Redirect] Client -(GET /abc)-> Link service -(get)-> Cache -(miss)-> *Database
%%

## Common mistakes
- **Boxes without paths.** A "search service" no requirement uses.
- **Unlabelled arrows.** Nobody knows what flows over them or whether the caller waits.
- **Product names as design.** "Kafka, Redis, Mongo" isn't an architecture.
- **Mixing zoom levels.** A whole subsystem and a helper function as equal boxes.
- **Drawing the scaled version first.** Start with the simplest design that meets the requirements; scale it when a number demands it.

> Takeaway: list entities and one API endpoint per requirement, then trace each endpoint through generic roles (logical design). Label arrows, mark where state lives, keep one zoom level, and pick products only when a requirement makes the choice obvious.

?? Client sends requests to an API service behind a load balancer. On create, the service takes a unique ID from an ID generator, base-62 encodes it, and writes (code → URL) to a database. On redirect, it reads the code from a cache, falls back to the database on a miss, and returns an HTTP redirect. Arrows: client to API (HTTP, sync), API to cache and database (sync reads and writes), API to ID generator (sync).

@01.03
## Quality attributes drive architecture
Two systems with identical features can look nothing alike. A to-do app for 100 people and one for 100 million share the same feature list (add a task, tick it off, share a list) but need completely different designs. The small one is a single server with SQLite. The big one needs load balancers, a replicated and sharded database, caches, multiple regions and an on-call rota. The difference lives entirely in the **non-functional requirements** (NFRs): the qualities the system must have, rather than the things it does.

That's why experienced engineers spend so long on NFRs. Features tell you what to build; NFRs tell you **how hard** it is to build, and which of the many possible designs is right.

## The common categories
- **Performance / latency**: how quickly an operation completes for one user. "Search results in under 300 ms."
- **Throughput**: how many operations per second the system handles. "50,000 writes per second at peak."
- **Scalability**: how the system copes as load and data grow. Can you add machines and get proportionally more capacity?
- **Availability**: the fraction of time, or of requests, the system serves correctly. "99.95% of requests succeed each month."
- **Durability**: whether acknowledged data survives crashes, disk failures and disasters. "No acknowledged payment is ever lost."
- **Consistency**: whether readers see the latest write, and in what order. "A user always sees their own posts immediately."
- **Security and privacy**: who can do and see what; encryption; audit trails; legal rules like GDPR.
- **Operability**: how easy it is to deploy, monitor, debug and recover. A design nobody can debug at 3 a.m. is a bad design.
- **Cost**: what it costs to build and run, per month and per unit of business (per order, per active user).

A useful outside checklist is the AWS Well-Architected Framework's six pillars: operational excellence, security, reliability, performance efficiency, cost optimisation and sustainability. When reviewing a real design, walking through those six catches gaps.

## Make each one measurable
NFRs must be **specific to the system** and **quantified**. Vague words feel safe but can't be checked, so they can't guide a design.

%%compare Vague qualities versus measurable ones. Only the measurable column can be tested, monitored and used to reject a design.
[Vague]
It should be fast
It should be highly available
It should scale
It should never lose data
- Can't be tested
- Can't decide between two designs
[*Measurable]
p99 feed load under 200 ms at 50k requests/s
99.95% of redirects succeed per 30 days
100M daily users, 5× bursts during live events
Zero loss of acknowledged messages, even if a zone fails
+ Testable and monitorable
+ Tells you which designs fail
%%

A measurable statement does two jobs:
- **It lets you check a design.** "Can a single Postgres primary take 50,000 writes per second with headroom?" Probably not, so the requirement forces batching, sharding or a different store.
- **It stops over-engineering.** "We need 99.9%" means multi-region active-active replication is unnecessary. You can save months of work and a lot of money.

A complete latency requirement names four things: **the operation**, **the percentile**, **the threshold** and **the load**. "p99 latency for loading the home feed is under 200 ms, measured at the server, at 50,000 requests per second." Drop any of the four and the statement gets slippery. "Under 200 ms" at what load? For which percentile? For which call?

## Use percentiles, not averages
Latency distributions have long tails. Most requests are quick, but a few hit a garbage-collection pause, a cold cache, a slow disk or a retry, and take ten or a hundred times longer. The average hides this completely.

Imagine 100 requests: 95 take 40 ms and 5 take 1,000 ms. The average is (95 × 40 + 5 × 1,000) / 100 = 88 ms, which sounds fine. But 1 in 20 users waits a full second. If a page makes 20 such calls, most page loads hit at least one slow one.

That's why latency is described with **percentiles**:
- **p50 (median)**: half the requests are faster than this. The "typical" experience.
- **p90 / p95**: the experience of the unlucky 1 in 10 or 1 in 20.
- **p99 / p99.9**: the bad-but-plausible case. At large scale, this is what many users hit every day.

%%bars One service's latency percentiles. The average (88 ms) hides that 1 in 100 requests takes over a second.
p50 | 40 | 40 ms
Average | 88 | 88 ms
p90 | 95 | 95 ms
p99 | 1050 | 1,050 ms
*p99.9 | 2200 | 2,200 ms
%%

Design for the high percentile you care about. If the SLO is "p99 under 200 ms", a design that achieves a 50 ms average but a 600 ms p99 fails, and you should know before you build it. Tail latency gets its own lesson (02.12), because fan-out to many servers makes it much worse.

### A measurement detail that matters
Percentiles can't be averaged. If ten servers each report their own p99, the average of those ten numbers is not the fleet's p99. To get a correct fleet-wide percentile, collect **histograms** (counts of requests per latency bucket) and add them up, then compute the percentile from the combined histogram. Lesson 10.12 covers how monitoring systems do this.

## Throughput and the read/write ratio
For each operation, write down how often it happens. Reads and writes stress systems differently:
- **Read-heavy** (feeds, product pages, URL redirects at 100:1): caching, read replicas and CDNs are the main tools.
- **Write-heavy** (metrics, logs, IoT sensors, chat): append-friendly storage, batching, partitioning and queues.
- **Balanced** (collaborative editing, order processing): usually the hardest, because caching helps less.

Also note **burstiness**. A ticketing site might average 100 requests per second but receive 100,000 in the minute a big concert goes on sale. Designing for the average there is designing for failure.

## Pick and rank
Choose the top 3 to 5 NFRs. Listing all nine categories, each with a number, wastes time and hides what actually matters. Then **rank** them, because they conflict:
- Strong consistency costs latency (replicas must coordinate) and availability (you may refuse requests during a network fault).
- Very high availability costs money: redundant servers, zones and regions, all paid for even when idle.
- Low latency worldwide means data in many regions, which makes consistency harder and more expensive.
- Strong security (encryption everywhere, fine-grained auth checks) costs some latency and engineering time.

%%matrix Common NFR conflicts. Strengthening the row's quality usually costs the column's.
Strengthen | Latency | Availability | Cost | Simplicity
Strong consistency | no, coordination adds round trips | no, may refuse during partitions | partial | partial
High availability | yes | yes | no, redundant capacity | no, failover logic
Global low latency | yes | yes | no, many regions | no, data placement
Durability (sync replicas) | no, wait for copies | yes | no, extra storage | partial
%%

Once the ranking is agreed ("a stale like count is fine but a double charge is not"), many later choices make themselves. The like counter can be eventually consistent and cached; the payment path must be strongly consistent and idempotent.

## Worked examples
! Example: chat app NFRs
! Latency: messages delivered to online recipients in under 500 ms at p99.
! Durability: no acknowledged message is ever lost, even if a server or a zone fails.
! Ordering: messages in one conversation appear in the same order for every participant.
! Availability: sending stays available even if read receipts and typing indicators are delayed (those are best-effort).
! Scale: 50 million daily users, 40 messages each per day: 2 billion messages a day, about 23,000 per second on average and perhaps 70,000 at peak.

! Example: what the NFRs imply for the chat design
! "Never lose an acknowledged message" means writing to durable, replicated storage before acknowledging the sender. "Same order for everyone" means a per-conversation sequence number assigned in one place (a partition per conversation). "Typing indicators are best-effort" means they can skip storage entirely and go over the live connection only. Three short sentences, three big design decisions.

## Common mistakes
- **Averages instead of percentiles.** Hides exactly the requests users complain about.
- **Global NFRs.** "The system should be strongly consistent." Which parts? Usually only a few operations need it.
- **No load attached.** A latency target without a request rate means nothing.
- **Everything is top priority.** If nothing is ranked, conflicts get resolved by accident.

> Takeaway: NFRs, not features, decide the architecture. Write them as numbers (operation, percentile, threshold, load), keep the list to 3 to 5, note read/write ratios and bursts, and agree which quality wins when they conflict.

@01.04
## There is no free choice
Every architectural decision buys something and pays for it somewhere else. A cache buys read speed and pays with staleness and a second copy to keep in sync. A queue buys resilience and pays with delay and harder debugging. Replicas buy availability and pay with money and with copies that can disagree. There is no option that is best on every axis, which is why "it depends" is the honest answer to most design questions.

Interviewers and reviewers care less about which option you pick than about whether you can name what it costs and why that cost is acceptable here. A useful sentence pattern is: **"We chose X because requirement Y matters more here than Z, and we accept cost C."** For example: "We'll serve like counts from a cache refreshed every few seconds, because read latency at 200,000 requests per second matters more than exact counts, and we accept that a count can be a few seconds behind."

This lesson covers the trade-offs you will meet in almost every design: performance against scalability, latency against throughput, consistency against availability, read speed against write speed, and simplicity against flexibility. It finishes with the question you will answer over and over: when is stale data acceptable?

## Performance vs scalability
These two words get used as synonyms, but they describe different problems with different fixes.
- A **performance** problem: the system is slow even for one user. One request takes 2 seconds on an idle server.
- A **scalability** problem: the system is fast for one user but slows down as load grows. One request takes 50 ms at 10 requests per second and 2 seconds at 10,000.

A system is **scalable** if adding resources increases capacity roughly in proportion. Double the servers, double the requests per second you can serve at the same latency. If doubling the servers gives you only 10% more capacity, something is shared and is limiting you.

%%compare Two different problems with the same symptom. A performance problem is slow with one user; a scalability problem is fast with one user and slow under load.
[Performance problem]
1 user -(request)-> Server -> 2 s response
Cause: slow code or query
Fix: index, algorithm, profiling
- More servers do not help
[Scalability problem]
1 user -(request)-> Server -> 50 ms
10k users -(same request)-> Server -> 2 s
Cause: contention, shared state
Fix: remove the bottleneck, partition
- Faster code helps only a little
%%

The fixes differ because the causes differ:
- **Performance** problems usually live in code, queries or algorithms: a missing index that turns a lookup into a full table scan, an O(n²) loop, a request that makes 40 database calls instead of one. You find them with a profiler or a query plan, on one request.
- **Scalability** problems usually live in something that is shared: a single database primary every write must go through, a lock that every request takes, a counter row that every transaction updates, a single-threaded component. You find them by load testing and watching which resource saturates first.

Telling them apart saves a lot of wasted effort. Adding ten more web servers to fix a query that scans 50 million rows does nothing, because each server runs the same slow query. Rewriting a query to be 20% faster does little if the real problem is that every request waits for the same row lock.

! Example: diagnosing a slow checkout
! At 3 a.m., with 5 users online, checkout takes 1.8 s. At noon, with 4,000 users, it also takes about 1.8 s. The latency does not depend on load, so this is a performance problem. The trace shows 1.6 s inside one query that filters orders by customer without an index. Adding the index brings checkout to 120 ms at any load.
! A different service: at 3 a.m. a search takes 60 ms; at noon it takes 900 ms, and the database CPU sits at 98%. Latency rises with load, so this is a scalability problem. The fix is more read capacity (replicas or a cache), not a faster query.

## Latency vs throughput
**Latency** is how long one operation takes, measured from the caller's point of view: 40 ms to load a profile. **Throughput** is how many operations complete per unit of time: 12,000 profile loads per second. The goal is usually maximal throughput with acceptable latency.

They are related through a simple law. **Little's Law** says that the average number of requests in the system equals the arrival rate multiplied by the average time each request spends in it: `L = λ × W`. A service handling 1,000 requests per second with 50 ms latency has, on average, 1,000 × 0.05 = 50 requests in flight. If each in-flight request needs a thread, you need about 50 threads. If latency rises to 500 ms, you need 500. This one formula explains why slow dependencies exhaust thread pools (lesson 02.11 uses it again).

The two often trade against each other:
- **Batching** raises throughput by paying a fixed cost (a network round trip, a disk flush) once for many items. But the first item in the batch waits for the last. Lesson 02.10 covers this.
- **Queueing** smooths load so a worker pool can run at high utilisation, but every item now waits in line.
- **Running hot** (high utilisation) gives the most throughput per server, but queueing theory says latency rises steeply as utilisation approaches 100%. A server at 50% busy has short queues; at 95% busy, queues grow very long. That is why capacity plans leave headroom.

%%bars Average time a request waits in a single queue as server utilisation rises, relative to the service time. Latency stays low until the server is busy, then explodes.
50% busy | 1 | 1x service time
70% busy | 2.3 | 2.3x
80% busy | 4 | 4x
90% busy | 9 | 9x
*95% busy | 19 | 19x
99% busy | 99 | 99x
%%

These numbers come from the simplest queueing model (one server, random arrivals), where the average wait is `utilisation / (1 − utilisation)` times the service time. Real systems differ in detail but show the same hockey-stick shape. The practical rule: plan to run shared resources at 50 to 70% of their capacity at peak, not 95%.

## Consistency vs availability
When data is copied across machines (replicas, caches, regions), the copies can disagree for a while. You must decide what readers see during that time. There are three common consistency patterns:
- **Weak consistency**: after a write, reads may or may not see it, with no promise. Memcached used as a best-effort cache, VoIP and live games work this way: if you miss an update, you just get the next one.
- **Eventual consistency**: if writes stop, all copies converge to the same value, usually within milliseconds to seconds. DNS and email work this way. It suits highly available systems because each copy can answer on its own.
- **Strong consistency**: once a write is acknowledged, every read sees it. Relational databases on a single primary and file systems on one machine work this way. You need it when correctness depends on it, such as money or unique usernames.

The trade-off becomes sharp during a **network partition**: a failure where some machines cannot talk to others, though each is still running. A replica cut off from the primary has two choices when a request arrives. It can answer with what it has (available, but possibly stale), or refuse until it can reach the primary again (consistent, but unavailable for that request).

%%seq A partition forces a choice. Replica B can no longer hear from the primary, so it either answers with what it has or refuses.
Client 1 > Primary : set price = 12
Primary > Replica B : replicate (lost, link down)
Client 2 > Replica B : get price
Replica B -> Client 2 : option A: 10 (stale but available)
Replica B -> Client 2 : option B: error (consistent, unavailable)
%%

Amazon often chose availability. Its shopping cart is always writable: if two copies accept changes during a partition, the cart merges them afterwards, so a deleted item can occasionally reappear. That is a deliberate trade: a slightly wrong cart costs little, while a cart that refuses "add to basket" costs sales.

### Quorums: tuning the dial
Many replicated stores expose three numbers that let you choose a point between the extremes:
- **N**: how many replicas hold each item (often 3).
- **W**: how many replicas must confirm a write before it counts as successful.
- **R**: how many replicas a read consults.

If **W + R > N**, every read set overlaps every write set in at least one replica, so a read will see the latest acknowledged write. With N = 3, choosing W = 2 and R = 2 gives that overlap and tolerates one replica being down. Choosing W = 1 and R = 1 makes reads and writes fast and highly available, but a read can easily miss a recent write. Module 6 covers quorums and their edge cases in depth.

! Example: what W and R buy you
! N = 3 replicas, each answering in 2 ms normally, but one is slow at 80 ms at any given time.
! W = 3, R = 1: every write waits for the slow replica, so writes take 80 ms. Reads take 2 ms and always see the latest write. If any replica is down, writes fail.
! W = 2, R = 2: writes and reads each wait for the two fastest, about 2 ms. Reads still overlap writes, and one replica can be down. This is the common default.
! W = 1, R = 1: everything is fast and survives two replicas down, but a read right after a write has about a two-in-three chance of hitting a replica that has not received it yet.

## Other classic trade-offs
- **Read speed vs write speed**: indexes, denormalisation, materialised views and caches all make reads faster by doing work at write time. Each index on a table is another structure to update on every insert. A table with eight indexes can make writes several times slower than the bare table.
- **Space vs time**: precomputing (a feed built ahead of time, a cached page) spends storage to save computation at read time. Computing on demand saves storage and costs latency.
- **Simplicity vs flexibility**: a monolith is simpler to build, test and debug. Microservices let many teams deploy independently, at the price of network calls and distributed data (lesson 01.09).
- **Cost vs everything**: more replicas, more regions and more headroom all improve availability and latency, and all cost money every month, even when idle (lesson 01.12).

Technique | Read latency | Write latency | Freshness | Cost
Cache in front of DB | yes, faster | same | no, can be stale | partial, extra servers
Extra index | yes, faster | no, slower | yes, exact | partial, more disk
Async replicas | yes, more capacity | yes, unaffected | no, lag | no, more servers
Sync replicas | partial | no, slower | yes, exact | no, more servers
Batching writes | same | no, items wait | partial | yes, fewer calls
%%

## When is stale data fine?
This is the question you will answer per feature, not per system. One system often has both kinds of data side by side.

Many web applications only need **user-perceived consistency**: the inconsistency window just has to be shorter than the time before the user would notice. If a replica is 500 ms behind, and a user takes 3 seconds to click to the next page, nobody notices. The one case users always notice is their own writes: post a comment, refresh, and it's gone. So a common pattern is **read-your-writes**: route a user's reads to the primary for a few seconds after they write, or remember the version they wrote and wait for a replica to catch up to it.

Data that tolerates seconds (or minutes) of staleness:
- Like counts, view counts, follower counts.
- Recommendations, "people you may know", trending lists.
- Search indexes (a new product appearing in search 30 seconds late is fine).
- Analytics dashboards.

Data that usually does not:
- Account balances before a withdrawal or transfer.
- Inventory or seat availability at the moment of purchase.
- Permission changes: after you remove someone's access, a stale cache that still lets them in is a security bug.
- Uniqueness checks, like "is this username taken?".

%%timeline Replication lag in action: a write lands on the primary, and a reader on a lagging replica sees the old value until the change arrives.
[Primary] 0 ms | balance set to 0
[Replica] 0 ms | still shows 100
[Reader] 120 ms | *reads 100 from replica
[Replica] 300 ms | change applied, shows 0
[Reader] 400 ms | reads 0
%%

The test to apply: **what is the worst thing that happens if a user acts on a value that is a few seconds old, and how much does it cost?** A like count that shows 1,203 instead of 1,205 costs nothing. A balance that shows $100 when it is $0 can let someone withdraw money twice. A ticket that looks available when it was sold a second ago produces a double booking and a refund. Even in those cases you can often keep the fast, stale read for display and do a strongly consistent check at the moment of commitment: show "3 seats left" from a cache, but decrement the count inside a database transaction when the user pays.

! Example: one product, two consistency choices
! An events site shows "1,240 people interested" on each concert page. It reads this from a cache refreshed every 30 seconds. At 50,000 page views per second, the cache serves almost all of them from RAM, and a 30-second-old number harms nobody.
! The same page sells tickets. The "Buy" button calls a service that runs `UPDATE seats SET held_by = ? WHERE seat_id = ? AND held_by IS NULL` in the primary database. If two users try the same seat at once, exactly one update affects a row; the other user is told the seat is gone. Display is stale and cheap; commitment is consistent and exact.

## Common mistakes
- **Not naming the cost.** "We'll add a cache" without saying what goes stale and for how long.
- **One consistency model for everything.** Making like counts strongly consistent wastes money; making balances eventually consistent loses it.
- **Confusing performance and scalability.** Throwing servers at a slow query, or optimising code when a shared lock is the bottleneck.
- **Designing for 95% utilisation.** Queues explode near full load; leave headroom.
- **Reading "eventual" as "a few milliseconds".** Under load or during failures, lag can grow to minutes. Know what happens then.

## In an interview
State the trade-off out loud every time you make a choice, and tie it to a requirement from the start of the session. Good questions to ask: "How stale can this be before users or the business notice?", "Is it worse to show an error or a slightly old value here?", "What does a double booking or a double charge cost us?" When you separate a feature's display path (fast, stale) from its commit path (exact), you show that you understand the trade-off rather than picking one side for the whole system.

> Takeaway: every choice buys one property with another, so always name the cost. Separate performance from scalability and latency from throughput, and decide per feature how stale data may be, often with a fast stale read for display and a strict check at the moment of commitment.

?? Accept stale data for a post's like count or a "people you may know" list: being a few seconds behind harms nobody and lets you serve it cheaply from a cache or replica. Don't accept it when checking a bank balance before a withdrawal or seat availability at the moment of booking, because acting on an old value causes overdrafts or double bookings; there you read from the primary or check inside a transaction.

## Four properties that pull against each other
This lesson covers the four qualities you will trade most often: **availability** (does it answer?), **durability** (is my data still there?), **consistency** (is the answer up to date?) and **cost** (what does all this cost each month?). They pull against each other. More replicas improve availability and durability but cost money and create copies that can disagree. Stronger consistency means replicas must coordinate, which adds latency and can make the system refuse requests during failures.

You need to be able to put a number on each one, compute how components combine, and explain what you would give up to get more of one.

## Availability and the "nines"
**Availability** is the fraction of time, or of requests, for which a service works correctly. It is usually described in "nines": 99.9% is "three nines". The useful habit is to convert each target into allowed downtime, because "99.99%" sounds abstract while "4 minutes a month" is something a team can plan around.

The arithmetic: a 30-day month has 30 × 24 × 60 = 43,200 minutes. At 99.9%, 0.1% of that is 43.2 minutes. An average calendar month is slightly longer (30.44 days), which gives 43.8 minutes. A year has about 525,600 minutes, so 99.9% allows about 526 minutes, or 8.8 hours.

| Target | Downtime per month | Downtime per year |
| 99% | 7.3 hours | 3.65 days |
| 99.9% | 43.8 minutes | 8.8 hours |
| 99.95% | 21.9 minutes | 4.4 hours |
| 99.99% | 4.4 minutes | 52.6 minutes |
| 99.999% | 26 seconds | 5.3 minutes |

%%bars Allowed downtime per year at each availability target. Each extra nine cuts the budget by ten times.
99% | 5256 | 3.65 days
99.9% | 526 | 8.8 hours
99.95% | 263 | 4.4 hours
99.99% | 52.6 | 52.6 minutes
*99.999% | 5.3 | 5.3 minutes
%%

Think about what each level means for operations. At 99.9%, one bad deploy that takes 30 minutes to roll back uses most of a month's budget. At 99.99%, you cannot afford a human to be paged, wake up, log in and diagnose: 4 minutes is gone before they open a laptop. That level needs automatic failover and automatic rollback. At 99.999%, even a fast automatic failover that takes 30 seconds, happening once a month, exceeds the budget.

### Time-based vs request-based
Time-based availability ("the service was up 99.9% of the minutes") is easy to understand but crude: a 10-minute outage at 3 a.m. and one at the Black Friday peak count the same. For large services it is better to measure **aggregate availability**: the share of requests that succeed. A service handling 2.5 million requests a day at 99.99% may fail 250 of them. A partial outage where 5% of requests fail for an hour then counts as 5% of an hour, not a full hour.

### MTBF and MTTR
Availability can also be written as `MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR)`: **mean time between failures** divided by the total of that plus **mean time to repair**. This form shows the two levers. You can fail less often, or recover faster. A server that fails once a month (MTBF ≈ 720 hours) and takes 1 hour to replace has availability 720 / 721 ≈ 99.86%. Cut repair to 6 minutes (0.1 hours) with automatic failover and it becomes 720 / 720.1 ≈ 99.986%, without the hardware getting any better. In practice, fast detection and recovery are usually cheaper than preventing failures.

## Availability composes
Real requests pass through several components. How their availabilities combine depends on whether they are in sequence or in parallel.

**In sequence** (a request needs every component), the availabilities multiply. If a request goes load balancer → API → database and each is 99.9%, the total is 0.999 × 0.999 × 0.999 ≈ 0.997, or 99.7%. That is about 2.2 hours a month instead of 43 minutes. Every synchronous hop eats into the budget, which is why long chains of service calls hurt.

**In parallel** (redundant copies, where any one is enough), the chance that *all* copies are down multiplies. Two 99.9% replicas are both down with probability 0.001 × 0.001 = 0.000001, so availability is 99.9999%. This assumes the failures are **independent**. Two replicas in the same rack, on the same power feed, running the same buggy software version, are not independent: one bad deploy can take them both down at once. Real redundancy means different racks, zones or regions, and staggered deploys.

%%widget nines Components in series multiply their availability, so every extra hop adds downtime. Redundant copies win most of it back.
%%

! Example: availability of a checkout path
! A checkout request goes through a load balancer (99.99%), the checkout service (99.95%), the payment service (99.9%) and a database (99.95%), all synchronously.
! Series: 0.9999 × 0.9995 × 0.999 × 0.9995 ≈ 0.9979, about 99.79%. That allows roughly 91 minutes of failures a month, worse than any single component.
! Now run the payment service as two independent copies in different zones, so the request succeeds if either is up. That part becomes 1 − 0.001² = 0.999999. The chain becomes 0.9999 × 0.9995 × 0.999999 × 0.9995 ≈ 0.9989, about 99.89%, cutting expected downtime from about 91 to about 48 minutes a month.
! The weakest links are now the checkout service and database at 99.95%. Making them redundant too is the next step, and each step costs more servers.

## Why not 100%?
100% is almost never the right target:
- **Each extra nine costs far more than the last.** Going from 99% to 99.9% might need a second server and a load balancer. Going from 99.99% to 99.999% might need multiple regions, automated failover tested monthly, and a team dedicated to reliability.
- **Users can't tell beyond a point.** Their home Wi-Fi, mobile network and phone already fail somewhere between 0.01% and 1% of the time. A 99.999% service behind a 99% network looks like a 99% service.
- **Reliability work competes with features.** Engineers improving the fifth nine are not building the product.
- **It is an economic decision.** If moving a $1M-a-year service from 99.9% to 99.99% recovers 0.09% of revenue, about $900, it is only worth doing if it costs less than $900 a year. It almost certainly costs more.

## SLIs, SLOs, SLAs and error budgets
These terms describe how you measure and promise availability (and latency):
- **SLI (service level indicator)**: what you measure. "The share of HTTP requests that return a non-5xx response in under 300 ms."
- **SLO (service level objective)**: the target for that SLI, over a window. "99.9% over a rolling 30 days."
- **SLA (service level agreement)**: a contract with customers that has consequences, usually service credits, if you miss it. Set it looser than the SLO (for example SLA 99.5%, SLO 99.9%), so you notice and react before you owe money.

%%layers From measurement to promise: each layer builds on the one below.
SLA | contract with customers, with penalties, e.g. 99.5%
*SLO | internal target, e.g. 99.9% of requests OK over 30 days
SLI | the measurement, e.g. share of requests OK and under 300 ms
Error budget | the 0.1% that may fail; spend it on releases and experiments
%%

The **error budget** is 100% minus the SLO. With a 99.9% SLO and 100 million requests a month, 100,000 requests may fail. While budget remains, teams ship freely, run experiments and take risks. When it is spent, releases slow down or stop and effort goes into reliability until the window rolls forward. This turns the eternal argument between "ship faster" and "be more stable" into a shared number nobody has to win by shouting.

Overachieving is a risk too. Google deliberately took its lock service Chubby down for planned outages when it had been "too reliable", because other teams had started building as if it never failed. When it eventually did fail, their systems broke. A planned outage forced those hidden assumptions out early.

! Example: spending an error budget
! SLO: 99.9% of requests succeed over 30 days. The service handles 50 million requests a month, so the budget is 50,000 failed requests.
! Day 6: a bad config push fails 20% of requests for 15 minutes. At about 1,160 requests per minute, that is 0.2 × 1,160 × 15 ≈ 3,500 failures, 7% of the budget.
! Day 19: a database failover takes 4 minutes with all requests failing: about 4,600 failures. Total spent so far: about 8,100, or 16%.
! The team has plenty of budget left, so a risky schema migration goes ahead. Had a big outage on day 20 consumed 45,000 failures, the policy would freeze feature launches for the rest of the window.

## Durability
**Durability** is the probability that data you acknowledged is still there, uncorrupted, later. It is a different property from availability. A system can be down without losing data (the database is unreachable, but every byte is safe on disk). It can also be up while silently losing writes (it acknowledged a write, then a crash lost it before it reached disk).

Durability comes from three things:
- **Replication across independent failures**: copies on different disks, machines, zones or regions. A single disk has an annual failure rate of roughly 1 to 2%. Three copies on independent disks, with failed copies replaced within hours, make losing all three extremely unlikely.
- **Writing to stable storage before acknowledging**: a database that replies "saved" before calling `fsync` (forcing data out of OS buffers to the disk) can lose that write in a power cut. Many databases let you choose: acknowledge after the local disk, after one replica, or after a quorum.
- **Backups and checksums**: replication copies mistakes too. If someone runs `DELETE FROM orders` by accident, every replica obediently deletes. Point-in-time backups protect against that. Checksums detect **bit rot**, where stored bits change silently over time.

Object stores advertise very high durability: Amazon S3 is designed for 99.999999999% ("eleven nines") durability per object per year. In practical terms, if you store 10 million objects, you would expect to lose one about every 10,000 years. That number covers hardware failure, not you deleting your own data, so backups and versioning still matter.

%%matrix Which protection survives which failure. Replication alone does not protect against human error or bad software.
Protection | Disk dies | Zone outage | Accidental delete | Silent corruption
Single disk | no | no | no | no
3 replicas, one zone | yes | no | no | partial, if checksummed
3 replicas, 3 zones | yes | yes | no | partial, if checksummed
Point-in-time backups | yes | yes | yes | partial, if verified
Versioned object store | yes | yes | yes | yes, checksums built in
%%

## Consistency, carefully
The **CAP theorem** says that during a network partition, a system must choose between consistency and availability. It is often quoted, and often misused, because its definitions are very narrow:
- "Consistency" in CAP means **linearisability**: the system behaves as if there were one copy of the data, and every read sees the latest completed write.
- "Availability" in CAP means **every non-failed node** must answer every request, even nodes cut off from the rest.

Under those definitions most real databases are neither "CP" nor "AP". A single-leader replicated database (one primary, async replicas) refuses writes on nodes that cannot reach the primary, so it is not CAP-available. Its async replicas can serve stale reads, so it is not linearisable either. Labelling it "CP" or "AP" tells you very little.

It is more useful to describe the actual guarantees:
- Can a user always **read their own writes**?
- Are reads **linearisable**, or can they be stale, and by how much?
- What happens during **failover**: can acknowledged writes be lost?
- Even without partitions, what latency do you pay for consistency? Synchronous replication to another region adds a cross-region round trip (often 50 to 150 ms) to every write.

Lessons in Module 6 cover these guarantees in detail.

## Cost
Each of the above costs money: replicas, cross-region links and data transfer, standby capacity that sits idle until a failover, backups, and the on-call time of engineers. A rough guide: going from one server to two in one zone roughly doubles compute cost; going multi-zone adds cross-zone transfer charges; going multi-region roughly doubles the whole stack again and adds replication traffic.

The right answer is the **cheapest design that meets the agreed SLOs**, not the most reliable one you can imagine. If the requirement is 99.9%, a well-run single-region, multi-zone deployment is usually enough. Proposing active-active across three regions for a 99.9% internal tool is a design mistake, not extra credit.

## Common mistakes
- **Quoting nines without converting them.** Always translate to minutes per month.
- **Forgetting series composition.** Five 99.9% services in a chain are not 99.9%.
- **Assuming independent failures.** Replicas in one zone, or with one shared config, fail together.
- **Confusing availability and durability.** Replicas do not replace backups.
- **Using CAP labels as a design.** Say what readers see and what happens on failover instead.

## In an interview
Ask for the availability target early, convert it to downtime, and use it to justify redundancy: "99.99% gives us about 4 minutes a month, so failover must be automatic." Mention what the SLI is (success rate, latency), and say what is redundant and across what failure domain (zone or region). For data, say how many copies, where, when a write is acknowledged, and how you recover from mistakes.

> Takeaway: set availability from user and business needs, convert it to downtime, measure it as an SLO with an error budget, and reach it with independent redundancy and fast recovery. Keep durability separate (replicas plus backups), and describe consistency by concrete guarantees instead of CAP labels.

?? 99.9% allows about 43 minutes of downtime a month (0.1% of 43,200 minutes is 43.2 minutes in a 30-day month; about 43.8 in an average month). 99.99% allows about 4.4 minutes a month, ten times less, which is short enough that failover and rollback must be automatic.

## Why estimate at all
Before you choose a database, a cache or a sharding scheme, you need to know roughly how big the problem is. **Back-of-the-envelope estimates** are quick thought experiments, using a handful of standard numbers, that tell you which designs are even possible. Does the data fit on one machine? Can one database take the write rate? Is the bandwidth bill $100 a month or $100,000?

The goal is to get the **order of magnitude** right, not the exact figure. Whether a number is 10³ or 10⁵ changes the design completely: 1,000 writes per second fits on one database; 100,000 does not. Whether it is 3 × 10⁴ or 4 × 10⁴ changes nothing. So you round hard, keep the arithmetic simple, and spend your effort on the assumptions.

## Numbers to keep in your head
### Powers of two and data sizes
| Power | Approximate value | Name |
| 2^10 | a thousand (10³) | 1 KB |
| 2^20 | a million (10⁶) | 1 MB |
| 2^30 | a billion (10⁹) | 1 GB |
| 2^40 | a trillion (10¹²) | 1 TB |
| 2^50 | a quadrillion (10¹⁵) | 1 PB |

Handy object sizes: a character of ASCII text is 1 byte (UTF-8 for other languages is 2 to 4); a 64-bit integer or timestamp is 8 bytes; a UUID is 16 bytes; a short text post with metadata is about 0.5 to 1 KB; a compressed phone photo is 1 to 3 MB; a minute of 1080p video is roughly 50 to 100 MB.

### Time
One day is 86,400 seconds. Call it **10⁵** for estimates (it is off by about 15%, which is fine). Useful consequences:
- 1 million events a day ≈ 12 per second.
- 100 million a day ≈ 1,200 per second.
- 1 billion a day ≈ 12,000 per second.

A month is about 2.6 million seconds (2.5 × 10⁶), and a year about 31.5 million (3 × 10⁷).

### The latency ladder
Rounded times for common operations:

| Operation | Rough time |
| L1 cache reference | 1 ns |
| Main memory reference | 100 ns |
| System call | 100 ns to 1 µs |
| Context switch | 1 to 5 µs |

| Read 1 MB sequentially from RAM | 10 to 50 µs |
| SSD random read (4 to 8 KB) | 100 µs |
| Same-zone network round trip | 100 to 500 µs |
| Simple key lookup in Redis or indexed query in MySQL | 0.5 to 1 ms |
| Read 1 MB sequentially from SSD | 0.5 ms |

| Cross-Atlantic round trip | 80 to 150 ms |

%%widget latency The latency ladder on a log scale. Each step down is roughly ten to a thousand times slower.
%%

The lessons hidden in the ladder: memory is about 1,000 times faster than an SSD random read, and an SSD is about 100 times faster than a spinning disk seek. A network round trip inside a data centre costs as much as thousands of memory reads, so making 50 sequential calls to a cache is slow even though each call is "fast". Crossing an ocean costs about 100 ms per round trip, a limit set by the speed of light in fibre, so no amount of engineering removes it.

### Throughput
Rough rates for one machine or one device:

%%bars Sequential throughput of common resources on one machine, in MB per second. Random small reads on a spinning disk are a hundred times slower than sequential ones.
Memory (one thread) | 10000 | ~10 GB/s
NVMe SSD sequential | 3000 | ~3 GB/s
10 Gbit network | 1200 | ~1.2 GB/s
HDD sequential | 200 | ~200 MB/s
*HDD random 8 KB | 2 | ~2 MB/s

The conclusions: keep hot data in memory, avoid random disk seeks, prefer sequential writes (logs, append-only files), and compress before sending data over the network, because a fast compressor runs at hundreds of MB per second while the network moves about 1 GB per second and costs money.

### What one box can do
Some rough capacities to sanity-check designs against:
- One web or API server: 1,000 to 10,000 simple requests per second, depending heavily on the work per request.
- One Postgres or MySQL primary on good hardware: a few thousand to tens of thousands of simple writes per second; more reads, especially with indexes cached in memory.
- One Redis instance: around 100,000 simple operations per second.
- One Kafka broker: hundreds of MB per second of appends.
- One large server: 64 to 192 cores and 0.5 to 2 TB of RAM are ordinary today.

## The method
Estimates go wrong in predictable ways, so follow a few rules:
- **Fermi decomposition**: break the question into quantities you can guess (users, actions per user, bytes per action), then multiply. Named after a physicist famous for estimating things like the number of piano tuners in Chicago from first principles.
- **Keep units** on every number. "150 million users × 2 tweets per user per day = 300 million tweets per day." If the units don't cancel to what you want, something is wrong.
- **Use exponents**: 1.5 × 10⁸ × 2 × 10³ = 3 × 10¹¹ is much easier than multiplying long numbers.
- **Write each assumption down**, and keep to about six. Too many and the errors compound; too few and you've missed something.
- **Round aggressively**: 99,987 / 9.1 becomes 100,000 / 10 = 10,000.
- **Separate average from peak**. Traffic is not flat over a day. A peak of 2 to 3 times the average is a common assumption; event-driven products (ticket sales, sports) can see 10 to 100 times.
- **Remember the multipliers**: replication (usually 3 copies), indexes and metadata (often 20 to 50% on top of raw data), and growth.

## Worked examples
! Example 1: Twitter-scale writes and media
! Assumptions: 300M monthly users, 50% daily active, 2 tweets per active user per day, 10% of tweets carry 1 MB of media, keep data 5 years.
! Daily active users = 300M × 50% = 150M.
! Tweets per day = 150M × 2 = 3 × 10⁸. Average write QPS = 3 × 10⁸ / 86,400 ≈ 3,500 per second. Peak at 2x ≈ 7,000 per second.
! Media per day = 3 × 10⁸ × 10% × 1 MB = 3 × 10⁷ MB = 30 TB per day.
! Five years ≈ 30 TB × 365 × 5 ≈ 55 PB, before replication. Tweet text at 1 KB each is only 0.3 TB a day, tiny by comparison. Media dominates storage completely.

! Example 2: the photo app from the tree above
! Photos per day = 50M users × 0.2 = 10⁷.
! Bytes per photo ≈ 2 MB + 0.1 MB ≈ 2.1 MB, call it 2 MB.
! Per day = 10⁷ × 2 MB = 2 × 10⁷ MB = 20 TB. Per year = 20 TB × 365 ≈ 7.3 PB, and with 3 copies about 22 PB.
! Upload bandwidth = 20 TB per day / 10⁵ s ≈ 200 MB/s on average, perhaps 600 MB/s at peak. That is a handful of machines' worth of network, so ingest is not the hard part; storage cost and serving reads are.

! Example 3: how many servers for the API?
! The app has 50M daily users who each load the feed 20 times a day: 10⁹ feed requests per day, about 12,000 per second on average and say 36,000 at a 3x peak.
! If one API server handles 2,000 feed requests per second at acceptable latency, you need 36,000 / 2,000 = 18 servers at peak. Add headroom (run at about 60% so a zone can fail) and you plan for about 30 servers.
! That is a modest fleet. The interesting question is the data behind it: 36,000 feed reads per second is far beyond one database, so this number argues for caching or precomputed feeds.

%%flow The estimation chain for traffic: users to requests per day, divide by seconds in a day, then apply the peak factor.
Daily active users -(x actions per day)-> Requests per day -(/ 100,000 s)-> *Average QPS -(x 2 to 3)-> Peak QPS -(/ per-server capacity)-> Servers
%%

## Turn numbers into decisions
The estimate is only useful if you say what it implies. Always finish with "so this means we need...":
- **7,000 writes per second** is too many for one relational primary to take comfortably with headroom, so you need batching, sharding, or a write-optimised store.
- **55 PB of media** means object storage, not a database. Databases store pointers to media, never the bytes themselves.
- **A 20 GB working set** fits in memory on one machine. You might not need a cache cluster at all; one Redis node, or the database's own buffer cache, will do.
- **36,000 reads per second for feeds** suggests precomputation or caching, because assembling a feed from scratch for every request would mean hundreds of thousands of database queries per second.
- **Bandwidth**: 1 GB per second of egress is about 2.6 PB a month. At roughly $0.05 to $0.10 per GB to the internet, that is $130,000 to $260,000 a month, which is why media-heavy products use CDNs and negotiate prices.

Cost is part of napkin maths too. Rough cloud prices to keep in mind: about $25 per vCPU per month, $2.50 per GB of RAM per month, $0.02 per GB per month of object storage, $0.10 per GB of SSD block storage per month, and $0.05 to $0.10 per GB of internet egress. A cloud object store includes its own replication in the price, so for Example 2 you pay for the 7.3 PB a year of photos: about 7.3 × 10⁶ GB × $0.02 ≈ $146,000 a month after the first year, growing by that much again every year. That is why such companies move older photos to cheaper tiers (lesson 01.12).

## Common mistakes
- **Forgetting the peak.** Designing for the average means falling over every evening.
- **Forgetting replication and overhead.** Raw data × 3 copies × 1.3 for indexes is four times the raw number.
- **Mixing bits and bytes.** Network speeds are quoted in bits: 10 Gbit/s is about 1.25 GB/s.
- **False precision.** Writing "3,472 QPS" suggests a confidence you don't have. Say "about 3,500, call it 4k".
- **Stopping at the number.** An estimate with no conclusion is wasted time.
- **Spending ten minutes on it.** In an interview, estimates should take two or three minutes, and only for numbers that change the design.

## In an interview
Ask the interviewer for the numbers they care about (daily users, actions per user, data retention) or propose them and ask if they are reasonable. Write assumptions on the board in a column, do the arithmetic visibly with rounded exponents, and box the conclusion. Many interviewers care less about the precise figures than about seeing you use them: "Peak writes are about 25,000 per second, more than one primary can take, so we'll partition writes by user ID."

> Takeaway: aim for the right power of ten, write every assumption down with its units, separate average from peak, add the multipliers for replication and overhead, and always finish with "so this means we need...".

?? Writes per day = 100M users × 10 = 10⁹. Average write QPS = 10⁹ / ~10⁵ s ≈ 10,000 to 12,000 per second, so plan for about 20,000 to 30,000 at a 2 to 3x peak. Storage = 10⁹ × 1 KB = 1 TB per day, about 365 TB per year of raw data, or roughly 1 PB a year with 3 replicas. ## The problem
A web server spends most of its life waiting. It waits for the client to finish sending a request over a slow mobile link. It waits for the database to answer. It waits for a disk read, for a downstream service, for the client to read the response. The CPU work per request is often tiny: parsing a few headers, running some business logic, serialising JSON, perhaps 200 µs in total. The waiting can be 100 ms or more.

So the real question is not "how do we compute faster?" but **"how do we keep thousands of mostly idle connections open without wasting resources on them?"** The answer shapes how NGINX, Node.js, Redis, Go and Java servers are built, and it explains limits you will hit in practice: thread pools running dry, one slow call freezing a whole server, memory blowing up at 10,000 connections.

## Concurrency is not parallelism
Two ideas that people blur:
- **Concurrency** is about structure: composing a program out of independently executing pieces, so that while one piece waits, another can make progress. "Dealing with lots of things at once."
- **Parallelism** is about execution: running computations at the same instant on multiple CPU cores. "Doing lots of things at once."

A well-known illustration uses gophers moving a pile of old manuals to an incinerator. Adding more gophers doesn't help on its own; they need carts, and they collide at the pile. Splitting the job into independent steps (load the cart, move it, unload it, bring the empty cart back) gives a concurrent design. Once the structure is right, running more gophers in parallel "falls out" naturally. A concurrent design can run on one core and still be correct; it just becomes easy to parallelise when more cores are available.

%%timeline Concurrency on one core interleaves two tasks while each waits; parallelism on two cores runs them at the same moment.
[1 core, concurrent] 0 ms | A computes
[1 core, concurrent] 1 ms | A waits on DB, B computes
[1 core, concurrent] 2 ms | B waits, A resumes
[Core 1, parallel] 0 ms | *A computes
[Core 2, parallel] 0 ms | *B computes
[Core 1, parallel] 1 ms | A waits on DB
[Core 2, parallel] 1 ms | B waits on DB
%%

For servers, concurrency is the important part. Even a single core can serve thousands of clients if it never sits idle while one of them is waiting. Parallelism then multiplies that by the number of cores.

## What a connection actually costs
To compare designs, look at what the operating system keeps for each open connection:
- A **socket** with a **file descriptor** (a small integer the process uses to refer to it) and kernel buffers for incoming and outgoing data, typically a few KB to tens of KB.
- Whatever state the application keeps: the half-parsed request, the user's session, a buffer for the response.

That is cheap. A machine with 16 GB of RAM can hold hundreds of thousands of idle TCP connections. The expensive part is whatever the **concurrency model** adds on top.

## Model 1: a thread (or process) per connection
The classic approach (Apache's prefork and worker modes, traditional Java servlet containers, many Python and Ruby servers) gives each connection its own thread or process. That thread reads the request, calls the database and waits, writes the response, and blocks whenever it has to wait. The code reads top to bottom like a simple script, which makes it easy to write, debug and reason about.

The costs appear with scale:
- **Memory per thread.** Each thread has its own stack. On Linux the default reservation is often 8 MB of virtual memory, and the JVM reserves about 1 MB per thread by default. The physical memory actually touched is smaller, but 10,000 threads still commonly use several GB.
- **Context switches.** When a thread blocks, the kernel saves its registers and loads another thread. A switch costs roughly 1 to 5 µs directly, and more in practice because the new thread finds the CPU caches full of the old thread's data. With thousands of runnable threads, a meaningful share of CPU time goes on switching rather than on work.
- **Scheduler and lock contention.** Thousands of threads competing for the same locks and run queues add overhead that grows with the count.
- **Hard limits.** Thread pools are usually capped (Tomcat's default is 200 threads). Once all threads are busy waiting, new connections queue or are refused, even though the CPU is nearly idle.

This limit was famously called the **C10K problem**: how to serve 10,000 concurrent clients on one machine. In the late 1990s, thread-per-connection servers could not do it comfortably. Event loops were the answer.

## Model 2: an event loop
The alternative is **non-blocking I/O** with an **event loop**. Sockets are put in non-blocking mode, so a read that has no data returns immediately with "would block" instead of putting the thread to sleep. One thread then asks the kernel, through an interface like **epoll** (Linux), **kqueue** (BSD and macOS) or **IOCP** (Windows), "which of these 10,000 sockets are ready to read or write?" The kernel returns only the ready ones. The thread handles each ready event quickly, a few microseconds of work, and loops back to ask again. It never sits waiting for a slow client.

The older `select` and `poll` interfaces did the same job, but they made the kernel scan the whole list of sockets on every call, which costs O(n) per wakeup. epoll keeps the interest list inside the kernel and returns only ready sockets, so the cost is proportional to activity, not to the number of idle connections. That difference is what makes 100,000 mostly idle connections cheap.

%%compare Thread per connection versus an event loop, for 10,000 connections that are idle most of the time.
[Thread per connection]
10k connections -(one each)-> 10k threads -> mostly blocked
+ Simple, linear code
+ One slow request blocks only its own thread
- GBs of stacks, many context switches
- Pool runs dry while the CPU idles
[*Event loop]
10k connections -(epoll)-> 1 thread per core -> only ready sockets
+ An idle connection costs only a few KB
+ Few context switches
- Any blocking call stalls everyone
- Code is split into callbacks or async steps
%%

### How NGINX is built
NGINX is the textbook example of this design:
- A **master process** reads the configuration, binds the listening ports (80, 443), and starts the workers. It does no request handling itself.
- It starts **one worker process per CPU core**. Each worker is single-threaded and runs its own event loop.
- Each worker keeps a small **state machine** per connection: where it is in reading the request, talking to the upstream server, sending the response, or waiting for the next request on a keep-alive connection. A worker can manage hundreds of thousands of these.
- Because there are only as many workers as cores, each worker usually has a core to itself, and context switches are rare.
- **Config reloads** have no downtime: the master starts new workers with the new configuration, stops sending them new connections to the old ones, and lets the old workers finish their in-flight connections before exiting. Even upgrading the NGINX binary works this way.

%%state The small state machine NGINX keeps for each client connection. A worker juggles many of these, advancing whichever one has a ready socket.
[*] -> Reading request
Reading request -> Reading request : more bytes arrive
Reading request -> Waiting on upstream : headers complete, proxy to app
Waiting on upstream -> Sending response : upstream replies
Sending response -> Sending response : socket writable again
Sending response -> *Keep-alive idle : response done
Keep-alive idle -> Reading request : next request
Keep-alive idle -> Closed : idle timeout
%%

Node.js and Redis follow the same pattern. Node runs your JavaScript on one event loop thread, built on a library called libuv. Redis executes all commands on one main thread, which is why each command is atomic and why one slow command (like `KEYS *` on a large database) blocks every other client.

! Example: memory for 50,000 WebSocket connections
! A chat service keeps 50,000 WebSocket connections open per server. Each user sends a message every minute or so, so at any moment almost every connection is idle.
! Thread per connection: 50,000 threads × even 256 KB of touched stack = about 12.8 GB, before any application data, plus constant scheduling of 50,000 threads. Most servers would fall over, and the default thread pool limits would refuse connections long before that.
! Event loop: each connection costs a socket (kernel buffers of roughly 4 to 16 KB in an idle state) plus perhaps 2 KB of application state. 50,000 × ~20 KB ≈ 1 GB. With 8 cores running 8 loops, each loop manages about 6,000 connections and is woken only for the roughly 800 messages per second the whole server receives.

## The catch: never block the loop
The event loop's efficiency comes with a golden rule: **don't block the event loop**. One thread serves everyone, so while it is doing any slow piece of work, no other connection on that loop makes progress. Common ways to break the rule:
- **CPU-heavy work whose cost grows with user input**: sorting a user-supplied list of a million items, resizing an image, rendering a large PDF.
- **Regular expressions with catastrophic backtracking (ReDoS)**: a pattern like `/(\/.+)+$/` can take exponential time on a crafted string, freezing the process for seconds or minutes.
- **Synchronous APIs** in a server: `fs.readFileSync`, `crypto.pbkdf2Sync`, or a database driver that blocks.
- **Huge JSON**: parsing or serialising a 50 MB string can take over a second, during which every other request waits.

%%seq One blocking call stalls every connection the loop serves.
Conn A > Event loop : request
Event loop > Event loop : sync DB call, 200 ms
Conn B > Event loop : request (waits)
Conn C > Event loop : request (waits)
Event loop -> Conn A : response
Event loop -> Conn B : served 200 ms late
Event loop -> Conn C : served 200 ms late
%%

The fixes:
- **Bound input sizes**: cap request bodies, list lengths and JSON payloads so no single request can demand unbounded work.
- **Partition** long work into small steps that yield back to the loop between them (for example, process 1,000 items, then schedule the next chunk with `setImmediate`).
- **Offload** it to a worker pool or a separate process. Node already offloads file system, DNS lookups and crypto to a libuv thread pool (4 threads by default), but long tasks can clog that pool too, so heavy work often goes to dedicated worker threads or another service.

! Example: one slow call, measured
! A Node.js API serves 2,000 requests per second, each needing 0.3 ms of CPU, so the loop is about 60% busy.
! A new endpoint calls `crypto.pbkdf2Sync` with 100,000 iterations, taking 80 ms of CPU. Ten such calls arrive per second.
! Now the loop needs 600 ms + 800 ms = 1.4 s of CPU per second of wall time. It cannot keep up: queues grow without bound and every endpoint's latency climbs into seconds, even the cheap ones.
! Switching to the async `crypto.pbkdf2`, which runs on the thread pool, puts the loop back at 60% busy. The password endpoint still takes 80 ms, but nothing else waits behind it.

## Modern hybrids
Writing everything as callbacks or async functions is a real cost, and many teams prefer code that looks like plain blocking code. Two popular runtimes give you that while keeping event-loop efficiency underneath:
- **Go's goroutines** start with a stack of about 2 KB that grows as needed, so a server can run hundreds of thousands of them. The Go runtime multiplexes goroutines onto a few OS threads (one per core by default). When a goroutine does a network read that would block, the runtime parks it, registers the socket with epoll, and runs another goroutine. You write `conn.Read(buf)`; the runtime does the event loop.
- **Java's virtual threads** (standard since Java 21) do the same on the JVM: millions of lightweight threads mounted on a small pool of carrier threads, unmounted whenever they block on I/O.

These models also run on all cores at once, so a CPU-heavy task only occupies one OS thread rather than freezing the whole server. You still need to bound concurrency: a million goroutines all querying the same database will overwhelm the database, not the runtime.

%%layers How a modern runtime serves many connections: cheap user-level tasks on top, a few OS threads in the middle, the kernel's readiness API at the bottom.
Your handlers | goroutines or virtual threads, written as blocking code
*Runtime scheduler | parks tasks that wait, runs ready ones
OS threads | about one per CPU core
Kernel | epoll or kqueue reports ready sockets
%%

## Choosing a model
- **I/O-heavy work with many connections** (proxies, API gateways, chat, WebSockets): event loops or goroutine-style runtimes.
- **CPU-heavy work** (image processing, ML inference): a pool of threads or processes sized to the core count, with a queue in front.
- **Moderate traffic, simple code wanted**: thread-per-request with a sensibly sized pool is fine. Thousands of requests per second is well within reach.

## Common mistakes
- **Blocking calls on an event loop**, often hidden in a library.
- **Huge thread pools** to "fix" slow dependencies: 2,000 threads waiting on a slow database just means 2,000 queries piling on it.
- **Confusing more threads with more speed.** If the CPU is the bottleneck, threads beyond the core count only add switching.
- **No timeout on downstream calls**, so waiting connections accumulate until memory runs out.

## In an interview
When asked how a server handles load, distinguish waiting from working. Say how many concurrent connections you expect, note that idle connections are cheap with an event loop or lightweight threads, and keep CPU-heavy work off the request path. Little's Law (lesson 01.04) gives the concurrency you need: 5,000 requests per second at 100 ms each means 500 requests in flight, which is fine for an event loop and needs a 500-thread pool in the thread-per-request model.

> Takeaway: thread-per-request is simple but costs a full thread for every waiting connection; event loops and lightweight-thread runtimes make idle connections cheap by only spending CPU on sockets that are ready. Match OS threads to cores, never block the loop, and push CPU-heavy work elsewhere.

?? Each NGINX worker uses non-blocking sockets and an event loop over epoll, so an idle connection costs only a file descriptor, kernel buffers and a small state machine, and the worker spends CPU only on sockets the kernel reports as ready. With one worker per core there are few context switches. A thread-per-request server spends a full thread (stack memory, scheduling, context switches) on every connection even while it just waits, so it runs out of memory or thread-pool slots at thousands of connections while the CPU sits mostly idle.

## Two ways to add capacity
When a system runs out of capacity, you have two basic options:
- **Vertical scaling (scaling up)**: give the machine more CPU, more RAM, faster disks or a faster network. For example, move a database from 8 vCPUs and 32 GB of RAM to 32 vCPUs and 64 GB.
- **Horizontal scaling (scaling out)**: add more machines and spread the work across them. For example, go from 3 web servers to 12 behind a load balancer.

Both work, both have limits, and nearly every real system uses both: horizontally scaled stateless services in front of a database that is scaled vertically for as long as possible. Understanding why that combination is so common is the core of this lesson.

%%compare Vertical versus horizontal scaling: a bigger machine, or more machines behind a load balancer.
[Vertical]
Traffic -> 1 bigger server -> Limit
+ No code or architecture changes
+ One machine to operate
- Ceiling: the biggest machine sold
- Still a single point of failure
- Upgrades often need a restart
[*Horizontal]
Traffic -> Load balancer -(spread)-> N servers
+ Add capacity in small steps
+ One server failing is survivable
+ Capacity near users in many regions
- Software must be designed for it
- More moving parts to run
%%

## Vertical: simple, but with a ceiling
Vertical scaling has a lot going for it:
- **No code changes.** The application doesn't know it's on a bigger machine. A database that was CPU-bound on 8 cores often runs comfortably on 32.
- **Simple operations.** One machine to monitor, back up and patch. No distributed coordination, no data spread across nodes.
- **No network between parts.** Everything on one box talks through memory, which is about a thousand times faster than a network round trip.
- **Often cheaper at first.** In the cloud, price per vCPU is roughly constant within an instance family up to fairly large sizes, so a 32-vCPU machine costs about four times an 8-vCPU one, without the overhead of extra load balancers and coordination.

And real limits:
- **A hard ceiling.** The largest cloud instances offer a few hundred vCPUs and a few TB of RAM (special high-memory instances reach tens of TB). Beyond that, there is nothing bigger to buy. On owned hardware, the top-end machines cost disproportionately more per core.
- **A single point of failure.** If that machine dies, the service goes with it. You can add a standby replica, but now you are already doing a form of horizontal scaling.
- **Downtime to resize.** Moving to a bigger instance usually means a restart or a failover, which is a few seconds to minutes of disruption.
- **Not everything scales with cores.** A workload limited by one hot lock, one single-threaded process or disk latency gets little from more CPUs.

## Horizontal: resilient, but more complex
Horizontal scaling adds machines:
- **Practically unlimited capacity**: keep adding nodes as traffic grows, in small, cheap steps.
- **Redundancy**: with 10 servers, losing one costs 10% of capacity, not 100%.
- **Elasticity**: autoscaling can add servers at the evening peak and remove them at night, so you pay for what you use.
- **Geography**: you can put capacity in several regions, close to users.

And costs:
- **More moving parts**: load balancers, service discovery, health checks, deployment orchestration, distributed monitoring.
- **The software must allow it.** This is the hard part. Any state a server keeps locally (sessions, uploaded files, in-memory caches, scheduled jobs) breaks when the next request lands on a different server.
- **Coordination overhead**: once servers must agree on anything, adding more of them adds communication. Throughput does not always grow linearly.

### Why scaling is rarely linear
If one server handles 1,000 requests per second, ten servers should handle 10,000. In practice they handle less, for two reasons. **Contention**: some part of the work is serialised through a shared resource (one database, one lock), and that part doesn't get faster with more servers. **Coherency**: nodes must exchange information to stay in agreement, and the amount of exchange can grow faster than the node count. A system where 5% of each request goes through a shared bottleneck can never exceed 20 times the throughput of one node, however many nodes you add, because that 5% sets the ceiling. The design goal of horizontal scaling is to make the shared part as close to zero as possible.

## How a web tier scales out
A standard path from one server to millions of users. Each step removes the current bottleneck:

%%timeline A typical scaling journey. Each stage is triggered by a measured bottleneck, not by fashion.
Stage 1 | one server runs app, DB and cache
Stage 2 | database moves to its own server
Stage 3 | load balancer plus several app servers
Stage 4 | *sessions move to a shared store: app tier is stateless
Stage 5 | DB primary plus read replicas
Stage 6 | cache and CDN in front
Stage 7 | queues and background workers
Stage 8 | database is sharded
%%

1. **One server** runs the web app, the database and the cache. Fine for a prototype and often for the first thousands of users.
2. **Split the database** onto its own machine so the two tiers can be sized and scaled independently. The database usually wants RAM and fast disks; the app wants CPU.
3. **Put a load balancer** in front of several web servers. Clients see only the balancer's public IP; servers sit on private IPs. If one server fails its health check, traffic goes to the others.
4. **Make the web tier stateless.** Move session data out of the web servers into a shared store (Redis, the database) or into signed tokens the client carries. Store uploaded files in object storage, not on local disk. Now any server can handle any request, and you can add or remove servers freely.
5. **Add database replication**: one primary for writes, several replicas for reads. This multiplies read capacity, at the cost of replication lag.
6. **Add a cache** for hot data and a **CDN** for static files and media, taking most reads off the database and the app servers.
7. **Add message queues** so slow work (emails, image resizing, reports) happens asynchronously on separately scaled workers.
8. **Shard the database**: split the data across several primaries by key, once writes or data size outgrow one machine.

%%flow The stateless web tier: any app server can take any request because session state lives in a shared store.
Users -> Load balancer -> *App servers 1..N -(session lookup)-> Shared session store
App servers 1..N -(queries)-> Database
%%

! Example: why "stateless" matters
! A shop runs 4 app servers behind a round-robin load balancer, and each server keeps shopping baskets in its own memory.
! A user adds an item on server 2. Their next request goes to server 3, which has no basket for them: the basket appears empty. Sticky sessions (always send a user to the same server) hide this, until server 2 is restarted for a deploy and every user pinned to it loses their basket. Autoscaling down has the same effect.
! Moving baskets into Redis fixes both. Each request reads the basket by session ID (a 0.5 ms lookup), any server can serve any user, and deploys or scale-downs lose nothing.

## Why databases are the hard part
Stateless servers are interchangeable, so adding one is trivial: start it, register it with the load balancer, done. A database holds **state**, and every copy must agree on it. That turns scaling into a set of hard problems:
- **Scaling reads with replicas** introduces **replication lag**. A user writes to the primary, then reads from a replica that hasn't caught up, and their change seems to have vanished. You need read-your-writes routing or tolerance for stale reads (lesson 01.04).
- **Replicas don't help writes.** Every write must still go through the primary, and every replica must replay it. Ten replicas give you roughly ten times the reads and exactly the same write capacity.
- **Scaling writes requires sharding**: splitting the data across several primaries by key. That brings queries that span shards, transactions across shards, rebalancing when a shard fills up, and hot shards when one key is far more popular than others (lesson 04.06).
- **Failover is delicate.** Promoting a replica when the primary dies risks losing the last few acknowledged writes, or ending up with two primaries.

That is why a common rule is: **scale the database vertically for as long as it is comfortable, add read replicas for read-heavy loads, and design the data model so you can shard later** (choose a partition key early, avoid queries that need the whole dataset).

! Example: vertical first, then replicas
! A Postgres primary on 8 vCPUs handles 3,000 queries per second at 70% CPU. Traffic is 90% reads and is growing 10% a month.
! Option A, scale up to 32 vCPUs. Roughly four times the CPU, so about 12,000 queries per second at the same utilisation. No code changes; a planned failover takes about 30 seconds. At 10% monthly growth, 4x lasts about 14 months.
! Option B, add three read replicas of the same size and route reads to them. Read capacity grows about fourfold, but writes stay on one 8-vCPU primary (300 per second, plenty of headroom), and the app must handle lag.
! Most teams do A first, because it is simpler, and B when the read share and growth justify it. Sharding only comes up if writes, or the total data size, outgrow the largest primary they can buy.

%%matrix How easily each tier scales out. State is what makes scaling hard.
Tier | Stateless? | Add a node | Main difficulty
Web or API servers | yes | yes, trivial | keep them stateless
Background workers | yes | yes, trivial | idempotent jobs
Cache cluster | partial, rebuildable | partial, keys remap | cold cache after changes
DB read replicas | no | partial, easy for reads | replication lag
DB write primary | no | no, needs sharding | cross-shard queries, rebalancing
%%

## Choosing
- **Vertical** fits less critical workloads with predictable growth, where keeping cost and complexity low matters most, and stateful components such as databases where splitting is expensive.
- **Horizontal** fits critical systems that need high availability, room for large growth, elastic capacity, or presence in multiple regions, and anything stateless.

In practice most systems do both: horizontally scaled stateless services in front of a vertically scaled database, which is later replicated and, only if needed, sharded.

## Common mistakes
- **Sharding too early.** It's very hard to undo, and a single modern database server goes a long way.
- **Hidden local state**: files written to local disk, in-process caches assumed to be authoritative, cron jobs that run on every server at once.
- **Relying on sticky sessions** instead of fixing state, which makes load uneven and failures lossy.
- **Assuming replicas add write capacity.** They add read capacity only.
- **Forgetting the load balancer is a component too.** It needs redundancy of its own (lesson 02.06).

## In an interview
Say which tiers are stateless and scale them horizontally behind a load balancer without further discussion. Spend your time on the stateful parts: estimate whether the database fits on one machine, say when you would add replicas, and name the partition key you would shard on if the numbers demand it. "Writes peak at 2,000 per second, which one primary handles, so we'll scale it vertically and add replicas for reads; if writes grow tenfold we'll shard by user ID" is a complete answer.

> Takeaway: scale stateless tiers horizontally behind a load balancer, after moving all state out of them. Scale the stateful database tier vertically first, then with read replicas, and shard only when writes or data size outgrow one machine.

?? Stateless web servers keep no data of their own (sessions and files live in shared stores), so any server can handle any request, and scaling out is just adding servers behind a load balancer. A database holds state that must stay correct and consistent across copies, so adding machines means replication, which brings lag, failover risk and no extra write capacity, or sharding, which means splitting data by key, cross-shard queries and transactions, rebalancing and hot shards.

## Two ways to structure an application
A **monolith** is one deployable application. It handles requests, runs all the business logic and talks to the database, and it ships as a single unit: one build, one deploy, one process (usually run as many identical copies behind a load balancer). Inside, it may be very well organised into modules, but those modules call each other with ordinary function calls and share one database.

**Microservices** build the application as a suite of small services. Each runs in its own process, owns its own data, communicates with the others over the network (HTTP, gRPC or messaging), and is deployed independently. An e-commerce site might have separate services for catalogue, basket, orders, payments, shipping and notifications, each owned by a different team.

Neither is "modern" or "legacy". They are two points on a trade-off between simplicity and independence, and the right choice depends mostly on team size, domain understanding and how differently the parts need to scale.

## What makes something "microservices"
Splitting code into several processes is not enough. The style is usually described by these characteristics:
- **Components as services**: parts can be deployed and replaced independently, behind explicit remote interfaces.
- **Organised around business capabilities**: one team owns "payments" end to end (API, logic, data, on-call), rather than teams split by layer ("the database team", "the UI team").
- **Products, not projects**: the team that builds a service keeps running it. "You build it, you run it."
- **Smart endpoints, dumb pipes**: business logic lives in the services; the network or message broker just carries messages. This is a reaction against the Enterprise Service Bus, where routing and transformation logic piled up in the middleware.
- **Decentralised governance and data**: teams choose their own tools within limits, and **each service owns its database**. Other services may not read its tables directly; they go through its API or its events.
- **Infrastructure automation**: with dozens of services, automated builds, tests and deployments (CI/CD) are mandatory, not nice to have.
- **Design for failure**: any remote call can fail or be slow, so services use timeouts, retries with backoff, circuit breakers and good monitoring (Module 2).
- **Evolutionary design**: if two services always change together, they probably belong together.

### Conway's law
A system's structure tends to mirror the communication structure of the organisation that builds it. Three teams building a compiler tend to produce a three-pass compiler. Microservices use this deliberately: give each team a service, and the boundaries in the code follow the boundaries between teams, so teams can work without constant coordination. The flip side is that if your service boundaries do not match your teams, you get endless cross-team meetings about every change.

## What microservices cost
The price is real, and much of it is invisible on an architecture diagram:
- **Remote calls are slow and can fail.** An in-process function call takes nanoseconds. A call to another service in the same data centre takes 0.5 to a few milliseconds, includes serialisation and deserialisation, and can time out, fail, or succeed without the caller learning about it.
- **No simple transactions across services.** In a monolith, "create the order and reserve the stock" is one database transaction. Across services there is no shared database, so you need **sagas**: a sequence of local transactions with compensating actions to undo earlier steps if a later one fails. You live with eventual consistency in between.
- **Availability multiplies down.** A request that synchronously calls five services, each 99.9% available, succeeds only about 99.5% of the time (lesson 01.05).
- **Refactoring across boundaries is hard.** Moving a function between modules is an IDE operation. Moving it between services means a new API, a versioned rollout and a data migration.
- **Operations multiply.** Every service needs deployment, monitoring, logging, alerts, dashboards, dependency upgrades and security patches. Debugging needs **distributed tracing** to follow one request across ten services.
- **Poor boundaries just move complexity** into the connections between services. A "distributed monolith", where every change still touches five services that must be deployed together, has the costs of both styles and the benefits of neither.

! Example: the latency bill for one page

With 1% of calls hitting a 100 ms slow path, the chance that a page hits at least one slow call is 1 − 0.99⁶ ≈ 6%.
! Running the calls in parallel cuts the typical time to the slowest single call, but adds code for timeouts, partial failures and fallbacks ("show the page without reviews"). The work is all doable; it just isn't free.

## Monolith first
Most microservice success stories started as a monolith that was later split. Many systems built as microservices from scratch got into trouble. Two reasons:
- **Early on, you don't know if anyone wants the product.** Speed and fast feedback matter more than independent deploys. The operational cost of microservices (sometimes called the "microservice premium") is paid every day, while its benefits only appear with many teams and a stable domain.
- **Good service boundaries are hard to draw before you understand the domain.** You learn where the seams are by building the product. Moving functionality between services is much harder than moving it inside one codebase, so boundaries drawn too early tend to be wrong and expensive to fix.

Two well-trodden paths:
1. **Build a well-modularised monolith**, with clear internal module boundaries (each module has its own public interface and, ideally, its own tables that other modules don't touch). Later, peel off services at the edges when there is a clear reason. Shopify runs one of the largest Ruby on Rails monoliths in the world this way, enforcing boundaries between components inside a single codebase.
2. **Build a deliberately throwaway first version** to learn the domain, then rebuild with better boundaries.

When you do split, the **strangler fig** approach is the usual one: put a routing layer in front of the monolith, build the new service, move one route at a time to it, and delete the old code once the route is fully moved. The monolith shrinks gradually and you can stop at any point.

## Cases in reverse
### Prime Video
Prime Video's stream-quality monitoring tool was built with serverless microservices. A conversion service split video streams into frames and wrote them to S3; a defect detector read them back to look for problems; AWS Step Functions orchestrated each step. At scale, two costs exploded. Step Functions charges per state transition, and the design made several transitions for every second of every stream. The detector also made huge numbers of S3 requests to fetch frames. The team merged conversion and detection into a single process that passes frames in memory, ran it on containers (ECS), and cut the tool's infrastructure cost by over 90%, while also letting it scale further. Only this tool became a monolith; the rest of Prime Video stayed service-based.

### Segment
Segment had split its event-delivery system into more than a hundred small services, one per destination (each analytics or marketing tool it forwarded data to). Each had its own queue, its own deployment and its own copy of shared libraries, which drifted out of sync. A small team spent most of its time keeping them all running. Merging them into one service with one shared codebase made the system easier to change and operate.

The common lesson: choose the structure the workload and the team need, estimate the costs (including per-request infrastructure charges) before committing, and don't treat the decision as permanent.

! Example: deciding whether to extract a service
! A 40-engineer company runs a monolith. The payments module changes weekly, needs PCI compliance audits, and is owned by one 6-person team that is blocked waiting for the shared release train twice a week.
! Reasons to split: a distinct team, a distinct compliance boundary, different deployment cadence. Reasons against: payments are called synchronously from checkout, so the split adds a network hop and a saga for refunds.
! Decision: extract payments behind a gRPC interface, keep the checkout call synchronous with a 2 s timeout and an idempotency key (lesson 02.09), and publish PaymentSucceeded events for everything else. The other modules stay in the monolith.

## When to split
Good reasons:
- **Separate teams need to deploy independently**, and coordinating releases is the bottleneck.
- **One part needs very different scaling or hardware**: a video transcoder needs GPUs, the rest of the app doesn't.
- **Fault isolation**: a memory leak in report generation should not take down checkout.
- **A security or compliance boundary**: payment card data in a small, audited service.

Bad reasons:
- It's fashionable, or "that's how big companies do it".
- The codebase is messy. A messy monolith split up becomes messy microservices, now with network calls.
- To use a different language for fun.

## Common mistakes
- **Starting with microservices** for a new product with a small team.
- **Sharing a database between services**, which keeps all the coupling and adds the network.
- **Splitting by technical layer** ("data service", "logic service") instead of by business capability.
- **Long synchronous call chains**, which multiply latency and failure.
- **No tracing or central logging**, so nobody can follow a failing request.

## In an interview
Most interview designs show several services, because the interviewer wants to see components. Draw them, but be ready to say which could start as modules in one deployable, why each boundary exists, and which calls are synchronous. If asked "monolith or microservices?", answer with the trade-off: team size, deployment independence, scaling needs, and the costs you would accept.

> Takeaway: start with a modular monolith. Split out a service when a team, scaling, isolation or compliance need clearly justifies the network calls, distributed data and operational overhead, and split along business capabilities, not technical layers.

## The problem it solves
In many applications, business logic is tangled with database code. SQL strings or ORM queries are scattered through request handlers, services and even templates. A function that decides whether an order can be allocated to a warehouse batch also builds a query, opens a session and commits a transaction.

That causes three problems:
- **Testing is slow and fragile.** Every test of a business rule needs a real database with the right tables and rows. A suite of 2,000 such tests can take many minutes, and it breaks when someone changes a fixture.
- **The domain is tied to the storage technology.** Model classes inherit from ORM base classes, so the business rules depend on how tables are laid out, and changing storage means touching business code.
- **Query logic is duplicated.** The same "find active orders for this customer" query appears in five places, each slightly different.

The **repository pattern** is a small, focused fix for all three. It is not specific to any language or framework; it shows up in Java (Spring Data repositories), C# (.NET), Python, Go and elsewhere.

## The pattern
A **repository** sits between the domain logic and the data-mapping layer, and behaves "like an in-memory collection of domain objects". Code adds objects, removes them and looks them up by ID as if they were in a list or a dictionary. The repository translates those operations into database work behind the scenes.

The benefits:
- **Query logic lives in one place.** All the SQL for orders lives in the order repository.
- **The domain stays free of persistence code.** Business classes are plain objects with behaviour, with no knowledge of tables, sessions or connections.
- **Dependencies point one way.** The business logic depends on an abstract repository interface; the database code depends on the business objects, not the other way round.

### The vocabulary: ports and adapters
- A **port** is an interface your core code defines and depends on: "something I can add orders to and get orders from".
- An **adapter** is a concrete implementation of a port for one technology: a Postgres adapter, a DynamoDB adapter, an in-memory adapter for tests.

This is the idea behind **onion**, **hexagonal** and **ports-and-adapters** architectures. They differ in diagrams and naming, but share one rule: **dependencies point inward**, toward the domain. The domain knows nothing about the outer layers.

%%layers Dependencies point inward. The domain at the centre knows nothing about the database; adapters on the outside depend on the interfaces the inner layers define.
Adapters | SQL repository, HTTP handlers, message consumers
Service layer | use cases: allocate order, cancel order
*Repository interface (port) | add, get, list: defined by the core
Domain model | plain classes and business rules, no imports from outer layers
%%

## Building one, step by step
The example domain: a warehouse allocates order lines to batches of stock.

### 1. Invert the dependency
In a typical ORM setup, model classes inherit from the ORM's base class, so the domain imports the database library. Instead, keep the domain model as plain classes and define the table mapping separately, so that the ORM layer imports the model and not the reverse. In SQLAlchemy this is "imperative" (classical) mapping: you declare tables, then tell the ORM to map them onto your plain classes.

### 2. Define a tiny interface (the port)
The interface should be as small as the domain needs. Often two methods are enough:
- `add(batch)`: store a new object.
- `get(reference)`: fetch one by its ID.

```
class AbstractRepository(abc.ABC):
    @abc.abstractmethod
    def add(self, batch: Batch) -> None: ...

    @abc.abstractmethod
    def get(self, reference: str) -> Batch: ...
```

Add methods only when a use case needs them (`list_for_sku(sku)`), and name them in the language of the domain, not of SQL.

### 3. Implement adapters
The production adapter wraps a database session:

```
class SqlAlchemyRepository(AbstractRepository):
    def __init__(self, session):
        self.session = session

    def add(self, batch):
        self.session.add(batch)

    def get(self, reference):
        return self.session.query(Batch).filter_by(reference=reference).one()
```

Committing is left to the caller, because one use case may touch several repositories and should commit them together. That job is usually given to a **Unit of Work** object, which owns the transaction and exposes the repositories.

The test adapter wraps an ordinary set:

```
class FakeRepository(AbstractRepository):
    def __init__(self, batches):
        self._batches = set(batches)

    def add(self, batch):
        self._batches.add(batch)

    def get(self, reference):
        return next(b for b in self._batches if b.reference == reference)
```

Each method is one line. A useful heuristic: **if a fake is hard to write, your abstraction is too complicated.**

### 4. Use the fake in tests
Service-layer tests build a `FakeRepository` holding a few objects and run real business logic against it, with no database, no network, no fixtures and no cleanup. They are fast, focused on behaviour, and don't break when the schema changes. A smaller set of integration tests then checks that the SQL adapter really does store and load objects correctly.

+ Real persistence and transactions
- Each test needs a database
[*Tests]

! Example: what the fake buys you in test time
! A service has 1,500 tests of business rules. Against a real database, each test sets up rows, runs, and rolls back, about 40 ms each: 1,500 × 40 ms = 60 s, and more on a busy CI machine.
! With a fake repository, each test runs in about 0.2 ms: 1,500 × 0.2 ms = 0.3 s. Developers can run the whole suite on every save.
! The team keeps about 30 integration tests against a real Postgres in Docker that check the adapter's queries and mapping: 30 × 100 ms = 3 s. Total feedback time drops from a minute to a few seconds, and failures point straight at the business rule that broke.

## What else a repository lets you swap
Tests are the most common reason, but the same seam helps elsewhere:
- **A different database.** Moving orders from Postgres to DynamoDB means writing one new adapter and changing one line of wiring. The business rules don't change. (In practice, a move like this also changes which queries are efficient, so the interface may need adjusting, but the change is contained.)
- **A cache layer.** A caching repository can wrap the real one: check Redis, fall back to the inner repository on a miss.
- **A remote service.** A repository can fetch data from another service's API rather than a table; the domain code doesn't care.
- **Read models.** You can have a separate, query-optimised repository (or a plain SQL view) for screens and reports, while writes go through the domain repository.

! Example: wrapping a repository with a cache
! Product lookups hit Postgres 8,000 times a second, mostly for the same 2,000 products. The team writes `CachedProductRepository(inner, redis)`. Its `get(id)` checks Redis first and, on a miss, calls `inner.get(id)` and stores the result with a 60-second time to live.
! Nothing in the service layer changes; only the wiring at startup does. If the cache serves 95% of reads, Postgres sees about 400 lookups a second instead of 8,000. Tests still use the plain fake and don't need Redis.

## Trade-offs
**Pros:**
- A simple boundary between storage and domain, which keeps both easier to change.
- Easy fakes and fast unit tests.
- You can model the business problem before thinking about tables.
- Full control over the schema, since the ORM maps onto your classes rather than shaping them.
- Swapping or wrapping storage later is easier.

**Cons:**
- An ORM already gives some decoupling, so a repository on top of an ORM can feel like an abstraction over an abstraction.
- Hand-maintained mappings are extra code that has to stay in sync with the tables.
- Every extra layer adds maintenance cost and a "why is this here?" moment for new developers.
- It can hide expensive queries. `for id in ids: repo.get(id)` looks innocent and is the **N+1 query problem**: 1 query becomes 100. Add batch methods like `get_many(ids)` when you need them.

## When not to use it
A simple CRUD app (forms that save rows and pages that show them) doesn't need a domain model or a repository. If the business logic is "validate input and save it", the extra layer is pure cost; use the ORM or plain SQL directly and test against a real database. The pattern pays off as the business logic grows complex: pricing rules, allocation, workflows with many states, anything you want to test thoroughly and quickly.

## Common mistakes
- **Leaking the ORM through the interface**, for example returning query objects that callers keep filtering. Then the domain depends on the ORM again.
- **Generic repositories with dozens of methods** (`find_by_x_and_y_order_by_z`). Keep the interface small and domain-shaped.
- **Committing inside the repository**, which stops a use case from combining several changes in one transaction.
- **Never testing the real adapter.** Fakes test logic; a few integration tests must still test SQL.

## In an interview
This comes up more in design discussions of code structure than in large-scale system design. If asked, explain the dependency direction, the small interface, and the two adapters (real and fake), and give the testing benefit as the main reason. Mention that it also isolates storage choices, so changing databases later is contained to one layer.

> Takeaway: put storage behind a small, collection-like interface that the domain defines, so business logic depends on the interface rather than the database. That lets you swap in an in-memory fake for fast tests, or a different or cached store in production. Use it when domain logic is substantial; skip it for thin CRUD apps.

## Requirements change; schemas resist
Products keep adding fields: a new profile attribute, a per-customer setting, a new event type, a product category with its own specifications. In code, adding a field is a one-line change. In a large, busy database, it can be anything from instant to a multi-hour operation that locks the table.

FriendFeed, an early social network, described tables of 10 to 20 million rows where adding an index or a column locked MySQL for hours, so the team could not ship features without downtime. That pain shaped a famous design. This lesson is about modelling data so it can evolve: when real columns are fine, when to store flexible documents, how to index them, and how to change formats that flow between services.

## Option 1: just add columns
For core fields that you filter on, join on, sort by or constrain (unique, not null, foreign key), real columns are still the best home. The database knows their type, enforces their rules, stores them compactly and can index them efficiently.

Schema changes are much less scary than they used to be:
- **Postgres** adds a nullable column, or a column with a constant default (since version 11), as a metadata-only change. It takes milliseconds regardless of table size, because existing rows are not rewritten; the default is stored once in the catalogue.
- **MySQL 8.0** supports `ALGORITHM=INSTANT` for adding columns in most cases, also without rewriting rows.
- **New indexes** can be built without blocking writes (`CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY` in Postgres, online DDL in MySQL), though the build still takes time and I/O on a big table.

What remains genuinely painful: changing a column's type, adding a column with a default that must be computed per row, backfilling values into hundreds of millions of rows, and adding constraints that must be checked against existing data. Those need online migration techniques (Module 3): add the new column, backfill in batches, write to both, switch reads, drop the old one.

%%timeline An online migration that renames and retypes a column on a 500-million-row table without downtime. Each step is safe to pause or roll back.
Day 1 | add new nullable column: instant
Day 1 | deploy code that writes both columns
Days 2 to 4 | *backfill old rows in batches of 10,000
Day 5 | switch reads to the new column
Day 12 | stop writing old column, then drop it
%%

## Option 2: schemaless storage inside SQL (FriendFeed)
FriendFeed stored every entity (posts, comments, likes) in one MySQL table with only a few real columns:
- `added_id`: an auto-incrementing primary key, so new rows are appended in order on disk. That keeps writes sequential and fast.
- `id`: a UUID identifying the entity.
- `updated`: a timestamp.
- `body`: a compressed, serialised dictionary holding all of the entity's properties. MySQL treated it as an opaque blob.

Adding a property to an entity now needed no schema change at all: the application simply started writing it into the dictionary.

To query by a property, they created **separate index tables**, each holding just the indexed value and the entity ID, for example `index_user_id (user_id, entity_id)`. Each index table was sharded by its own key, so "all entities by user 42" was one lookup on one shard. Adding a new queryable field meant creating a new index table (a fresh, empty table, so no locks on anything existing) and letting a background process called **the Cleaner** populate it by scanning the entities.

%%flow FriendFeed's write path: the entity is stored as one opaque blob, and each index gets its own small table.
[Write] Application -(serialised dict)-> *entities table
[Write] Application -(user_id, entity_id)-> index_user_id table
[Write] Application -(link_url, entity_id)-> index_link table
%%

Index tables could briefly disagree with the entities, because they lived on different shards and were written in separate steps, with no transaction spanning them. FriendFeed accepted that:
- The entities table was the **source of truth**.
- Reads used an index only to find **candidate IDs**, then fetched the real entities and **re-checked** the condition, discarding any that no longer matched.
- The Cleaner kept fixing inconsistencies in the background, within seconds.

%%seq A query by user under FriendFeed's scheme. The index may be stale, so the reader re-checks every candidate against the entity itself.
App > index_user_id : entity ids for user 42
index_user_id -> App : e1, e2, e3
App > entities : fetch e1, e2, e3
entities -> App : 3 blobs
App > App : decode, drop e2 (user changed)
App -> App : return e1, e3
%%

The trade-off: total flexibility for adding fields, in exchange for doing in application code what a database normally does (indexing, consistency, type checking). Many later document stores and key-value systems offer a similar model natively.

## Option 3: JSON columns (Postgres JSONB)
Modern relational databases let you mix the two: real columns for core fields, plus a JSON column for the flexible part. Postgres has two JSON types:
- `json` stores the text exactly as given (keeping whitespace, key order and duplicate keys) and re-parses it on every access.
- `jsonb` stores a decomposed binary form. It is slightly slower to write, much faster to query, and indexable. Postgres recommends `jsonb` for most uses.

The main query operators:
- **Containment** `@>` tests whether a document contains a given structure: `attrs @> '{"colour": "red"}'` or `doc @> '{"tags": ["sale"]}'`.
- **Existence** `?` tests for a top-level key: `attrs ? 'warranty_years'`.
- **Extraction** `->` returns a JSON value and `->>` returns text: `attrs ->> 'size'`.

Indexing:
- A **GIN index** (generalised inverted index, which maps each key and value to the rows containing it) speeds up containment and existence queries. The default operator class `jsonb_ops` indexes every key and every value: flexible, but larger. `jsonb_path_ops` only supports `@>` but is smaller and faster.
- An **expression index** on one key, such as `CREATE INDEX ON products ((attrs ->> 'brand'))`, is a normal B-tree on that extracted value. It is the best choice when queries target one specific key.
- A **generated column** can promote a JSON key to a real typed column that the database keeps in sync: `brand text GENERATED ALWAYS AS (attrs ->> 'brand') STORED`.

Two cautions:
- **Keep a somewhat predictable structure.** "Schemaless" in practice means the schema lives in your application code instead. Document which keys exist and what types they have, and validate on write.
- **Keep documents small.** An update to any key rewrites the whole value, and with it a new version of the row. Values over about 2 KB are compressed and moved out of line into a separate storage area (TOAST), so frequently updating a large document is expensive. Put a counter that changes every second in a real column, not inside a 50 KB JSON blob.

%%compare Two ways to store product attributes that differ by category. Real columns suit shared, queried fields; JSONB suits sparse, varying ones.
[One column per attribute]
products: id, name, price, screen_size, wattage, fabric, ...
+ Typed, constrained, compact
- Hundreds of mostly null columns
- Every new category needs a migration
[*Core columns plus JSONB]
products: id, name, price, category, attrs jsonb
+ New attributes need no migration
+ GIN or expression indexes when needed
- Types and rules enforced by your code
- Whole value rewritten on update
%%

! Example: an online shop's product attributes
! The shop sells 2 million products in 400 categories. Every product has a name, price, category and stock count: those are real columns with constraints and indexes. Televisions have screen_size and refresh_rate; kettles have wattage and capacity; shirts have fabric and fit. No attribute applies to more than 5% of products.
! As real columns, that would be over 3,000 mostly-null columns, and every new category would need a migration. Instead, `attrs jsonb` holds the category-specific attributes, typically 300 bytes per product.
! The filter "televisions over 55 inches" runs as `WHERE category = 'tv' AND (attrs ->> 'screen_size')::numeric > 55`, using the category index to narrow to 40,000 rows first. When marketing wants "brand" filtering everywhere, the team adds an expression index on `attrs ->> 'brand'`, then later promotes brand to a generated column once every category uses it.

### The anti-pattern: entity-attribute-value
A common alternative is a table of `(entity_id, attribute_name, value)` rows, one row per attribute. It looks flexible but performs badly: rebuilding one product means joining or pivoting dozens of rows, values are all stored as text, and constraints are impossible. A JSON column gives the same flexibility with far less pain.

## Option 4: schemas that evolve (for data between services)
Data passed between services, written to logs, or stored in files outlives any single deploy. A producer and its consumers often run different versions of the code at the same time. Serialisation formats such as Protocol Buffers, Thrift and Avro are designed so different schema versions can still read each other's data:
- **Protobuf and Thrift** tag every field with a number, and the number is what goes on the wire. New fields get new numbers; old readers skip tags they don't recognise. You may rename a field freely (names are not encoded), but you must **never change a field's type or reuse a tag number**, because old data with that tag would be misread. Mark removed tags as `reserved`.
- **Avro** has no tags. Data is written without field names or numbers, and the reader needs the **writer's schema** to decode it, matching fields by name against its own schema. A new field must have a **default value**, so a new reader can fill it in when reading old data. You can only remove a field that had a default, so an old reader can fill it in when reading new data.

The general rules:
- Add new fields as **optional**, with defaults.
- **Never repurpose** an old field for a new meaning, or reuse its name or number.
- When a change does require coordination, **roll out readers before writers**, so nothing ever sees data it can't understand.
- For a truly breaking change, create a new message type or version and run both until everyone has moved.

Lesson 02.08 applies the same rules to events and schema registries.

! Example: evolving a protobuf message safely
! Version 1: `message User { string id = 1; string name = 2; string email = 3; }`.
! The team adds a phone number: `string phone = 4;`. Old services reading new messages skip tag 4. New services reading old messages see an empty phone. Both keep working, in any deploy order.
! Later they drop email in favour of a contacts list. They delete field 3 and write `reserved 3; reserved "email";`, then add `repeated Contact contacts = 5;`. A developer who later tries to reuse tag 3 for a different string gets a compile error instead of silently misreading years of stored data.

%%matrix Where each kind of field belongs. Real columns win when the database must understand the field; documents win when only the application does.
Need | Real column | JSONB | Separate index table
Filter or sort often | yes | partial, with index | yes
Join or foreign key | yes | no | partial
Constraints (unique, not null) | yes | no | partial
Sparse, varies by row | no | yes | partial
Add without migration | partial, often instant | yes | yes, build in background
Updated independently, often | yes | no, rewrites value | yes
%%

## Common mistakes
- **Putting everything in JSON** because migrations feel scary, then reimplementing constraints and types badly in application code.
- **Joining or filtering heavily on JSON keys** without an index, turning queries into full scans.
- **Large, frequently updated documents**, which rewrite kilobytes for every small change.
- **Reusing a protobuf tag or Avro field name** for a new meaning.
- **Entity-attribute-value tables** in place of a JSON column.

## In an interview
When requirements say "users can define custom fields" or "each category has different attributes", say you will keep core fields as columns and put the variable part in a JSONB column (or a document store), with expression or GIN indexes for the few keys you filter on. For data passed between services, mention a schema format with compatibility rules and additive changes only.

> Takeaway: keep core, frequently queried, constrained fields as real columns, since adding one is often instant now. Put sparse or fast-changing attributes in JSONB with targeted indexes, and use evolvable schemas (protobuf, Avro) with strict additive-only rules for data passed between systems.

?? Use a jsonb column for optional, sparse or per-tenant attributes that vary from row to row and are mostly read together with the record, such as product specifications that differ by category or user-defined custom fields, especially when new attributes appear often and you don't want a migration for each. Add a real column instead for fields you filter, sort or join on heavily, need constraints or foreign keys on, or update often on their own, since the database can type, enforce and index those far better.

## Cost is a design requirement
Two designs can meet the same latency and availability targets and differ in monthly cost by ten or a hundred times. The difference is rarely in clever tuning. It comes from a few structural choices made early: where data is stored, how much of it moves across network boundaries, how much compute sits idle, and how many billable operations each user action triggers.

The first rule of cost-aware architecture is to **make cost a non-functional requirement**. Treat it like security or availability, from design through operations. A business whose infrastructure costs grow faster than its revenue is in trouble, however elegant the system is. In the cloud this matters even more, because every engineer can create costs with a single API call, and the bill arrives a month later.

## Seven principles
A widely used set of principles for cost-aware design:
1. **Make cost a non-functional requirement.** Write it down next to latency and availability: "under $0.002 of infrastructure per order".
2. **Systems that last align cost to business.** Find the unit that drives revenue (orders, active users, gigabytes processed) and design so cost grows with it, not faster. A system whose cost per user rises as users grow has a structural problem.
3. **Architecting is a series of trade-offs.** Cost, resilience and performance conflict. Aim for the best value for each component, not the lowest bill.
4. **Unobserved systems lead to unknown costs.** If you can't see cost per feature, per service or per team, waste hides. Tag resources and report spend by owner.
5. **Cost-aware architectures implement cost controls.** Split systems into tiers by how critical they are, so less important parts can run on cheaper capacity or be scaled down when needed.
6. **Cost optimisation is incremental.** Keep revisiting; small savings compound at scale.
7. **Unchallenged success leads to assumptions.** Don't assume today's stack is still the cheapest way to do the job. Prices, workloads and products change.

## Where the money actually goes
Rough cloud prices (they vary by provider and region, but the ratios are what matter):

| Resource | Rough price | Notes |

The big levers follow directly:
- **Idle and oversized compute** is the most common waste. A fleet sized for peak and left running all night, or instances at 8% average CPU, can double a bill. Autoscaling, right-sizing and spot or committed pricing each cut compute cost by 30 to 70%.
- **Storage tier.** Data that nobody has read for 90 days does not need to be on SSD or even standard object storage. Lifecycle rules that move it to infrequent-access and then archive tiers can cut storage cost by 10 times or more.
- **Data transfer.** Traffic between zones and out to the internet is charged per GB. A service that calls another in a different zone 10,000 times a second with 10 KB responses moves about 260 TB a month, roughly $5,000 at a cent per GB in each direction.
- **Request counts.** Object stores and many managed services charge per operation. Millions of tiny objects or per-second orchestration steps can cost more than the storage itself. This is what hurt Prime Video's design (lesson 01.09).
- **Logs, metrics and traces.** Logging every request at debug level, or high-cardinality metrics with a label per user, can cost more than the servers being observed.
- **Managed service premiums.** Managed databases, search and queues cost more per unit of capacity than running the software yourself, in exchange for less operational work. That is often a good trade, but it should be a conscious one.

! Example: the cost of a log line
! An API serves 20,000 requests per second. Each request writes a 1 KB structured log line.
! Volume = 20,000 × 1 KB × 86,400 s ≈ 1.7 TB per day, about 52 TB per month.
! At ~$0.50 per GB ingested and indexed, that is about $26,000 a month, more than the API servers themselves (perhaps 40 instances at ~$150 each, about $6,000).
! Fixes: log successful requests at 1% sampling and every error in full (cuts volume by about 95%), drop bulky fields, and send raw logs to object storage at $0.02 per GB for the rare deep investigation. The bill drops to around $2,000 a month without losing what on-call engineers actually use.

! Example: lifecycle rules for user uploads
! A photo app stores 2 PB of uploads, growing by 50 TB a month. 90% of reads hit photos uploaded in the last 30 days.
! All on standard object storage: 2,000,000 GB × $0.023 ≈ $46,000 a month.
! With a lifecycle rule (standard for 30 days, infrequent access until 1 year, archive after that), suppose 50 TB stays standard, 550 TB is infrequent and 1,400 TB is archive: 50,000 × 0.023 + 550,000 × 0.0125 + 1,400,000 × 0.004 ≈ $1,150 + $6,900 + $5,600 ≈ $13,700 a month, about 70% less. Retrieval fees for the rare old photo are small by comparison.

## Design choices that change cost by 10x
Some decisions move the bill by an order of magnitude, so they deserve attention at design time:
- **Storage tier for cold data**: SSD-backed database versus archive object storage is about 100 times.
- **Where bytes travel**: serving media from a CDN or keeping traffic inside one zone versus shipping it across regions or straight from origin to the internet.
- **Per-request orchestration versus in-process work**: one function call versus a billable workflow step or storage request per item.
- **Pricing model**: on-demand versus committed or spot for steady or interruptible workloads (2 to 4 times), and for steady, large workloads, rented versus owned hardware.
- **Precompute versus compute on read**: rendering a page once and caching it versus rebuilding it on every view.
- **Sampling** logs, traces and analytics events versus keeping everything.

Archive tier for cold data | yes, 10x to 100x | yes, lifecycle rule | partial, slow retrieval
CDN in front of media | yes, 2x to 10x on egress | yes, config | yes, low
Spot or committed compute | yes, 2x to 4x | partial | partial, interruptions
Sample logs and traces | yes, 10x to 20x | yes, config | partial, less detail
In-process instead of per-step billing | yes, 10x or more | no, redesign | partial
Right-size idle instances | partial, 1.5x to 3x | yes | yes, low
%%

## Make cost visible
You cannot optimise what you can't see:
- **Tag every resource** with team, service and environment, and report spend by tag weekly.
- **Track unit cost**: dollars per thousand orders, per active user, per GB processed. A rising unit cost is an early warning even while the total bill looks fine during growth.
- **Put cost in design reviews**: every new component's proposal includes its expected monthly cost at today's and next year's load.
- **Alert on anomalies**: a forgotten test cluster or a runaway job should page someone in a day, not show up at month end.

## Cost controls by tier
Not every part of a system deserves the same spend. Split components by criticality:
- **Tier 1** (checkout, login): on-demand or committed capacity, multi-zone, generous headroom.
- **Tier 2** (recommendations, search suggestions): can degrade or be switched off under pressure.
- **Tier 3** (batch analytics, report generation, thumbnail regeneration): spot instances, off-peak schedules, can wait a day.

This gives you knobs: during a cost squeeze or a capacity crunch, you scale down tier 3 and degrade tier 2 without touching tier 1.

## The provider's view
The major cloud providers publish cost-optimisation guidance that adds practical habits: build cost awareness across teams, pay only for what you use and scale with demand, measure efficiency as business output per dollar, let managed services handle undifferentiated work, and attribute spend to teams and features.

## Cloud is not always cheaper
The opposite case also exists. 37signals (the company behind Basecamp and HEY) paid over $500,000 a year for managed database and search services alone on AWS, and over $3 million a year in total, and moved its steady workloads back to hardware it owns in rented data-centre space. The cloud shines in two cases: simple, low-traffic apps where the bill is small anyway, and highly irregular load, such as HEY's launch, which got 300,000 sign-ups in three weeks against a forecast of 30,000 in six months. For a mid-sized company with steady, predictable growth and an experienced operations team, owning hardware can be much cheaper over a few years.

%%compare Renting cloud capacity versus owning hardware. Neither is always cheaper; it depends on load shape and team skills.
[Cloud]

+ No upfront cost, capacity in minutes

Whether you agree with that move or not, the point is to **do the maths for your workload** instead of assuming. Steady-state compute that runs 24 hours a day for years is where owned or committed capacity wins. Bursty or uncertain load is where on-demand cloud wins.

## Common mistakes
- **Ignoring transfer costs** in a design that copies data across zones or regions.
- **Storing everything hot** forever.
- **Logging everything** at full detail.
- **No ownership of spend**, so nobody notices waste.
- **Optimising tiny line items** while a structural choice costs ten times more.

## In an interview
Mention cost when you choose storage tiers, replication across regions, or a CDN, with a rough number. "Five years of media is 55 PB; on standard object storage that's about $1.2M a month, so we'll move anything older than 90 days to an infrequent-access tier" shows cost awareness without derailing the design.

> Takeaway: design so cost tracks business value, make spend visible per team and feature, and focus on the structural levers that move the bill by 10x: storage tier, data transfer, idle compute, request counts and observability volume.

?? Examples of 10x choices: storing cold data in an archive or infrequent-access tier instead of SSD-backed databases or hot object storage (10 to 100 times cheaper per GB); keeping traffic in one zone and serving media through a CDN instead of paying cross-region or internet egress from origin; replacing per-request orchestration or per-item storage calls (like a billable workflow step per second of video) with in-process work; sampling logs and traces instead of keeping all of them; and running steady workloads on committed, spot or owned hardware instead of on-demand.

## Every component has a running cost
Adding a cache, a queue, a search cluster or a new database solves a problem. It also adds a thing to deploy, monitor, upgrade, secure, back up, capacity-plan and understand at 3 a.m. when it misbehaves. Each new component brings its own failure modes: a cache can serve stale data or stampede the database when it empties; a queue can back up silently; a second database can drift out of sync with the first.

Design interviews and real architecture reviews both reward knowing when **not** to add something. The simplest design that meets the requirements, with evidence that it does, beats a diagram full of boxes that nobody needed.

%%timeline The lifetime of a new component. Building it takes weeks; running it takes years.
Week 1 | prototype works, team is happy
Month 1 | production deploy, dashboards, alerts
Month 4 | first incident: memory full, nobody knows the tuning
Year 1 | major version upgrade, breaking changes
Year 2 | *original author leaves, knowledge goes with them
Year 3 | security patch needed across 4 environments

## Choose boring technology
A useful mental model: a company gets roughly three **innovation tokens**. Each unusual technology choice (a new database, a new language, a service discovery tool that is a year old) spends one. Most companies are not database companies. Their limited attention is better spent on their actual product than on learning the quirks of a novel datastore under production load.

"Boring" does not mean bad. Postgres, MySQL, Python, Java, Memcached, Redis and cron are boring because their behaviour and failure modes are well understood. They still have problems, but mostly **known unknowns**: you know they exist, there are answers on the internet, and someone on the team has seen them before. New technology has far more **unknown unknowns**: failure modes nobody has hit yet, in combinations nobody has tried.

The key point is that **the cost of a technology is mostly in operating it over years, not in building with it**. Picking "the right tool for each job" looks optimal locally, one problem at a time, but leaves the company running dozens of tools, each with its own monitoring, deployment, testing, upgrade path and expertise. The sum of many locally optimal choices is a globally expensive system.

%%compare Two ways to solve a new problem. The boring option is often harder at first and much cheaper over years.
[New tool for the job]
Problem -> New datastore -> Tuned for this case
+ Fits the problem neatly
+ Interesting to build
- New monitoring, backups, upgrades
- Unknown failure modes
- One more thing on call must know
[*Existing stack]
Problem -> Postgres plus app code -> Good enough
+ Known failure modes, known fixes
+ Shared tooling and expertise
- More work up front
- May hit limits sooner
%%

At Etsy, the activity feed was built on the existing PHP, MySQL, Memcached and Gearman (a job queue) stack instead of adopting Redis, which looked like a better fit. It was harder at first. But because it ran on shared, well-understood infrastructure that every engineer knew how to operate, it grew about 20 times without dedicated attention. Etsy did adopt Solr for faceted search, because doing that on MySQL was impractical. The rule is not "never add anything"; it is "make each addition earn its place".

Before adding a technology, ask:
- **How would we solve this without anything new?** If the honest answer is "we could, but someone wants to try the new thing", stop.
- **Write down exactly what makes the current stack too costly** for this problem, with numbers.
- **If it replaces something, commit to migrating fully off the old thing.** Two systems doing the same job is worse than either alone.

## Your existing stack can do more than you think
Many components that teams reach for have a "good enough" version inside tools they already run:
- **Queue**: a Postgres table with `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` lets many workers each claim different jobs without blocking each other. It handles hundreds to a few thousand jobs per second, and the job is enqueued in the same transaction as the business change, which a separate broker can't give you.
- **Cache**: the database's own buffer cache already keeps hot rows in memory. A well-indexed query on a cached page often takes under a millisecond.
- **Search**: Postgres full-text search with a GIN index covers simple search over millions of rows.
- **Pub/sub**: Postgres `LISTEN/NOTIFY` or Redis pub/sub for low-volume notifications.
- **Scheduled jobs**: cron, or a scheduler library, before a workflow engine.

These hit limits eventually, and when they do you will have the numbers that justify the dedicated tool.

## Single machines are very powerful
Two well-known experiments show how far one machine goes.

**235 times faster than Hadoop.** One experiment reproduced a job that counted chess game results (white wins, black wins, draws) from about 2 million games. A 7-node Hadoop cluster took about 26 minutes. A shell pipeline on a laptop (`find | xargs -P4 mawk ...`) took about 12 seconds, on twice as much data. It won by streaming the data line by line with almost no memory, using all cores through parallel processes, and skipping the cluster's job startup, scheduling and shuffle overhead.

**Scalability, but at what COST?** Another experiment compared big graph-processing systems (Spark, Giraph, GraphLab, GraphX), running on 128 cores, against a single-threaded program on a laptop. For PageRank and connected components on graphs with billions of edges, the single thread was often faster than all of them. With a better algorithm (union-find instead of label propagation) and a memory-friendly data layout, connectivity dropped to 15 to 30 seconds, against hundreds or thousands of seconds for the clusters. The point: a system can "scale well" (get faster as you add machines) and still be inefficient, because it started from a very slow baseline. The metric that came out of it is **COST**, the "configuration that outperforms a single thread": how many cores a distributed system needs before it beats one good thread. For some systems the answer was hundreds; for some, never.

%%bars Time to run the same chess-results job, in seconds. A laptop pipeline beat a 7-node cluster by over 100 times.
7-node Hadoop cluster | 1560 | ~26 minutes
*Laptop shell pipeline | 12 | ~12 seconds
%%

Today a single cloud server can have 192 cores, 1 to 2 TB of RAM and tens of TB of NVMe storage. Many companies' entire datasets fit on one such machine. Before you design a distributed system, check whether you have a distributed problem.

## Know your numbers first
Before adding infrastructure, you need two pieces of evidence:
1. **The load**: current traffic and data size (requests per second at peak, writes per second, dataset size), and its growth rate.
2. **The bottleneck**: where time or capacity is actually going, measured, not guessed. Database CPU at 85%? p99 query time of 400 ms on one endpoint? Disk I/O saturated during batch jobs?

With those two, the decision is usually obvious:
- If Postgres serves your peak at 20% CPU with good p99s, a cache adds risk (staleness, invalidation bugs, a new failure mode) without benefit.
- If the whole dataset fits in RAM on one box, a distributed system may add only overhead.
- If one endpoint is slow because of a missing index, the fix is the index, not a cache in front of the slow query.
- If growth is 5% a month and you are at 30% of capacity, you have roughly two years before it becomes urgent.

%%tree A decision path before adding a cache, queue or new datastore. Most branches end without new infrastructure.
Is there a measured problem?
  No | stop: nothing to fix
  Yes: what is the bottleneck?
    Slow query | add an index or fix the query
    CPU or IO saturated
      Will scaling up fix it? | bigger instance first
      *Still short at peak plus growth | add the component, with numbers
    Work too slow for the request | queue, or a table plus workers
%%

! Example: do we need a cache?
! A product API peaks at 3,000 requests per second. Each request makes two indexed queries. Postgres, on 16 vCPUs, sits at 25% CPU at peak, and p99 query time is 4 ms. Traffic grows 4% a month.
! Headroom: at 25% CPU, the database could take about three times the load before reaching a comfortable 75%. At 4% monthly growth, tripling takes about 28 months (1.04²⁸ ≈ 3).
! Conclusion: no cache now. A cache would save a few milliseconds per request and add invalidation logic and a new on-call component. Revisit when CPU at peak passes 50%, or if a requirement asks for sub-millisecond reads.

! Example: do we need Kafka?
! A team wants Kafka to send welcome emails after sign-up. Sign-ups peak at 20 per second.
! A `jobs` table, with the job inserted in the same transaction as the new user and workers claiming rows with `FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED`, handles that easily, needs no new system, and never loses a job when the broker and database disagree, because there is no broker.
! Kafka becomes worth it if they later need several teams consuming the same event stream, replay of history, or tens of thousands of events per second. That is a real requirement, so they write it down as the trigger for revisiting.

## When adding is right
Add the component when the numbers and requirements demand it:
- Reads exceed what the database can serve even after indexing and a bigger instance, and the data tolerates staleness: **add a cache**.
- Work is slow, bursty or must survive a downstream outage: **add a queue**.
- Search needs relevance ranking, facets and typo tolerance over large text: **add a search engine**.
- Writes or data size exceed the largest single machine: **partition the database**.

In each case, also plan who will operate it, how you will monitor it, and what happens when it fails.

## Common mistakes
- **Résumé-driven design**: choosing technology because it is interesting.
- **Adding a cache to hide a missing index.**
- **Designing for a scale ten thousand times the current load** "just in case".
- **Running two systems that do the same job** because the migration was never finished.
- **Benchmarking a distributed system only against itself**, never against a single well-written machine.

## In an interview
Interviewers often push you to scale. That is fine, but show your reasoning: estimate the load first, say what one machine or one database can handle, and add a component only when the numbers exceed it. "At 2,000 reads per second one Postgres primary with replicas is enough; if we reach 50,000, we'd add a Redis cache for product pages" shows judgement. Asking "What is the current peak load and what is the bottleneck?" is a strong question in a real design review.

> Takeaway: default to the tools you already run well, because operating a component costs far more than building with it. Add new infrastructure only when measured load and a measured bottleneck show the current stack can't cope, and check that the new system beats one well-written machine.

?? Know the current load and its growth (for example peak requests or writes per second, data size, and monthly growth rate), and where the bottleneck actually is, measured (for example database CPU at peak, or the p99 time of the slow query). Without both you can't show the new component solves a real problem, or that a simpler fix such as an index or a bigger instance wouldn't.
