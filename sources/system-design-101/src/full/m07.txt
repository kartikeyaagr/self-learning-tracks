## What a storage engine has to survive
A **storage engine** is the part of a database that turns "put this key" and "get that key" into bytes on a disk, and back again. Everything above it (the query planner, the network protocol, replication) assumes the engine keeps its promises: a value you wrote can be read back, and a write that was acknowledged survives a crash.

The obvious first design is to keep all the data in one file and rewrite it whenever something changes. That works for a settings file. It falls apart the moment the data is large, the writes are frequent, or the power goes off at the wrong instant. This module builds up, step by step, the designs real databases use instead. This first lesson sets out the two forces that shape every one of them:
- **The disk is slow, and it has rules.** It rewards large, sequential operations and punishes small, scattered ones. The penalty is huge on spinning disks and smaller, but still real, on SSDs.
- **The process can crash at any instant.** That includes halfway through writing a single page. The engine must come back up in a consistent state no matter where the crash landed.

Every engine you will meet in this module (B-trees, Bitcask, LSM trees, the append-only stream layer of a cloud store) is a different answer to the same question: how do you turn random updates into sequential writes, without losing anything when you crash?

## The memory and storage hierarchy
Before the disk, look at the whole ladder. Each step down is bigger, cheaper per byte, and much slower. An engine's job is largely to keep the hot data high on the ladder and to visit the bottom rungs as rarely, and as efficiently, as possible.

%%layers The storage hierarchy, fastest at the top. Each step down is roughly 10 to 1,000 times slower but far cheaper per byte.
CPU cache | about 1 to 10 ns, megabytes
*RAM (page cache, memtables) | about 100 ns, tens to hundreds of GB
NVMe SSD | about 20 to 100 µs per random read, terabytes
SATA SSD | about 100 to 200 µs per random read
Hard disk (HDD) | about 5 to 10 ms per random read, tens of TB
Object storage over network | about 10 to 100 ms per request, effectively unlimited
%%

Put the numbers side by side and the shape of the problem is obvious. A random read from RAM is roughly 100,000 times faster than a random read from a hard disk. An engine that does one unnecessary disk seek per request has thrown away the speed of everything else in the stack.

%%bars Approximate latency of one small random read at each level. Note the log scale: the gap between RAM and a hard disk is about five orders of magnitude.

## Why sequential beats random on a hard disk
A hard disk is a stack of spinning platters with a mechanical arm that moves a read/write head across them. To read a block at a random location the drive must:
1. **Seek**: move the arm to the right track. Typically 4 to 9 ms.
2. **Wait for rotation**: wait for the right sector to spin under the head. At 7,200 rpm one revolution takes 8.3 ms, so the average wait is about 4.2 ms.
3. **Transfer**: actually read the bytes. At 200 MB/s, a 4 KB block takes about 0.02 ms.

Look at those three numbers. Moving the data is almost free; getting to it costs about 10 ms. That fixed cost is why a hard drive manages only about **100 to 200 random operations per second** (call it 120), and that figure has barely changed in twenty years. Since IBM's first disk in 1956, capacity has grown about 7.2 million times, but seek time has improved only about 150 times, because arms and platters are mechanical and physics does not scale like transistors.

A sequential read pays the seek and rotation once, then streams at full bandwidth. Reading 100 MB sequentially takes about half a second. Reading the same 100 MB as 25,000 random 4 KB blocks takes about 25,000 × 10 ms = 250 seconds. Same bytes, 500 times slower.

! Example: random versus sequential on one hard disk
! A drive does 120 random I/Os per second and streams 200 MB/s sequentially. With random 4 KB writes it can absorb 120 × 4 KB = 480 KB/s of updates. If the same updates are appended to a log in 1 MB batches, each batch costs one positioning (about 10 ms) plus 5 ms of transfer, so about 66 batches a second, or 66 MB/s.
! That is roughly 140 times more write throughput from identical hardware, purely by changing the access pattern. This one calculation explains why log-structured engines exist.

### Drives keep getting bigger, not faster
Because capacity grows and random I/O does not, the number of random operations per terabyte keeps falling. A 4 TB drive with 120 IOPS offers 30 IOPS per TB; a 26 TB drive with the same 120 IOPS offers under 5 IOPS per TB. Large storage services treat that as their central constraint: data that is rarely touched can live on huge drives, but hot data has to be spread over many drives so that no single one is asked for more than it can deliver. Lesson 07.14 shows how this "heat" problem shapes S3.

## SSDs: smaller gap, different reasons
An SSD has no moving parts, so there is no seek. A random 4 KB read on a modern NVMe drive takes roughly 20 to 100 µs, and the drive can serve hundreds of thousands of them per second if you keep many in flight. So why do storage engines still care about sequential writes on SSDs? Because of how flash memory works underneath.
- **Pages and blocks.** Flash is read and written in **pages** (typically 4 to 16 KB) but can only be erased in much larger **erase blocks** (often 1 to 4 MB, hundreds of pages).
- **No overwrite in place.** A page that holds data cannot be rewritten until its whole erase block is erased. So when you "overwrite" 4 KB, the drive's firmware (the **flash translation layer**, or FTL) writes the new data to a fresh page somewhere else, updates its internal map from logical address to physical page, and marks the old page as stale.
- **Garbage collection.** Eventually the drive runs short of fresh pages. It picks an erase block that is partly stale, copies its still-valid pages elsewhere, then erases the block. Those copies are extra writes the drive does on its own, called **device-level write amplification**.
- **Wear.** Each erase block survives a limited number of erase cycles (a few thousand for consumer TLC flash). Extra internal copying uses up the drive's life faster.

Now compare two workloads. Small random overwrites scatter stale pages across every erase block, so when garbage collection runs, each victim block still has many live pages to copy. Large sequential writes fill whole erase blocks with data that tends to become stale together (for example, a whole old log file deleted at once), so garbage collection finds blocks that are entirely stale and erases them with no copying at all.

%%compare What the SSD's garbage collector faces after two different write patterns. Sequential, whole-file writes leave erase blocks that die together.
[Small random overwrites]
Stale pages scattered in every block
Each victim block still has live pages
- GC copies live pages before erasing
- Device write amplification 2x to 5x
- Latency spikes when GC runs
[*Large sequential writes]
Whole blocks filled by one file
File deleted, block entirely stale
+ GC erases with no copying
+ Device write amplification near 1x
+ Steadier latency, longer drive life
%%

There are two more effects. The operating system and the drive both **prefetch** (read ahead) and **merge** sequential requests, so a sequential stream turns into fewer, larger I/Os down the stack. And a big sequential request lets the drive spread the work across its many internal flash chips in parallel. On an SSD, sequential writes might be "only" two to five times better than random ones, rather than a hundred times, but they also make the drive last longer and behave more predictably.

## Durability is a contract with the operating system
The second force is crashes. Here is the trap almost everyone falls into the first time: calling `write()` does **not** put bytes on the disk. It copies them into the **page cache**, a region of RAM the operating system uses to buffer file data, and returns at once. The kernel writes those "dirty" pages to the disk later, in its own time, typically within about 30 seconds. If the machine loses power before that, the data is gone, even though your program was told the write succeeded.

To be sure, you call `fsync(fd)` (or `fdatasync`). It blocks until the kernel has sent the file's dirty pages to the device and the device reports them on stable storage. fsync is the **only durability barrier** you get from a normal filesystem. Nothing is durable until it returns.

%%seq A write is only durable once fsync returns. Before that it sits in the page cache in RAM and a power cut loses it.
App > OS page cache : write(fd, 4 KB)
OS page cache -> App : returns at once (not durable)
App > OS page cache : fsync(fd)
OS page cache > Disk : flush dirty pages + cache flush
Disk -> OS page cache : on stable storage
OS page cache -> App : fsync returns (durable)
%%

fsync is slow. On a hard disk it costs at least one rotation, several milliseconds. On a consumer SSD it might take 1 to 10 ms, because the drive must flush its own volatile cache. Datacentre SSDs with power-loss protection (capacitors that keep the drive alive long enough to save its cache) can acknowledge in tens of microseconds. Either way, you cannot afford one fsync per tiny update at high rates, so engines use **group commit**: collect many updates from many clients, write them together, fsync once, and then acknowledge all of them.

! Example: group commit arithmetic
! A service receives 50,000 small writes a second, and an fsync on its SSD takes 2 ms. One fsync per write would need 50,000 × 2 ms = 100 seconds of fsync time per second: impossible. With group commit, the engine fsyncs every 2 ms and each fsync covers whatever arrived in that window, about 100 writes.
! That is 500 fsyncs a second, each write waits at most about 4 ms (the current window plus the fsync), and every acknowledged write is durable. The cost is a few milliseconds of latency per write, paid for with a large gain in throughput.

### Torn writes and lying hardware
A crash can also land in the middle of a write. A database page is often 8 or 16 KB, but a disk only guarantees that a single sector (512 bytes or 4 KB) is written atomically. If power fails while a 16 KB page is being written, the page on disk can be part old and part new: a **torn write** (or torn page). An engine has to detect this, usually with a **checksum** stored in each page or record, and repair it, usually from a log. Lesson 07.03 shows the standard techniques.

Some hardware also lies: cheap drives may report a write as complete while it still sits in a volatile cache. Engines cannot fix that, which is why production databases run on drives with power-loss protection and why "fsync returned" is the strongest promise software can get.

## Common mistakes
- **Treating `write()` as durable.** Acknowledging a client after `write()` but before fsync loses data on power failure. It works perfectly in every test, because tests rarely pull the plug.
- **One fsync per operation.** Correct, but caps you at a few hundred or a few thousand writes a second. Batch with group commit instead.
- **Assuming SSDs make access patterns irrelevant.** Random small overwrites still cost device write amplification, wear and latency spikes.
- **Forgetting the directory.** Creating or renaming a file changes the directory, and that change also needs an fsync of the directory itself (lesson 07.03).
- **Ignoring torn pages.** Without checksums, a half-written page is silently read back as valid data.

## In an interview
When storage comes up, show that you know where the time goes. Useful lines: "A hard disk does about 120 random I/Os a second but streams 200 MB/s, so I want writes to be sequential." "Nothing is durable until fsync, so I'll batch commits." "On SSDs, sequential writes keep garbage collection cheap and extend drive life." Then connect it to a design choice: an append-only log for the write path, an in-memory index for reads, and periodic compaction. Asking "what's the write rate and the durability requirement?" tells the interviewer you know those two numbers decide the engine.

! Example: the counter file
! A service stores a counter by overwriting the 8 bytes at offset 0 about 2,000 times a second, with an fsync after each write. Each overwrite is a random write plus an fsync, so the disk, not the CPU, caps throughput, and on an SSD every overwrite also creates a stale flash page.
! Switching to appending each increment to a log and fsyncing once per batch of 100 turns the work into sequential writes and only 20 fsyncs a second. A crash loses nothing that was acknowledged, because acknowledgements wait for the batch's fsync, and the latest value is simply the last record in the log.

> Takeaway: disks reward large sequential I/O and punish small random I/O, and nothing is durable until fsync returns. Every storage engine is a way to turn random updates into sequential writes, batch the fsyncs, and survive a crash in the middle of a write.

?? A random I/O pays a fixed cost before any data moves. On a hard disk that is a seek plus half a rotation, about 10 ms, against microseconds to transfer 4 KB, so a drive does only about 120 random operations a second but streams around 200 MB/s. Large sequential writes fill and later free whole blocks, and the OS and drive can prefetch, merge and parallelise them into large requests.

## Two families
Lesson 07.01 showed the two forces every storage engine must deal with: disks hate small random writes, and crashes can strike mid-write. Almost every engine in production answers them in one of two ways:
- **B-tree (update in place)**: data lives in fixed-size pages organised as a balanced tree. An update finds the page that holds the key and rewrites that page. PostgreSQL, MySQL's InnoDB, SQL Server, Oracle and SQLite work this way.
- **LSM tree (log-structured merge tree)**: writes go to an in-memory buffer and a log, then are flushed as sorted, immutable files that are merged in the background. RocksDB, LevelDB, Cassandra, ScyllaDB, HBase and many time-series databases work this way.

Both keep data sorted by key, so both support point lookups and range scans. The difference is **when** the sorting work happens. A B-tree pays at write time, by putting each key in its final place immediately. An LSM tree pays later, by appending now and sorting in large background merges. That single choice ripples into every other property: write throughput, read latency, space use, and how the engine behaves under load.

## The B-tree, page by page
A B-tree (strictly, the variant almost every database uses is a **B+ tree**) is built from **pages**, fixed-size blocks of typically 4, 8 or 16 KB. PostgreSQL uses 8 KB, InnoDB 16 KB. A page is the unit the engine reads from and writes to disk.

Two structures work together:
- **Leaf pages** hold the actual entries, sorted by key. In a secondary index an entry is the key plus a pointer to the row (a row ID or the primary key). In a clustered index, like InnoDB's primary key, the leaf holds the whole row. Each leaf also points to the previous and next leaf, forming a **doubly linked list**, so a range scan can walk sideways along the leaves forwards or backwards without going back up the tree.
- **Branch (internal) pages** sit above the leaves. Each entry holds a separator key and a pointer to a child page. A lookup starts at the **root**, picks the child whose key range covers the search key, and repeats until it reaches a leaf.

The tree is **balanced**: every leaf is the same distance from the root. That is guaranteed by how inserts work. When a leaf is full, it **splits** into two half-full pages and a new separator key is pushed into the parent. If the parent is full it splits too, and occasionally the split reaches the root, which splits and makes the tree one level taller. The tree grows from the top, so it never becomes lopsided. (The old advice to "rebuild unbalanced indexes" is a myth for this reason; a rebuild can reclaim space from half-empty pages, but balance is never the problem.)

%%tree A three-level B+ tree. Branch pages hold separator keys; only leaves hold entries, and leaves are linked for range scans.
*Root page | keys: 400, 800
  Branch: keys below 400 | 100, 200, 300
    Leaf 1 to 99 | sorted entries, next/prev links
    Leaf 100 to 199
    Leaf 200 to 299
    Leaf 300 to 399
  Branch: 400 to 799 | 500, 600, 700
    *Leaf 500 to 599 | lookup for 557 ends here
    Leaf 600 to 699
  Branch: 800 and up | 900, 1000
    Leaf 800 to 899
%%

### Why the tree is so shallow
The trick is the **fan-out**: how many children each branch page has. An 8 KB page holding 16-byte keys plus 8-byte pointers fits roughly 300 entries. So:
- 1 level: 300 leaves.
- 2 levels: 300 × 300 = 90,000 leaves.
- 3 levels: 27 million leaves.
- 4 levels: about 8 billion leaves.

Depth grows logarithmically, which is why real-world indexes with millions or billions of rows are only three to five levels deep. Better still, the root and branch pages are few and constantly used, so they stay in the buffer pool (the database's page cache). A point lookup often costs just **one disk read**, for the leaf.

! Example: sizing a B-tree index
! A table has 500 million rows. Its index on an 8-byte `user_id` stores key plus 8-byte row pointer, about 20 bytes per entry with overhead, so about 400 entries per 8 KB leaf when full. Pages are typically about 70% full after random inserts, so about 280 per leaf: 500M / 280 ≈ 1.8 million leaf pages, about 14 GB.
! Branch fan-out is also a few hundred, so 1.8 million leaves need about 6,000 branch pages (50 MB), which need about 20 pages above them, then one root. That is 4 levels. The top three levels total about 50 MB and live in RAM, so a lookup costs one leaf read from disk, about 0.1 ms on an SSD.

### Three steps of an indexed query
An indexed lookup really has three steps: (1) the tree traversal down to the first matching leaf, (2) following the leaf chain sideways while keys still match, and (3) fetching each matching table row. Only the first is bounded by the tree depth. The second and third can touch many pages, which is why a query can be slow even when it uses an index: an index range that matches 50,000 rows scattered across the table can cost 50,000 random page reads.

### The cost of B-tree writes
B-trees are excellent at reads. The price is on writes:
- **Whole-page writes.** Changing a 100-byte row rewrites a whole 8 or 16 KB page. That is a write amplification of 80 to 160 times for that update, before any logging.
- **Random writes.** The pages to update are wherever the keys happen to live, scattered across the file.
- **Double writing for crash safety.** Because a page write can be torn by a crash, the engine first records the change in a **write-ahead log** (WAL) and may write full page images to it (PostgreSQL's full-page writes, InnoDB's doublewrite buffer). So each change is written at least twice.
- **Fragmentation.** Splits leave pages half full, and deletes leave holes, so a B-tree typically uses 1.3 to 1.5 times the space of its data.

Buffering helps. A modified page stays dirty in memory and many updates to it can be written out together at a checkpoint. But with a working set much bigger than RAM and keys spread randomly, the engine ends up doing one random page write for nearly every update.

## The LSM tree in brief
An LSM tree refuses to update anything in place. A write is appended to a write-ahead log (for crash safety) and inserted into a **memtable**, a sorted structure in RAM. When the memtable reaches a size limit (64 MB by default in RocksDB), it is written out in one sequential pass as a **sorted string table** (SSTable, or SST file): an immutable file of key-value pairs sorted by key, with an index. Over time many SST files pile up, arranged in **levels**, and a background process called **compaction** merges them, keeping the newest value of each key and discarding overwritten values and deletion markers. Lessons 07.07 to 07.12 take each piece apart.

%%compare The same single-row update on each engine. The B-tree rewrites a page where the key lives; the LSM appends and sorts later in bulk.
[B-tree]
Update -(find leaf)-> Read 8 KB page -(modify)-> Rewrite page in place
+ Read path stays short: one place per key
- Random page write per update
- Full page rewritten for a few bytes
[LSM tree]
Update -(append)-> WAL + memtable -(flush later)-> Sorted file
+ Writes are sequential and batched
+ Compresses well, gentle on SSDs
- Reads may check several files
- Compaction rewrites data in background
%%

Writes are cheap and sequential: a write costs a log append and a memory insert, and the flush writes tens of megabytes at once. Reads are the price. The newest version of a key could be in the memtable or in any of several files, so a read may need to look in several places. That is why LSM engines lean on **Bloom filters** (a compact in-memory structure that says "this key is definitely not in this file") and a block cache (lesson 07.11).

## Comparing them properly
The honest comparison is in terms of three costs, which lesson 07.12 defines carefully: **read amplification** (disk reads per logical read), **write amplification** (bytes written to disk per byte the application wrote) and **space amplification** (disk used divided by live data size). No engine wins all three.

%%matrix How the two families compare on the properties that usually decide the choice. "partial" means it depends heavily on tuning.
Property | B-tree | LSM tree
Point read latency | yes, one path | partial, filters needed
Range scan | yes, walk leaves | partial, merge several runs
Write throughput | partial, random pages | yes, sequential
Space efficiency | partial, half-full pages | yes, compressed, compacted
Predictable latency | yes, mostly | partial, compaction spikes
SSD wear | partial, page rewrites | yes, large writes
Transactions, locking | yes, mature | partial, engine-specific
%%

Some details behind that grid:
- **Compression.** LSM files are written once and never modified, so they can be compressed in large blocks. A B-tree page must leave room for future inserts and is rewritten in place, which makes compression awkward. Facebook moved its main MySQL user database from InnoDB to MyRocks (MySQL on RocksDB) largely for this: storage use roughly halved.
- **Write amplification is not one-sided.** An LSM rewrites data during compaction, often 10 to 30 times with leveled compaction. But those rewrites are large and sequential, while a B-tree's page writes are small and random. On SSDs the LSM's bytes are cheaper bytes.
- **Tail latency.** A B-tree's cost is paid inline, so it is steady. An LSM's cost is deferred, and if compaction falls behind, the engine must slow or stall incoming writes to catch up. That shows up as latency spikes at the 99.9th percentile.
- **Transactions.** B-tree engines have decades of work on locking, MVCC (multi-version concurrency control) and recovery. LSM engines support transactions too (RocksDB, CockroachDB's Pebble, TiKV), but the B-tree path is more mature.

! Example: metrics ingestion versus user accounts
! A monitoring system writes 500,000 data points a second, 100 bytes each: 50 MB/s of raw writes, keyed by series and timestamp, and mostly reads recent windows. A B-tree with a working set far bigger than RAM would need to modify hundreds of thousands of scattered leaf pages a second, far beyond what the disks can do.
! An LSM engine absorbs the writes in the memtable, flushes a 64 MB sorted file every 1.3 seconds, and compresses the repetitive numeric data four to ten times. Compaction keeps reads bounded. The same company's user-account table (10 million rows, 50 writes a second, 20,000 lookups a second, needs foreign keys and transactions) stays in PostgreSQL, where every lookup is one buffered root-to-leaf walk.

## Common mistakes
- **"LSM is for writes, B-tree is for reads" as the whole answer.** True as a first cut, but say why: random in-place page writes versus sequential appends, and one place to look versus several.
- **Ignoring the working set.** If the whole B-tree fits in RAM, random writes are absorbed by the buffer pool and checkpointed in bulk, and the difference shrinks a lot.
- **Forgetting compaction is real work.** An LSM's write throughput is limited by how fast compaction can keep up, not by how fast the log can append.
- **Choosing an exotic engine for a small workload.** At a few thousand writes a second, PostgreSQL is fine and far easier to operate.

## In an interview
State the trade in one line, then tie it to numbers from the prompt: "This is 200,000 writes a second of append-mostly events, so I'd use an LSM-based store like Cassandra or RocksDB: writes become sequential appends and compress well. Reads are mostly by recent time range, which the LSM serves from the newest levels." For a transactional system with many reads and joins, say the opposite and pick PostgreSQL or MySQL. Good questions to ask: read/write ratio, value size, whether data is overwritten or only appended, and how strict the p99 latency target is.

> Takeaway: B-trees keep data sorted in place, so reads are one short path down the tree but every update is a random page write. LSM trees append now and sort later in big background merges, so writes are sequential and compact but reads may check several files and compaction competes for disk. Choose by read/write mix, data size and how much latency jitter you can accept.

?? Choose an LSM tree when the workload is write-heavy or the data is large: logging, metrics, time series, event stores, messaging, feeds. It turns every write into a sequential log append and memory insert, flushes large sorted files, compresses well (immutable files) and is gentle on SSDs, so it sustains far higher write rates than a B-tree doing random page rewrites. The price is reads that may check several files (mitigated by Bloom filters and caching), background compaction I/O and occasional latency spikes. Choose a B-tree when reads, especially point lookups and range scans with predictable latency, dominate, or when you need mature transactional behaviour.

## The problem
The simplest possible database is a dictionary (a map from keys to values) saved to a file. Your program loads the file at startup, keeps the dictionary in memory, and writes it back whenever something changes. Many real tools start exactly like this: config files, desktop app state, small caches, a CLI's list of saved credentials.

The question this lesson answers is deceptively hard: **how do you update that file so that a crash, at any instant, never leaves it half-written?** The answer introduces three ideas that every later engine in this module reuses: write somewhere new then switch atomically, append instead of overwrite, and detect damage with checksums.

## The naive version and how it breaks
The obvious code opens the file for writing (which truncates it to zero length), writes the new contents and closes it. Consider where a crash can land:
- **After truncating, before writing.** The file is now empty. Every setting is lost, including the ones that weren't changing.
- **In the middle of writing.** The file holds the first part of the new contents and nothing after it, or (if the filesystem reuses blocks) a mix of new and old bytes. JSON or binary data in that state is usually unreadable.
- **After writing, before the OS flushes.** As lesson 07.01 explained, `write()` only fills the page cache. Without `fsync`, a power failure can leave the file empty or partly written even though your program finished.
- **A concurrent reader** (another process, a backup job) can open the file while it is half written and read garbage, even without any crash.

The core mistake is **modifying the only copy**. During the update, there is a window in which neither the old version nor the new version exists in full on disk.

## The rename trick
The standard fix is to never touch the live file. Build the complete new version beside it, make sure it is on disk, then swap it in with one atomic operation.
1. Write the full new contents to a temporary file **in the same directory**, for example `data.tmp`.
2. Call `fsync` on the temporary file, so its bytes are on stable storage.
3. Call `rename("data.tmp", "data")`. On POSIX filesystems, rename within one filesystem is **atomic**: any process that opens `data` sees either the complete old file or the complete new file, never a mix, and there is no moment when `data` does not exist.
4. Call `fsync` on the **directory**. The rename changed the directory's list of names, and that change is itself only in memory until the directory is synced. Skip this and a crash can bring back the old name after you told the user the save succeeded.

%%seq The rename trick: readers keep seeing the old file until one atomic rename swaps in the complete new one.
App > Disk : write data.tmp (full new contents)
App > Disk : fsync(data.tmp)
Disk -> App : temp file durable
App > Disk : rename(data.tmp, data)
App > Disk : fsync(directory)
Disk -> App : new name durable
%%

Why "same directory"? Because rename is only atomic within one filesystem. If the temp file lives in `/tmp` and that is a different filesystem (often a RAM-backed one), rename fails or the library falls back to copy-then-delete, which reintroduces the half-written window.

### Walking every crash point
The test of a crash-safety design is to stop the world at each step and ask what you would find on reboot.

%%timeline What a reboot finds if power fails at each point of the rename trick. Every outcome is either the full old file or the full new one.
Before write | data = old version, no temp file
During temp write | data = old, data.tmp partial: delete it
After fsync(tmp) | data = old, data.tmp complete: delete or use it
After rename, before dir fsync | data = old or new, both complete
After dir fsync | *data = new version, durable
%%

At no point is `data` partial. The worst case is a stray `data.tmp`, which startup code simply deletes. This pattern is used everywhere: editors saving files, package managers installing, Git updating refs (it writes `ref.lock` then renames it), and databases writing new manifest files.

! Example: saving settings safely
! An app saves `settings.json` (4 KB). It writes `settings.json.tmp`, calls fsync (about 2 ms on an SSD), renames it to `settings.json`, then fsyncs the directory (another 1 to 2 ms). The save costs about 4 ms.
! Power fails 1 ms after the rename. On reboot, the directory change may or may not have reached disk, so the app finds either the old or the new `settings.json`, both complete, and perhaps a leftover `.tmp` it deletes. The user may lose the very last change, but never all their settings, and the app was not yet claiming the save had succeeded, because it acknowledges only after the directory fsync.

### The cost: rewriting everything
The rename trick makes each update atomic, but it rewrites the whole file every time. Changing one key in a 1 GB dictionary means writing 1 GB, plus two fsyncs. At 500 MB/s that is two seconds per update. That is fine for a settings file and hopeless for a database. To go further you need updates whose cost is proportional to the change, not to the file.

## Append-only logs
The next step is to stop rewriting and start **appending**. Each update becomes a small record added to the end of a log file: `set user:42 = {...}` or `delete user:17`. To rebuild the dictionary, you start empty and replay every record in order; later records override earlier ones.

This is a big improvement:
- **Writes are small and sequential.** An update costs one append, regardless of how big the dictionary is.
- **Old data is never touched.** A crash can only damage the record being appended at the end of the file (the tail). Everything before it was complete and fsynced earlier.
- **Batching is natural.** Append many records, fsync once, acknowledge them all (group commit, from lesson 07.01).

### Detecting a torn tail
A crash can still leave a **torn** final record: only some of its bytes reached the disk. Worse, a filesystem may extend the file's length before the data blocks are written, so the tail can be full of zeros or old garbage. The reader must be able to tell a good record from a bad one. So each record is framed:

%%layers The framing of one log record. The length says where the record ends; the checksum proves its bytes are exactly what was written.
Length | 4 bytes: size of the payload
*CRC32C | 4 bytes: checksum of the payload
Payload | key size, key, value size, value, or a delete marker
%%

A **checksum** is a short number computed from the bytes (CRC32C is the common choice: fast, often hardware accelerated, and good at catching the burst errors disks produce). On recovery the reader goes record by record: read the length, read that many bytes, recompute the checksum and compare. The first record whose checksum fails, or whose length runs past the end of the file, marks the end of valid data. Everything from there on is discarded and the file is truncated to the last good record.

%%flow Recovery replays records until the first one whose checksum fails, then truncates the torn tail.
Record 1 OK -> Record 2 OK -> Record 3 OK -> *Record 4 CRC fails -(truncate here)-> Resume appending
%%

Is it safe to throw that record away? Yes, if the engine follows one rule: **acknowledge a write only after the fsync that covers it returns**. A torn record was by definition never fully fsynced, so no client was told it succeeded. Dropping it loses nothing that was promised.

! Example: replaying a log after a crash
! A dictionary log has 1.2 million records, 180 MB. The process is killed while appending record 1,200,001. On restart, the reader scans the file at about 1 GB/s: about 0.2 seconds of reading. Record 1,200,001's length field says 312 bytes but only 90 remain before end of file, so the reader stops, truncates those 90 bytes, and has rebuilt the dictionary from 1,200,000 records.
! The client that sent record 1,200,001 never got an acknowledgement, so it retries. Everything that was acknowledged is present.

### The log grows forever: compaction
The log has a new problem. If a key is updated 10,000 times, the log holds 10,000 records for it, and only the last matters. The file keeps growing, and startup replay gets slower. The fix is **compaction**: periodically write a fresh file containing just the current value of each live key (using the rename trick to swap it in), then delete the old log. Many systems do this as a **snapshot plus log**: a compact snapshot of the whole state, and a short log of changes made since. Recovery loads the snapshot then replays the short log. Redis does exactly this with its RDB snapshots and append-only file, and the same idea, refined, becomes Bitcask (07.08) and the LSM tree (07.09).

%%compare Three ways to persist a dictionary. Each step fixes the previous one's flaw and introduces the next problem to solve.
[Overwrite in place]
Truncate, write, close
- Crash leaves empty or partial file
- Readers can see half a file
[Write temp, then rename]
Write tmp -(fsync)-> rename -> fsync dir
+ Always old or new, never mixed
- Rewrites whole file per update
[*Append-only log]
Append record -(group fsync)-> ack
+ Cost per update is tiny
+ Only the tail can tear
- Needs checksums and compaction
%%

## Building up to a real engine
The path from here to a database is short. Replace "load the whole file into memory" with a **pager**: a component that reads and writes the file in fixed-size pages (say 4 KB) and caches the hot ones. Organise those pages as a B-tree so you can find a key without reading everything. Add a write-ahead log so page updates can be made crash safe. That is essentially SQLite, and every step uses the techniques in this lesson.

## Common mistakes
- **Skipping fsync.** The code works in every test and loses data on the first real power cut.
- **Forgetting the directory fsync.** The rename is not durable until the directory is synced.
- **Temp file on another filesystem.** Rename across filesystems is not atomic.
- **No checksum on log records.** A torn tail is read back as a valid but corrupt record.
- **Acknowledging before fsync.** Then truncating a torn tail can drop a write the client thinks succeeded.
- **Stopping at the first bad record in the middle of a log** without asking why. A bad record followed by good ones is not a torn tail; it is corruption, and should raise an alarm rather than silently discard data after it.

## In an interview
If a design keeps state in files (a coordinator's metadata, a checkpoint, a leader's term number), say how it is updated: "write a temp file, fsync, rename, fsync the directory" for small state, or "an append-only log with length and CRC per record, truncated at the first bad record on recovery" for frequent updates. Mention that writes are acknowledged only after the covering fsync. It is a short sentence that shows you understand durability rather than assume it.

> Takeaway: never modify the only copy of data a crash must not corrupt. Either write a complete new file and atomically rename it into place (fsyncing the file and the directory), or append length-and-checksum framed records to a log, acknowledge only after fsync, and on recovery cut the log at the first record that fails its checksum.

?? Never modify the live file in place. Write the complete new version to a temporary file in the same directory, fsync it, atomically rename it over the original (POSIX rename within one filesystem is atomic, so readers see the old or new file, never a mix), then fsync the directory so the rename itself is durable; a crash at any point leaves either the full old file or the full new one, plus at most a stray temp file. For frequent small updates, append records framed with a length and a checksum to a log, acknowledge only after the covering fsync, and on recovery replay until the first record that fails its checksum and truncate that torn tail.

## Why design a binary format at all
Sooner or later every storage engine has to decide exactly which bytes go into its files: log records, index pages, data blocks, headers. You could use text, such as JSON or CSV. Text is easy to read in a terminal and easy to debug, but it is a poor choice for the core of an engine:
- **It is large.** The number 1700000000 takes 10 characters as text but fits in 4 bytes as a binary integer. Field names repeat in every record.
- **It is slow to parse.** To find the fifth field of a JSON record you must scan every byte before it, handling quotes, escapes and nesting. A binary format with fixed sizes or explicit lengths lets you jump straight to it.
- **It is ambiguous.** Is `1.10` a number or a version string? What character encoding is this? Binary formats state their types and byte order explicitly.

But binary has a cost too. You cannot eyeball a file and see what's wrong with it, and a reader that misunderstands the layout will happily turn garbage into plausible-looking numbers. So a good binary format is designed to **identify itself, describe its own structure, evolve over time, and fail loudly**. This lesson takes those properties one at a time, using two well-known formats as worked examples: Protocol Buffers for messages and SQLite for whole database files.

## The design checklist
Here is what every serious file format needs, and why. The rest of the lesson shows each item in practice.
- **A magic number** at the very start: a fixed byte sequence that says "this is a file of type X". Tools can recognise the file instantly and reject the wrong one.
- **A version** next to it, so new software can read old files and old software can refuse new ones cleanly.
- **Explicit lengths or fixed sizes**, so a reader can skip what it doesn't need or doesn't understand.
- **Checksums** on blocks or records, to detect corruption and torn writes (lesson 07.03).
- **A fixed byte order** (endianness), so a file written on one machine reads correctly on another.
- **Room to grow**: reserved bytes, flags, or a tagging scheme that lets you add fields later.

## Magic numbers: what is this file?
A **magic number** is a short, fixed sequence of bytes at offset 0. Some familiar ones:

| Format | First bytes | Notes |
| PNG | `89 50 4E 47 0D 0A 1A 0A` | `\x89PNG\r\n\x1a\n` |
| gzip | `1F 8B` | two bytes |
| ELF executable | `7F 45 4C 46` | `\x7fELF` |
| Parquet | `50 41 52 31` | `PAR1`, at the start and the end |
| SQLite | `SQLite format 3\0` | 16 bytes |

Magic numbers do several jobs. A tool like `file` can identify a file without trusting its name. A database that is pointed at the wrong path (a log file, a backup in another format, an empty file) refuses to open it instead of interpreting random bytes as pages and "repairing" them. Good magic numbers are also chosen to catch specific damage. PNG's begins with a byte above 127, which breaks if the file passes through a 7-bit channel, and contains `\r\n` and `\n`, which change if a tool converts line endings. One look at the first eight bytes tells you the file was mangled in transit.

%%tree What a careful reader does when it opens a file. Every branch that does not match ends in a clear error, not a guess.
Open file
  Read first 16 bytes
    Magic matches? | no: "not a data file", stop
      Version known? | read version field
        *Version I understand | parse normally
        Newer read version | refuse: "upgrade software"
        Newer write version only | open read-only
      Checksum of header OK? | no: "corrupt header", stop
%%

## Versions: surviving change
Formats change. You add compression, a new index, a bigger counter. Without a version number a reader has no way to know which layout it is looking at, and the failure mode is the worst kind: silently wrong data.

With a version, the rules become simple:
- **New code reading old files**: check the version and use the old layout. Keep that code path for as long as old files exist.
- **Old code reading new files**: see an unknown version and refuse with a clear message ("this file was written by version 3; please upgrade").

SQLite refines this with **two** version numbers, which is a neat trick. The header holds a **write version** and a **read version**. If a reader doesn't recognise the write version, it can still read the file safely but must not modify it, so it opens it read-only. If it doesn't recognise the read version, it can't even read the file, so it refuses. This lets a format add a feature that only matters to writers (such as a new journaling mode) without locking out older readers.

## Case study: the SQLite file header
An SQLite database is one file made of fixed-size **pages**. The first page begins with a 100-byte header, and all multi-byte integers in it are **big-endian** (most significant byte first), so the file reads identically on any CPU.

%%layers The start of every SQLite file. The first 100 bytes tell any reader what the file is and whether it may open it.
*Magic: "SQLite format 3\0" | bytes 0 to 15, identifies the file
Page size | bytes 16 to 17, how to cut the file into pages
Write version, read version | bytes 18 to 19, open read-only or refuse
Reserved space per page | byte 20, room for extensions such as checksums
File change counter | bytes 24 to 27, lets caches detect changes
Page count, freelist, schema cookie | more fields, up to byte 99
B-tree pages | the rest of page 1 and every following page
%%

A few details show the thinking. The page size (any power of two from 512 to 65,536 bytes) is stored once, so every later offset can be computed. The **file change counter** is incremented on each transaction, so a process holding cached pages can tell cheaply whether another process has changed the file. The "reserved space" byte lets extensions (like encryption or per-page checksums) claim a few bytes at the end of every page without changing the format. Crash recovery is also part of the format: SQLite's rollback journal and its WAL file each have their own headers with magic numbers, salts and checksums, so a leftover journal from a crash can be recognised and replayed or discarded correctly.

## Case study: Protocol Buffers on the wire
Protocol Buffers (protobuf) is built for messages rather than files, and it solves a different problem well: compactness and **schema evolution**, meaning old and new programs can exchange messages even after fields are added.

### Varints
Most integers are small, so protobuf stores them as **varints** (variable-length integers) of 1 to 10 bytes. Each byte holds 7 bits of the value plus a **continuation bit** (the top bit): 1 means "more bytes follow", 0 means "this is the last byte". The least significant group comes first.

Take 150. In binary that's `1001 0110`, which needs 8 bits, so it takes two bytes. The low 7 bits are `001 0110` (0x16); add the continuation bit and you get `1001 0110` = **0x96**. The remaining bits, `1`, go in the next byte with no continuation bit: **0x01**. So 150 encodes as `96 01`. Any number below 128 takes a single byte.

### Tags and wire types
A message is a sequence of fields, each written as a **key** followed by a **value**. The key is `(field_number << 3) | wire_type`. The **wire type** (3 bits) says how to find the end of the value: 0 is a varint, 1 is 64-bit fixed, 2 is length-delimited (a varint length, then that many bytes: strings, bytes, nested messages), 5 is 32-bit fixed. Field 1 as a varint gives key `(1 << 3) | 0 = 08`, so "field 1 = 150" is the three bytes `08 96 01`.

%%seq How a reader decodes "field 1 = 150". The wire type tells it how long the value is, even for a field it has never heard of.
Reader > Byte 08 : read key varint
Byte 08 -> Reader : field 1, varint
Reader > Byte 96 : more follows, bits 0x16
Reader > Byte 01 : last byte, value 1
Reader > Reader : 0x16 + (1 << 7) = 150
%%

The wire type is the clever part. Because every value's length can be determined without knowing the schema, a reader that meets **an unknown field number simply skips it**. That is what makes protobuf evolve safely: a new writer adds field 4; an old reader skips field 4 and carries on. The rule for schema authors follows: never reuse or renumber a field, only add new ones.

### ZigZag for negative numbers
A plain `int32` of -1 is stored as a 64-bit two's-complement number, all ones, which takes the maximum **10 bytes** as a varint. The `sint32` and `sint64` types fix this with **ZigZag** encoding, which interleaves signs: 0 → 0, -1 → 1, 1 → 2, -2 → 3, 2 → 4. Small magnitudes, positive or negative, become small unsigned numbers and take one byte. Use `sint` types for fields that are often negative, such as deltas.

! Example: one sensor reading, JSON versus protobuf
! The record is user_id 123456, timestamp 1700000000, temperature 21.5. As JSON, `{"user_id":123456,"ts":1700000000,"temp":21.5}` is 46 bytes.
! As protobuf: field 1 (varint 123456, which is below 2^21 so 3 bytes) plus its 1-byte key is 4 bytes; field 2 (varint 1700000000, below 2^35 so 5 bytes) plus key is 6 bytes; field 3 (a 32-bit float, wire type 5) is 1 + 4 = 5 bytes. Total **15 bytes**, about a third of the JSON. At 1 billion readings a day that is 31 GB saved daily, and decoding needs no string parsing at all.

%%bars Size of the same sensor reading in three encodings.
JSON text | 46 | 46 bytes
Fixed-width binary struct | 20 | 20 bytes (8 + 8 + 4)
*Protobuf | 15 | 15 bytes
%%

## Byte order and checksums
**Endianness** is the order in which a multi-byte number's bytes are stored. The 32-bit number 0x01020304 is `01 02 03 04` in big-endian and `04 03 02 01` in little-endian. x86 and ARM CPUs are little-endian in practice; network protocols and SQLite use big-endian. Which you choose matters less than choosing **one** and writing it down. A format that "uses the machine's native order" breaks the day a file moves between machines.

**Checksums** belong on every unit that can be independently damaged: each record in a log, each page in a data file, each block in an object. Use CRC32C for speed or a cryptographic hash (SHA-256) if you also need to detect deliberate tampering. Store the checksum next to the data it covers and verify it on every read. Lesson 07.20 explains why checksums at this level catch errors that the network and disk never report.

## Common mistakes
- **No version field.** The first format change forces a painful guess about which files are which.
- **Native byte order or struct dumps.** Writing a C struct straight to disk bakes in the compiler's padding and the CPU's endianness.
- **Implicit lengths.** If a reader must understand a record to know where it ends, it can't skip unknown records, and one bad byte desynchronises everything after it.
- **Reusing protobuf field numbers.** An old reader decodes the new field as the old one, silently.
- **Silent "best effort" parsing.** A format should fail with a clear error, not return wrong numbers.

! Example: a log segment header that evolves
! A team designs a log segment file: 4 bytes magic `SEG1`, 2 bytes format version, 2 bytes flags, 8 bytes base offset, all little-endian, then records of [4-byte length][4-byte CRC32C][payload]. That is a 16-byte header and 8 bytes of framing per record.
! A year later they add compression. They bump the version to 2 and set flag bit 0 to mean "payload is LZ4-compressed". New readers handle both versions. An old reader that finds a version-2 segment on a restored backup refuses it with "unsupported segment version 2" instead of feeding compressed bytes to the application as if they were records. Without the version field, it would have read garbage lengths, failed checksums on every record, and possibly truncated the file as a "torn tail".

> Takeaway: a binary format should identify itself with a magic number, state its version, make every piece's length explicit, fix its byte order and checksum its blocks. Then it can be read fast, evolve safely, and fail loudly instead of silently returning wrong data.

?? A magic number lets any program instantly tell this file type from every other and reject garbage, an empty file or the wrong file before misreading it, and a well-chosen one also exposes transfer damage such as line-ending conversion. A version number lets readers cope with format changes: new code can recognise and still read old layouts, and old code can detect a newer file and refuse it cleanly (or, with separate read and write versions as in SQLite, open it read-only) instead of interpreting the new bytes with the old layout and silently returning or writing corrupt data.

## The problem: big objects, small reads
Object stores such as S3, Google Cloud Storage and Azure Blob Storage hold files (objects) that can be gigabytes or terabytes in size. Often you only need a small part: one record, one column, one frame of a video, the last kilobyte of a log. Downloading a 10 GB object to read 4 KB of it would take a minute and cost real money in transfer. So two questions matter:
1. **Can the store return just a slice of an object?** Yes: HTTP range requests.
2. **How do you know which slice you need?** You need an index that maps "the thing I want" to a byte offset and length.

Put those together and object storage starts to behave like a very large, very slow, random-access file. That is the foundation of modern data lakes, where query engines read Parquet files directly from S3, and of features like video seeking and resumable downloads.

## HTTP range requests
HTTP has supported partial downloads since HTTP/1.1. The mechanism is small:
- A server advertises support with the response header `Accept-Ranges: bytes`.
- The client sends `Range: bytes=1000-1999` to ask for bytes 1,000 to 1,999 inclusive (1,000 bytes). Variants: `bytes=1000-` means "from 1,000 to the end"; `bytes=-500` means "the last 500 bytes", which is very useful when the index is at the end of the file.
- The server replies **`206 Partial Content`** with `Content-Range: bytes 1000-1999/10737418240`, where the number after the slash is the full object size.
- Several ranges can be requested at once (`bytes=0-99,5000-5099`) and come back as a multipart response. S3's GetObject supports only **one range per request**, so clients issue several requests in parallel instead.
- A range that starts past the end of the object gets `416 Range Not Satisfiable`.
- `If-Range: "etag"` means "send the range only if the object still has this version; otherwise send the whole new object". It protects a resumed download from stitching together pieces of two different versions.

%%seq A range request: the client asks for 1,000 bytes and the server returns only those, with status 206 and the total size.
Client > Server : GET /big.bin, Range: bytes=1000-1999
Server > Server : check range within object size
Server -> Client : 206 Partial Content
Server -> Client : Content-Range: bytes 1000-1999/10737418240
Server -> Client : 1,000 bytes of body
%%

### What a range request costs
On object storage, each GET has a fixed overhead regardless of size: **time to first byte** of roughly 10 to 100 ms, plus a per-request charge. After that, a single connection streams at maybe 50 to 100 MB/s. So the economics are different from a local disk:
- Reading 4 KB costs about as much latency as reading 1 MB. Small ranges are fine for single lookups, but reading thousands of tiny ranges one after another is slow.
- Large reads are best split into parallel ranges (say 8 to 16 MB each over many connections). This is how fast S3 downloaders reach gigabytes per second: many concurrent range GETs on one object.
- Nearby ranges should be **coalesced** into one request if the gap between them is small. Reading 100 KB of unwanted bytes is cheaper than paying another 30 ms round trip.

## You need an index to know which bytes
A range request is only useful if you know where the data is. There are three common ways to provide the index:
- **An index inside the object**, usually in a footer at the end (Parquet, ORC, ZIP's central directory).
- **A separate index object** stored beside the data (`data.bin` plus `data.idx`), or rows in a database that hold the offset and length of each record.
- **Fixed-size records**, where record N starts at N × record size and no index is needed at all.

### Why the index goes at the end
Writers produce large files in one streaming pass. They don't know where each block will land until they've written it, so they can't put a complete index at the front without either buffering the whole file or going back to patch the start (which object stores don't allow: objects are immutable once written). The solution is to **write the data first and the index last**, followed by the index's length at a fixed position from the end. A reader then starts from the end.

## Case study: Parquet
Parquet is the standard columnar file format for analytics. "Columnar" means values of the same column are stored together, so a query that needs 2 columns out of 50 reads only those 2.

%%tree The structure of a Parquet file. Readers navigate down this hierarchy using offsets stored in the footer.
Parquet file | starts and ends with magic PAR1
  Row group 1 | for example 128 MB of rows
    Column chunk: user_id | contiguous bytes
      Page 1 | about 1 MB, compressed
      Page 2
    Column chunk: event_type
    Column chunk: payload
  Row group 2
    Column chunk: user_id
    Column chunk: event_type
  *Footer metadata | schema, offsets, min/max per chunk
  Footer length + PAR1 | last 8 bytes
%%

- The file starts and ends with the 4-byte magic `PAR1`.
- Rows are split into **row groups** (commonly 128 MB to 1 GB of data each).
- Within a row group, each column is stored as a **column chunk**, a contiguous byte range, made of **pages** (around 1 MB) that are compressed and encoded independently.
- The **footer** holds the file metadata: the schema, and for every row group and column chunk its byte offset, size, and **statistics** such as min and max values and null counts.
- The last 8 bytes are a 4-byte footer length and the closing `PAR1`.

A reader does the reverse of the writer:
1. Read the last 8 bytes (a suffix range, `bytes=-8`) to get the footer length and check the magic. Many readers simply read the last 64 KB in one go, which usually captures the whole footer and saves a round trip.
2. Read the footer with one range request.
3. Use the statistics to skip row groups that cannot match the query (**predicate pushdown**). If the query wants events from 12 March and a row group's timestamp min/max is 1 to 5 March, it is skipped without reading a byte of it.
4. Fetch only the needed column chunks of the surviving row groups, with range requests in parallel.

%%seq Reading one column from a Parquet file in object storage: two small reads to find the index, then only the bytes that matter.
Query engine > Object store : GET Range: bytes=-8
Object store -> Query engine : footer length 200 KB + PAR1
Query engine > Object store : GET footer range
Object store -> Query engine : schema, offsets, min/max stats
Query engine > Query engine : stats prune 77 of 80 row groups
Query engine > Object store : 3 parallel GETs, user_id chunks only
Object store -> Query engine : about 30 MB
%%

! Example: one column from a 10 GB file in S3
! A query needs `user_id` for events on one day. The engine range-reads the last 8 bytes of the 10 GB Parquet file, then the 200 KB footer. The footer lists 80 row groups of about 125 MB each, with min and max timestamps. Statistics show only 3 of the 80 row groups overlap that day.
! Each of those row groups has 20 columns; `user_id` is about 10 MB per row group compressed. The engine issues 3 range GETs, about 30 MB in total, in parallel. Total: about 30.2 MB read and 5 requests, versus 10 GB and one very long download. At 100 MB/s per connection, that is under half a second instead of over a minute and a half.

%%bars Bytes transferred to answer the same query, by approach.
Download whole object | 10240 | 10 GB
Read only matching row groups | 375 | about 375 MB
*Matching row groups, one column | 30 | about 30 MB
Footer only | 0.2 | 200 KB
%%

## Other designs that rely on byte ranges
The same pattern shows up in many places:
- **Packed small objects.** Storing billions of tiny files as separate objects is expensive (per-request costs, metadata overhead). Systems pack many small records into one large object and keep an index of `(object, offset, length)` per record in a database. Facebook's Haystack (lesson 07.13) does this for photos; many log and backup systems do it too.
- **Video streaming.** An MP4's `moov` box is an index of where every frame lives. Players read it, then fetch the ranges for the part being watched, which is how seeking works without downloading the whole film. (Encoders often move `moov` to the front, called "fast start", so playback can begin immediately.)
- **ZIP files.** The central directory is at the end. A tool can list or extract one file from a remote ZIP with two or three range requests.
- **Resumable and parallel downloads.** Download managers split a file into ranges, fetch them concurrently and retry only failed ranges, using `If-Range` or an ETag check to make sure the object didn't change between pieces.
- **Cloud-optimised formats.** Geospatial imagery (Cloud-Optimised GeoTIFF) and genomics files are laid out with internal tiling and an index so that a map viewer can fetch only the tiles on screen.

%%compare Two ways to give a reader the offsets it needs. Both turn a whole-object download into a few small range reads.
[Index inside the object]
Read footer -(offsets)-> Range GET data
+ One object, can't get out of sync
+ Written in one streaming pass
- Two round trips before data
[Index in a database]
Query DB -(object, offset, len)-> Range GET data
+ One round trip to storage
+ Can index billions of packed records
- Index and data can drift apart
%%

## Pitfalls
- **Too many tiny ranges.** Thousands of 4 KB reads at 30 ms each are slower than one larger read. Coalesce nearby ranges and read in parallel.
- **Mixing versions.** If an object is overwritten while you read it in pieces, you can stitch together parts of two versions. Pin the version with `If-Match` or `If-Range` on the ETag, or use a versioned object ID.
- **Tiny row groups or pages.** A Parquet file written with 1 MB row groups has a huge footer and thousands of chunks to fetch. Too large and pruning becomes coarse. 128 MB to 512 MB row groups are a common middle ground.
- **Assuming multi-range support.** S3 serves one range per request; code written for multi-range HTTP servers must split requests.
- **Forgetting cost.** Requests are billed. A design that issues 50 range GETs per user query at millions of queries a day can cost more in requests than in storage.

## In an interview
When a design stores large files in object storage but needs fine-grained access, say: "I'll store an index, either in the file's footer or in the metadata database, with offset and length per record, and read with HTTP range GETs." Mention parallel ranged reads for throughput, and coalescing for latency. If the prompt involves analytics on a data lake, name Parquet's footer and min/max statistics as the reason a query on a 10 TB table can read only a few gigabytes.

> Takeaway: HTTP range requests let you read any slice of a large object, returning 206 with just those bytes, and an index (a footer such as Parquet's, or offsets in a database) tells you which slice to ask for. Together they make immutable object storage behave like a random-access file.

?? Store an index that maps each record to a byte offset and length, either inside the object (typically a footer at the end, as Parquet and ZIP do, with its length in the last few bytes) or alongside it in a database. The client first fetches the index with a small range request (for example `Range: bytes=-8` to get the footer length, then the footer), looks up the record's offset and length, then sends `Range: bytes=start-end` and receives a `206 Partial Content` with only those bytes. Statistics in the index can also rule out whole blocks, and an ETag check (If-Range or If-Match) ensures the pieces come from the same version of the object.

## Never edit, always add
Most of us learned databases as things that change rows in place: `UPDATE accounts SET balance = 90 WHERE id = 7` finds the row and overwrites it. Large analytics tables on object storage work the other way round. Table formats such as **Apache Iceberg**, **Delta Lake** and **Apache Hudi** never modify a data file once it has been written. To change data, they:
1. Write **new** data files containing the new or changed rows.
2. Atomically publish a new **version** (also called a snapshot) of the table: a small piece of metadata listing exactly which data files make up the table now.

Old data files are left untouched, and old versions keep pointing at them. This lesson explains how that works, why it makes concurrency and rollback almost trivial, and what it costs.

There's a practical reason this design took over data lakes: object stores don't let you modify part of an object. You can only write a whole new object or replace one entirely. But the benefits go well beyond that constraint, and the same principle appears in LSM trees (07.09), the stream layer of a cloud store (07.18), Git, and container images.

## Delta Lake's transaction log
Delta Lake keeps a directory called `_delta_log` next to the table's data files. That directory **is** the table's history.

%%tree The layout of a Delta table. Data files never change; the log directory records which of them belong to each version.
sales/ | the table
  part-0001.parquet | data file, immutable
  part-0002.parquet
  part-0003.parquet
  _delta_log/
    00000000000000000000.json | version 0
    00000000000000000001.json | version 1
    00000000000000000010.checkpoint.parquet | state at v10
    *00000000000000000011.json | latest commit
    _last_checkpoint | points at v10
%%

Each commit is a JSON file named by a zero-padded 20-digit version number. It contains a list of **actions**:
- `add`: this data file is now part of the table (with its path, size, partition values and column statistics).
- `remove`: this data file is no longer part of the table. The file itself stays on storage; it just isn't in the new version.
- `metaData`: the schema or table settings changed.
- `commitInfo`: who made the change and what operation it was.

The current state of the table is computed by **replaying** the log in order: start with nothing, apply each version's adds and removes, and you end with the set of live files. Replaying thousands of small JSON files would be slow, so every so often (by default every 10 commits) Delta writes a **checkpoint**: a Parquet file summarising the full state at that version. A reader finds the latest checkpoint via `_last_checkpoint`, loads it, and replays only the few JSON commits after it.

%%timeline Four commits to a Delta table and the set of live data files after each. Old files stay on storage after they are removed from the table.
v0 | add A, B: table is A, B
v1 | add C (new rows): table is A, B, C
v2 | remove A, add D (rows in A updated): table is B, C, D
v3 | *remove C (bad load rolled back): table is B, D
Later | VACUUM deletes A and C once older than 7 days
%%

Notice how an **update** works at v2. Some rows in file A changed. Delta cannot edit A, so it writes D, a full copy of A with those rows changed, then commits "remove A, add D" as one version. This is called **copy-on-write**: the cost of changing one row is rewriting the whole file that contains it.

## How a commit stays atomic
The whole design rests on one property: creating the next version file is atomic and **only one writer can create a given version**. A writer that wants to commit version 11 tries to create `00000000000000000011.json` with a "create only if it doesn't exist" operation (put-if-absent). If two writers race, one succeeds and the other fails.

This is **optimistic concurrency control**: writers do all their work (writing new data files, possibly gigabytes) without taking any lock, and only check for conflicts at the tiny commit step.

%%seq Two writers race to commit version 11. The loser checks whether the winner's commit conflicts with its own work, then retries as version 12.
Writer A > Log : latest is v10
Writer B > Log : latest is v10
Writer A > Storage : write data files
Writer B > Storage : write data files
Writer A > Log : create 11.json
Log -> Writer A : committed v11
Writer B > Log : create 11.json
Log -> Writer B : already exists
Writer B > Log : read v11: conflict?
Writer B > Log : no: create 12.json
Log -> Writer B : committed v12
%%

What counts as a conflict? If writer A removed a file that writer B read and rewrote, B's commit would resurrect stale data, so B must abort and redo its work. If A only appended new files to a different partition, B can simply retry at the next version number. Its data files are already written, so the retry is cheap.

On HDFS and Azure storage, atomic create-if-absent was always available. On S3 it historically wasn't, so multi-writer Delta tables on S3 needed an external coordinator (typically a DynamoDB table) to serialise commits. S3 later added conditional writes (`If-None-Match: *`), which provide exactly this primitive.

## Iceberg: the same idea, a different tree
Iceberg arranges metadata as a tree rather than a flat log:
- A **catalog** holds one pointer per table: the location of the current metadata file.
- The **metadata file** (JSON) lists the schema, partitioning and the table's snapshots.
- Each **snapshot** points to a **manifest list**, which points to **manifest files**, each listing a batch of data files with their statistics.

A commit writes new data files, new manifests, a new manifest list and a new metadata file, all as new objects, and then performs an atomic **compare-and-swap** on the catalog pointer: "change the pointer from metadata v41 to v42, but only if it is still v41". If someone else committed first, the swap fails and the writer retries. Iceberg's guarantees match Delta's: reads use a committed snapshot and are never affected by concurrent writes, and a write is either fully visible or not at all.

Because rewriting a whole 512 MB file to delete one row is expensive, Iceberg (from format version 2) also supports **merge-on-read**: instead of rewriting the data file, it writes a small **delete file** that lists the rows to ignore (by file and position, or by key values). Readers apply the delete files as they read. Writes are cheap; reads get slower as delete files accumulate, until a compaction job rewrites the data files and drops them.

%%compare Two ways an immutable table handles an update to a few rows. Both keep old files intact; they differ in who pays.
[Copy-on-write]
Rewrite whole file -(commit)-> remove old, add new
+ Reads stay simple and fast
- One changed row rewrites 512 MB
- Slow for frequent small updates
[Merge-on-read]
Write delete file -(commit)-> add delete file
+ Updates are small and quick
- Every read must apply deletes
- Needs periodic compaction
%%

## Why immutability helps so much
- **Readers need no locks.** A reader picks a version when it starts and reads exactly the files listed in it. Those files never change, so the reader can't see a half-written file or a mix of old and new rows. This is **snapshot isolation**, and it costs nothing at read time. A query that runs for two hours sees a perfectly consistent table even while hundreds of commits happen around it.
- **Writers barely coordinate.** All the heavy work, writing gigabytes of data, happens in parallel with no locks. Writers only race on the commit, a write of a few kilobytes.
- **Rollback is instant.** Every old version is still a complete, valid list of files. Undo means committing a new version that restores an older file list (Delta's `RESTORE`, Iceberg's rollback), which takes seconds whatever the table's size.
- **Time travel.** You can query the table "as of version 411" or "as of yesterday 09:00" to reproduce a report, debug a pipeline or train a model on exactly the data it saw before.
- **Caching and replication are trivial.** An immutable file can be cached anywhere, forever, with no invalidation. Copying a table to another region means copying files that never change, then the metadata.
- **Auditing.** The log is a record of who changed what and when.

## What it costs
- **Storage grows** until old versions are cleaned up. Delta's `VACUUM` deletes files no longer referenced by any version within the retention period (7 days by default); Iceberg expires old snapshots and then deletes orphaned files. Clean too aggressively and long-running queries or time travel break, because files they need disappear.
- **Write amplification for small changes.** Copy-on-write rewrites whole files; merge-on-read pushes the cost onto readers.
- **Small files.** Frequent small commits (a streaming job committing every 10 seconds) create thousands of tiny files and a long log. Background **compaction** (also called "optimize") rewrites them into fewer large files, committed as one more version.
- **Write conflicts under contention.** Optimistic concurrency works well when commits rarely touch the same data. Many writers hammering the same partition will retry a lot.

! Example: a bad backfill
! A job rewrites last month's sales at version 412 with a bug that doubles every amount. It removed 300 files and added 300 new ones. Analysts notice an hour later, by which time versions 413 to 418 have also been committed by unrelated hourly loads.
! Because version 411 still lists the 300 original files, and VACUUM won't delete them for 7 days, the team commits version 419, which removes the 300 bad files and re-adds the 300 originals listed at version 411, leaving the later hourly loads in place. (A full table `RESTORE` to 411 would also undo those loads.) It completes in seconds and copies no data. Nothing had been overwritten, so nothing had to be recovered from backup. Had VACUUM retention been 0 hours, the originals would have been gone.

! Example: sizing the cost of copy-on-write
! A 2 TB table is stored as 4,000 files of 512 MB. A daily job updates 50,000 rows scattered randomly across the table. With copy-on-write, those rows likely touch most of the 4,000 files, so the job rewrites close to 2 TB a day to change a few megabytes. With merge-on-read it writes perhaps a few hundred small delete files, a few megabytes, and a weekly compaction rewrites the affected files once. This is why update-heavy tables use merge-on-read, and append-only tables don't need either.

## In an interview
If a design includes a data lake or historical tables, say "data files are immutable; each commit writes new files and atomically publishes a new snapshot through a put-if-absent or compare-and-swap on the metadata pointer". Then cash it in: readers get consistent snapshots without locks, rollback is a pointer change, and the cost is storage until vacuum plus compaction of small files.

> Takeaway: write new files, then publish a new version with one atomic, conditional commit. Readers get consistent snapshots without locks, writers only coordinate on a tiny commit, and rollback or time travel is just choosing an older version, until a vacuum deletes the files it needs.

?? If files never change, a reader can never see a partially written file or a mix of old and new rows; it simply reads the set of files listed in the version it started with, so it needs no locks and gets snapshot isolation for free. Writers do their heavy work without locks and coordinate only on one tiny atomic commit (put-if-absent of the next log entry, or compare-and-swap of a metadata pointer), retrying if another writer won and checking whether its changes conflict. ## The log as the core abstraction
A **log** in this sense is not a debug text file. It is an **append-only, totally ordered sequence of records**, where each record gets the next sequential number (its **offset** or log sequence number). You can only add to the end. You can read from any position. Records are never modified once written.

That sounds too simple to matter, yet logs sit underneath almost every serious data system:
- **Databases** write every change to a write-ahead log before applying it (lesson 07.10), so a crash can be recovered by replaying the log.
- **Replication** works by shipping the log. If replicas start in the same state and apply the same records in the same order, they end in the same state. This is called **state machine replication**, and it is how PostgreSQL streaming replication, MySQL's binlog replicas and Raft-based systems all work.
- **Data integration** uses logs as the hub between systems. Change data capture streams a database's log into Kafka, and every downstream system (search index, cache, warehouse) reads from there.

### Tables and logs are two views of the same thing
A **table** holds the current value of each key. A **log** holds the history of changes. If you replay the log from the start, you get the table. If you record every change to the table, you get the log. This **table/stream duality** is why a log is enough to rebuild a database, populate a cache, or create a new replica: they are all "the state after applying the log up to offset N".

### Why a central log simplifies integration
Picture N systems (a database, a search index, a cache, a warehouse, a recommendation service...) that all need each other's data. Wiring each pair directly gives up to N × (N − 1) pipelines, each with its own format, failure modes and backfill story. With 10 systems that is 90 pipelines. A central log changes it to N writers and N readers: each system publishes its changes once and subscribes to what it needs.

%%compare Integrating many systems. Point-to-point pipelines grow with the square of the number of systems; a shared log grows linearly.
[Point-to-point]
DB -> Search, DB -> Cache, DB -> Warehouse
- 10 systems: up to 90 pipelines
- Each pipeline fails differently
- Adding a system touches many others
[*Central log]
DB -(append)-> Log -(subscribe)-> Search, Cache, Warehouse
+ 10 systems: 10 writers, 10 readers
+ New reader replays from offset 0
+ One ordering, one format
%%

LinkedIn built Kafka on exactly this idea and ran it at over 60 billion writes a day. The performance came from treating the log as a disk-friendly structure: the log is **partitioned** so many machines append in parallel, reads and writes are **batched** so each disk operation moves a lot of data, and data is sent from the page cache to the network with **zero-copy** (`sendfile`), so bytes never pass through the application's memory.

## Log-structured storage
Applied to a storage engine, the idea is radical: **every write is appended to the end of a file, and nothing is ever updated in place**. Setting `user:42` appends a record. Setting it again appends another record. Deleting it appends a third. The engine's job is to make reads efficient despite that.

What you get immediately:
- **Fast writes.** Every write is a sequential append, the access pattern disks love (lesson 07.01). Writes can be batched and fsynced together.
- **Simple crash recovery.** Old data is never touched, so a crash can only damage the record being appended at the tail. Recovery checks the tail's checksum and truncates (lesson 07.03).
- **Easy replication and backup.** Immutable data can be copied while the engine keeps running.

What you lose: the newest value of a key could be anywhere in the file. Scanning the whole log to answer a read is obviously unacceptable, so log-structured engines add an index. There are two main designs:
- **Hash index in memory**: keep a hash table from each key to the file offset of its latest record. Reads are one lookup plus one disk read. This is Bitcask (lesson 07.08). The limit is that every key must fit in RAM, and there are no range scans.
- **Sorted runs**: buffer recent writes in a sorted in-memory structure, the **memtable**, and when it is full write it out as a **sorted string table** (SSTable), an immutable file of key-value pairs sorted by key with a small index. Over time you have many SSTables, so you merge them in the background. This is the **LSM tree** (lessons 07.09 to 07.12).

%%flow The log-structured write path: sorted in memory, flushed as immutable sorted files, merged in the background.
Write -(append)-> WAL -> Memtable -(when full)-> SSTable 1 -(compaction)-> *Merged SSTable
%%

### Deletes are writes too
You can't remove a record from the middle of an immutable file, so a delete appends a **tombstone**: a marker that says "this key is gone as of now". Reads that find the tombstone first report "not found". The tombstone has to stay until compaction has merged it with every older file that might still hold a value for the key; drop it too early and the old value would reappear, a bug known as **data resurrection**. Cassandra's `gc_grace_seconds` (10 days by default) exists for this reason in a replicated setting: tombstones are kept long enough for every replica to have seen them.

### Compaction
**Compaction** is the background process that pays the bill. It reads several files, merges them by key, keeps only the newest value for each key, drops overwritten values and (when safe) tombstones, and writes the result as new files. Then it deletes the inputs. Because the input files are sorted, this is a streaming merge, like the merge step of merge sort: read each input sequentially, write the output sequentially, use little memory.

## What you pay later
Append-only writes are fast because they defer work. The deferred work comes back in four forms.

### 1. Garbage (space amplification)
Every overwrite and delete leaves a dead record on disk until compaction removes it. A workload that updates the same keys repeatedly can hold many times more bytes on disk than it has live data.

%%timeline Disk use for a log-structured store whose live data stays at 10 GB but whose keys are constantly overwritten. Garbage builds up until compaction reclaims it.
00:00 | 10 GB on disk, all live
01:00 | 16 GB on disk: 6 GB dead versions
02:00 | 22 GB on disk: compaction starts
02:20 | *Compaction rewrites live data: needs 10 GB extra temporarily
02:40 | Inputs deleted: back to 10 GB
%%

Note the moment of peak usage: during compaction both the inputs and the output exist. You need free disk headroom for compaction to run at all. Running a log-structured store at 95% disk full is how outages start.

### 2. Compaction I/O (write amplification)
Data is written once to the log, then rewritten every time compaction merges it. In a leveled LSM tree a byte may be rewritten 10 to 30 times over its life. That is **write amplification**, defined properly in lesson 07.12. The writes are sequential, so they are cheap per byte, but they still consume disk bandwidth, CPU (for decompression, merging and recompression) and SSD endurance.

### 3. Read cost (read amplification)
A key might be in the memtable or in any of several files. A read checks them newest first and stops at the first hit, but a key that doesn't exist must be checked everywhere. Engines add per-file indexes, Bloom filters and caches (lesson 07.11) to keep this cheap.

### 4. Latency spikes
Compaction competes with live traffic for disk, CPU and cache. If writes arrive faster than compaction can merge them, files pile up, reads slow down, and eventually the engine must **throttle or stall** incoming writes to let compaction catch up. That shows up as sudden p99 latency spikes, usually under the heaviest load.

%%bars On-disk bytes for one session key updated 1,000 times in an hour (500-byte records), before and after compaction.
Live data | 0.5 | 0.5 KB
*Before compaction | 500 | about 500 KB
After compaction | 0.5 | 0.5 KB
%%

! Example: overwriting a hot key
! A user's session record (500 bytes) is updated 1,000 times in an hour. A log-structured engine writes 1,000 records, about 500 KB, each a cheap sequential append. Until compaction runs, 999 of them are dead weight. Multiply by 2 million active sessions and the engine holds about 1 TB of mostly dead records for 1 GB of live data.
! Compaction must read all of that to keep the last version of each key. In practice LSM engines soften this: overwrites of a key still in the memtable replace it in memory and never reach disk, and frequent flushes plus compaction keep only a few generations around. But it shows why update-heavy workloads drive compaction cost.

! Example: running out of headroom
! A node has a 2 TB disk, 1.6 TB used (80%). A large compaction needs to merge 500 GB of input into roughly 450 GB of output. Until the inputs are deleted, the disk needs 1.6 + 0.45 = 2.05 TB. The compaction fails for lack of space, files keep piling up, read latency climbs, and the node stalls writes. The fix is operational: alert well before disks are 70 to 80% full, and add capacity rather than wait.

## Common mistakes
- **Treating write speed as free.** The log is fast because compaction pays later. Plan disk bandwidth and space for it.
- **Dropping tombstones early.** Old values resurrect. Tombstones must outlive every older copy of the key.
- **Filling disks.** Compaction needs temporary space. A full disk stops compaction, which makes things worse, not better.
- **Ignoring tail latency.** Average write latency looks great; p99.9 during a compaction backlog does not.

## In an interview
When you propose a log-structured store (Cassandra, RocksDB-backed systems, Kafka-like logs), name the bill in the same breath: "Writes are sequential appends, so ingest is cheap; the cost is compaction, which uses disk bandwidth and temporary space and can cause latency spikes, and reads may check several files, which Bloom filters mitigate." Ask about the update pattern: append-only time series compacts cheaply; heavy overwrites of the same keys does not.

> Takeaway: append-only storage makes writes fast, sequential and crash-safe, and the log doubles as the basis for replication and integration. The bill comes later as garbage on disk, compaction I/O, reads that may search several files, and latency spikes when compaction falls behind.

?? You pay in space, because overwritten values and tombstones stay on disk until compaction removes them and compaction itself needs free headroom while inputs and outputs coexist. You pay in extra I/O and CPU, because compaction rereads and rewrites the same data many times (write amplification). You pay in read cost, because the newest value of a key may be in the memtable or any of several files, so reads need indexes, Bloom filters and caches, and missing keys are expensive. And you pay in latency spikes, because compaction competes with live traffic and, if it falls behind, the engine must throttle or stall writes.

## The simplest real log-structured engine
Bitcask was written at Basho as a storage backend for the Riak key-value database. It is worth studying because it is the log-structured idea from lesson 07.07 in its most direct form, with almost nothing added: an append-only log on disk plus a hash table in memory. It is small enough to understand completely, it was genuinely used in production, and its one big limitation teaches you exactly why LSM trees exist.

Its design goals were modest and clear: low latency for every read and write, high write throughput, datasets much larger than RAM (for values, at least), crash friendliness, easy backup, and simple code.

## The pieces
### Data files
A Bitcask is a **directory** of data files. At any moment exactly one file is **active**: it is open for appends. When it reaches a size limit (for example 2 GB) it is closed and becomes **immutable**, and a new active file is opened. Only one process (the writer) may write to a Bitcask directory at a time; it holds a lock. Any number of readers can read.

Every write, whether an insert or an update, appends one record to the active file:

%%layers One Bitcask record on disk. The key and value are stored together, and the CRC detects a torn or corrupted record.
*CRC | 4 bytes: checksum over the rest of the record
Timestamp | 4 bytes: when it was written
Key size | 2 bytes
Value size | 4 bytes
Key | variable
Value | variable, can be large
%%

There is no in-place update. Setting a key that already exists simply appends a newer record; the old one stays in its file, now dead.

### The keydir
The clever part is in memory. The **keydir** is a hash table with one entry for **every live key**, mapping it to:
- the **file id** where the latest record lives,
- the **value position** (byte offset) within that file,
- the **value size**, and
- the **timestamp**.

A write appends to the active file, then updates the keydir entry to point at the new record. The write is now complete (subject to the fsync policy you choose: every write, on an interval, or left to the OS).

%%seq A Bitcask get: one hash lookup in RAM gives the exact location, then one disk read fetches the value.
Client > Keydir (RAM) : get("user:42")
Keydir (RAM) -> Client : file 7, offset 81,920, size 512
Client > Data file 7 : pread 512 bytes at 81,920
Data file 7 -> Client : value (check CRC)
%%

### Why reads are so predictable
A read is: one hash lookup in memory (microseconds), then **one** positioned read from disk at a known offset and length. That's at most one seek per read, whatever the dataset size. If the file's pages are already in the OS page cache, it costs no disk I/O at all. There is no tree to walk, no multiple files to check, no read amplification. Writes are pure sequential appends. Bitcask's authors reported 5,000 to 6,000 writes per second on a laptop of the time, with low, steady latency.

! Example: tracing a write then a read
! `put("cart:991", 300 bytes)` arrives. Bitcask appends a record of 4 + 4 + 2 + 4 + 8 + 300 = 322 bytes at offset 1,048,000 of active file 12, and sets keydir["cart:991"] = (file 12, value at 1,048,022, size 300, ts). Total cost: one memory update and a 322-byte sequential append.
! A moment later `get("cart:991")` looks up the keydir, then reads 300 bytes at offset 1,048,022 of file 12. Those bytes were just written, so they are almost certainly still in the page cache: the read costs no disk I/O.

## Deletes, garbage and merging
### Deletes
A delete appends a special **tombstone** record for the key and removes the key from the keydir. From then on reads miss in the keydir and return "not found" immediately without touching disk. The tombstone matters for recovery: if the keydir is rebuilt from the files, the tombstone tells the rebuild that the older value for that key is dead.

### Merge
Immutable files accumulate dead records: overwritten values and deleted keys. Left alone, the directory grows forever. Bitcask's compaction process is called **merge**:
1. Pick some or all of the immutable files (never the active one).
2. Read them record by record. For each record, ask the keydir: is this the latest version of this key? Only if the keydir points at exactly this file and offset is the record live.
3. Copy live records to new merged files. Skip dead ones and tombstones whose older values are being merged away too.
4. Update the keydir entries to point at the new locations, then delete the old files.

Merges are triggered by thresholds such as the fraction of dead bytes in a file (fragmentation) or the total amount of dead data, and can be restricted to quiet hours. The writer keeps appending to the active file during a merge, so foreground traffic is not blocked, though the merge does compete for disk bandwidth.

%%timeline One key's life in Bitcask: overwrites leave dead records behind until a merge copies only the latest version and writes a hint file.
09:00 | put v1: appended to file 3, keydir points at file 3
09:30 | put v2: appended to file 4, v1 now dead
10:00 | File 4 full: closed, file 5 becomes active
02:00 next day | *Merge reads files 3 and 4, copies only v2 to merged file 9
02:05 | Hint file 9 written, files 3 and 4 deleted
%%

### Hint files and fast restart
The keydir lives only in memory, so after a restart it must be rebuilt. The slow way is to scan every data file from start to end, reading every key and value. For hundreds of gigabytes that takes a long time. So alongside each merged file, Bitcask writes a **hint file**: a compact list of `(timestamp, key size, value size, value position, key)` for each record, **without the values**. On startup, Bitcask reads the hint files (small) and only scans data files that have no hint file (typically just the recent ones). Restart time then depends on the number of keys, not the volume of values.

%%tree A Bitcask directory. Only the active file takes writes; merged files come with hint files for fast restart.
bitcask/ | one writer holds the lock
  9.bitcask.data | merged, immutable
  9.bitcask.hint | keys and offsets only
  10.bitcask.data | merged, immutable
  10.bitcask.hint
  11.bitcask.data | closed, not yet merged
  *12.bitcask.data | active: appends go here
%%

### Crash recovery
Only the active file can be damaged by a crash, because closed files are never written again. On startup, Bitcask scans the active file and verifies each record's CRC. A torn last record fails its CRC and is ignored (lesson 07.03's torn-tail technique). Everything else is intact by construction. Backups are equally simple: closed files never change, so you can copy them with ordinary tools while the database runs.

## The limit: every key in RAM
The design's strength is also its ceiling. Because the keydir holds an entry for **every** key, the number of keys is bounded by memory. Values can be huge and live on disk, but keys can't. Each keydir entry costs the key itself plus fixed overhead: the hash table slot, file id, offset, size, timestamp and allocator overhead, typically a few tens of bytes per key depending on the implementation.

! Example: sizing a Bitcask node
! A cache stores 200 million keys averaging 40 bytes, with about 30 bytes of keydir overhead per entry: 200M × 70 bytes = 14 GB of RAM just for the keydir, regardless of value size. A 32 GB node fits it, with room for the OS page cache.
! Now the product team doubles the key count to 400 million: 28 GB of keydir on a 32 GB node leaves almost nothing for the page cache, so most reads now go to disk, and growth beyond that is impossible. The options are to shard across more nodes (Riak spreads keys across many Bitcask instances anyway), shorten keys (hash long keys to 16 bytes), or move to an engine that doesn't keep every key in memory.

%%bars Keydir memory for 200 million keys as the average key size grows, with 30 bytes of overhead per entry.
16-byte keys | 9.2 | 9.2 GB
40-byte keys | 14 | 14 GB
*100-byte keys | 26 | 26 GB
200-byte keys | 46 | 46 GB
%%

The second limit: the keydir is a **hash table**, so keys have no order. There is no efficient range scan ("all keys from `order:1000` to `order:2000`") and no prefix listing. You'd have to walk every key. If you need ordered access, you need a sorted structure, which is exactly what the LSM tree's memtable and SSTables provide.

## When Bitcask fits
| Good fit | Poor fit |
| Key count fits comfortably in RAM | Billions of small keys |
| Point reads and writes only | Range scans or prefix queries |
| Values from bytes to megabytes | Tiny values where key overhead dominates |
| Need predictable single-seek reads | Need secondary indexes |
| Frequent overwrites, merge in quiet hours | Disk space very tight |

The same "index in RAM, data in append-only files" pattern shows up elsewhere: Facebook's Haystack photo store keeps a small in-memory index of where every photo sits in huge volume files (lesson 07.13), and many caches and queue engines follow it too.

## Common mistakes
- **Forgetting the key count is the real capacity limit.** Disk space looks plentiful while RAM quietly runs out.
- **No merge schedule.** Dead data accumulates and disk fills; or merges run at peak and compete with traffic.
- **Expecting range scans.** A hash index cannot provide them.
- **Never fsyncing.** With the OS deciding when to flush, a power cut can lose recent writes even though the format itself is crash-safe.

## In an interview
Bitcask is a good answer when the prompt is "a key-value store with very fast point reads and writes and a bounded number of keys", for example a session store or a cache with persistence. Describe it in one breath: "append-only data files, an in-memory hash from key to file and offset, one disk read per get, tombstones for deletes, a background merge that keeps only live records and writes hint files for fast restart." Then name the limit before the interviewer does: every key must fit in RAM and there are no range queries.

> Takeaway: Bitcask gives one-seek reads and append-only writes by keeping every key in an in-memory hash table pointing into immutable log files. Deletes are tombstones, merging reclaims dead records and writes hint files for fast restarts, and the price is that RAM bounds the number of keys and there are no range queries.

Holding every key in the in-memory keydir limits the total number of keys (not the data volume) to what RAM can hold, at roughly the key size plus a few tens of bytes per key, so a node with 200 million 40-byte keys needs about 14 GB just for the index; and because the keydir is a hash table there is no key order, so no range scans or prefix listings. A delete appends a tombstone record and removes the key from the keydir, so later reads miss immediately; the background merge then copies only records the keydir still points at into new files, drops dead values and tombstones, deletes the old files and writes hint files of keys and offsets so the keydir can be rebuilt quickly on restart.

## Where the idea came from
The **log-structured merge tree** (LSM tree) was first described in 1996, for workloads with very high insert rates and comparatively few reads, such as history tables that record every transaction. The problem then was the same as now: a B-tree index has to do a random disk write for almost every insert once the index is bigger than memory, and disk arms (the mechanical heads of a hard drive) are expensive. The proposal was to stop inserting into the on-disk structure one key at a time.

The original design had two components:
- **C0**, a small sorted tree held entirely in memory. Inserts go here, which costs no disk I/O.
- **C1**, a much larger sorted tree on disk.

When C0 grows past a threshold, a **rolling merge** takes a contiguous range of entries from C0 and merges them into C1, reading and writing C1 in large multi-page blocks. Because the merge walks both structures in key order, disk access is sequential, and one page write carries many inserts instead of one. The design generalises to a chain of components C0, C1, ..., Ck, each larger than the last.

The original cost example made the point in money. For 1,000 inserts a second into an index, a B-tree needed about **$56,400** of hardware (mostly disks bought for their I/O rate, not their space, plus memory for the upper levels), while the LSM tree needed about **$11,400**, because each disk page write was amortised across many inserts. It also used the **five-minute rule** (data accessed more often than roughly every five minutes is cheaper to keep in memory than to fetch from disk each time) to reason about how big C0 should be.

%%bars Disk plus memory cost to sustain 1,000 inserts a second in the original 1996 comparison. The LSM tree amortises each page write over many inserts.
B-tree | 56400 | about $56,400
*LSM tree | 11400 | about $11,400
%%

Modern engines (LevelDB, RocksDB, Cassandra, ScyllaDB, HBase, Pebble in CockroachDB, and many others) keep the core idea but replace the in-place rolling merge with immutable files: the memory component is flushed as a whole sorted file, and files are merged into new files in the background. This lesson follows one write through RocksDB, the most widely used embedded LSM engine, from the API call to its final resting place.

## The pieces you need to know
- **WAL (write-ahead log)**: an append-only file on disk recording every write in arrival order. It exists only for crash recovery (lesson 07.10).
- **Memtable**: a sorted in-memory structure that holds recent writes. RocksDB's default is a **skip list**, a linked list with extra "express lane" pointers that allows O(log n) search and insert and, importantly, lets many threads read while one inserts. Default size: 64 MB (`write_buffer_size`).
- **Immutable memtable**: a full memtable that no longer accepts writes and is waiting to be flushed.
- **SST file (SSTable)**: an immutable file of key-value pairs sorted by key, divided into blocks, with an index and a Bloom filter (lesson 07.11).
- **Levels**: SST files are organised into levels L0, L1, ..., L6. L0 holds freshly flushed files, which may overlap each other in key range. From L1 down, each level's files cover **non-overlapping** key ranges, and each level is about **10 times larger** than the one above.
- **Sequence number**: every write gets a monotonically increasing 56-bit number. Internally a key is stored as (user key, sequence number, type), where type is "value" or "deletion". The newest version of a key is the one with the highest sequence number. Snapshots and consistent reads are built on this.

%%tree The shape of a RocksDB LSM tree with default-style sizing. L0 files overlap; deeper levels are sorted runs about ten times larger each.
Memory
  *Active memtable | 64 MB, takes writes
  Immutable memtable | waiting to flush
Disk
  WAL | every write, for recovery
  L0 | 4 or so overlapping files of about 64 MB
  L1 | 256 MB, non-overlapping
  L2 | 2.5 GB
  L3 | 25 GB
  L4 | 250 GB
  L5 | 2.5 TB, most of the data lives here
%%

## A write, step by step
Here is what happens to `Put("user:42", value)`:
1. **Assign a sequence number.** The write is stamped with the next sequence number, say 9,001,234.
2. **Append to the WAL.** The record (key, value, sequence number, type) is appended to the current WAL file. Whether it is also fsynced depends on the write option `sync`. With `sync=true`, the write waits for the fsync; with `sync=false` (the default), it reaches the OS page cache and survives a process crash but not a power failure.
3. **Insert into the active memtable.** The skip list now holds the new entry, sorted among the others.
4. **Return to the caller.** That's it for the foreground path. No SST file was touched, nothing was read from disk, and no existing data was modified.
5. **Memtable fills.** When the active memtable reaches 64 MB, it is marked **immutable** and a fresh memtable (and usually a fresh WAL file) takes new writes. This switch takes microseconds.
6. **Flush.** A background thread iterates the immutable memtable in key order and writes it out as a new SST file in **L0**: data blocks, index, filter, footer. If the same key was written several times while in the memtable, only the newest version is written. The flush is a single sequential write of tens of megabytes.
7. **Retire the WAL.** Once the flush is durable and recorded in the **MANIFEST** (RocksDB's log of which files make up the database), the WAL file that covered that memtable is no longer needed and is deleted or recycled.
8. **Compaction.** When L0 has accumulated enough files (4 by default), a compaction merges them with the overlapping files in L1, producing new non-overlapping L1 files. When L1 exceeds its target size, some of its files are merged into L2, and so on down. Over its life, a key value moves down through the levels, rewritten at each step.

%%seq A RocksDB Put returns after the WAL append and memtable insert. Flush and compaction happen later on background threads, off the request path.
Client > DB : Put(user:42, v)
DB > WAL : append, seq 9001234
DB > Memtable : skip list insert
DB -> Client : OK, microseconds
Memtable > Memtable : 64 MB: immutable
Memtable > SST files : flush to new L0 file
SST files > WAL : old WAL retired
SST files > SST files : compact L0 into L1
%%

### Group commit
Many client threads write at once. RocksDB batches them: one thread becomes the **leader** of a write group, appends all the group's records to the WAL in one write (and one fsync, if requested), then the records are inserted into the memtable, possibly in parallel. This is the group commit idea from lesson 07.01, and it is why an LSM engine can sustain hundreds of thousands of small synced writes a second on a good SSD.

## The memtable's lifecycle
%%state A memtable's life: it takes writes until full, waits as an immutable table, is flushed to an L0 file, and is then freed along with its WAL.
[*] -> Active
Active -> Immutable : reaches write_buffer_size
Immutable -> Flushing : background flush thread picks it
Flushing -> *Flushed : SST written, MANIFEST updated
Flushed -> Freed : WAL deleted, memory released
%%

Reads can be served by the active memtable, any immutable memtables waiting to flush, and all the SST files, which is why a read path checks them in that order (lesson 07.11).

## What can slow a write down: stalls
The foreground path is designed to be fast, but it depends on background work keeping up. If it doesn't, RocksDB deliberately slows writers rather than let reads degrade without limit:
- **Too many memtables.** `max_write_buffer_number` (2 by default) caps active plus immutable memtables. If the active one fills while the previous one is still flushing, writes **stop** until the flush finishes.
- **Too many L0 files.** Every L0 file must be checked on a read because L0 files overlap. When L0 reaches 20 files (`level0_slowdown_writes_trigger`), writes are artificially slowed; at 36 (`level0_stop_writes_trigger`) they stop.
- **Too much pending compaction.** If the estimated bytes waiting to be compacted exceeds a threshold, writes are slowed, then stopped.

These **write stalls** are the LSM tree's characteristic failure mode: average latency looks great until ingest exceeds what flush and compaction can sustain, and then latency jumps from microseconds to seconds.

! Example: one write traced through time
! `Put("user:42", 120-byte value)` writes about 140 bytes to the WAL and the memtable and returns in about 10 µs. At 100 KB/s of writes, the 64 MB memtable takes about 11 minutes to fill; it is then flushed as a new L0 file in under a second.
! An hour later, an L0 to L1 compaction rewrites the record into an L1 file. Days later, as L1 overflows, it moves to L2; weeks later to L3. Each move rewrites it once along with its neighbours. If `user:42` was overwritten in the meantime, the older version is dropped in whichever compaction first sees both. The user only ever waited for the first step.

! Example: sizing flushes and stalls
! A service ingests 80 MB/s. With 64 MB memtables, a memtable fills every 0.8 seconds. Flushing 64 MB to an SSD that sustains 500 MB/s takes about 0.13 seconds, so flush keeps up easily. But compaction must also handle 80 MB/s entering L0, multiplied by the write amplification (say 15x): 1.2 GB/s of compaction writes, more than the disk can do.
! L0 files pile up: about 1.25 new files a second, so 20 files (the slowdown trigger) arrive within 15 to 20 seconds of compaction falling behind, and writes slow. Fixes: larger memtables, more compaction threads, a faster disk, tiered compaction with lower write amplification (lesson 07.12), or sharding the data across more machines.

## Why this design wins for writes
Compare the cost per write with a B-tree whose working set doesn't fit in memory:
| Step | B-tree insert | LSM insert |
| Durability | WAL append | WAL append |
| Data structure | Read leaf page (often a random disk read), modify | Insert into memory |
| Eventual disk write | Random 8 to 16 KB page write | Share of a sequential 64 MB flush |
| Background | Page splits, checkpoints | Compaction, rewriting data several times |

The LSM tree replaces a random read and a random write per insert with a sequential share of a big flush, plus later sequential compaction. On spinning disks that is a huge win; on SSDs it is still a large win in throughput and device wear.

## In an interview
If asked to walk through an LSM write, give the steps in order and say which are synchronous: "WAL append, memtable insert, acknowledge. Later, the memtable is frozen and flushed as a sorted SST file in L0; compaction merges it down into larger, non-overlapping levels; the WAL segment is deleted once its data is in SSTs." Then add one sentence on the risk: "if compaction can't keep up, L0 files pile up and the engine stalls writes, so I'd size disk bandwidth for ingest times write amplification."

> Takeaway: an LSM tree buffers writes in a sorted in-memory memtable, makes them durable with a sequential write-ahead log, and moves them to disk in large sorted batches. Background compaction merges those batches down through levels to keep reads manageable, and if it falls behind the engine stalls writes.

## Three pieces, three jobs
Lesson 07.09 followed a write through an LSM engine. This lesson looks closely at the three structures that write touches, because each one exists to solve exactly one problem, and seeing them separately makes the whole engine make sense:
- The **memtable** makes writes **fast**: it's in memory and keeps keys sorted.
- The **SSTable** makes data **compact and searchable on disk**: sorted, immutable, indexed, compressed.
- The **WAL** makes writes **durable** in between: until a memtable is safely flushed, the log is the only copy on disk.

The question at the heart of this lesson is the one people usually ask first: if the memtable is going to be flushed to an SSTable anyway, why write a WAL at all?

%%layers Where a recent write lives at each moment, and which copy makes it survive a crash.
*WAL on disk | durable copy until its memtable is flushed
Active memtable | in RAM, takes new writes, sorted
Immutable memtable | in RAM, frozen, waiting to flush
SST files in L0 and below | on disk, sorted, immutable, durable
%%

## The memtable
The memtable is an in-memory sorted map from key to value. All writes go into it, and reads check it first because it holds the newest data.

### Why a skip list
RocksDB's default memtable is a **skip list**. Picture a sorted linked list where some nodes also have "express" pointers that skip ahead 2, 4, 8 or more nodes. A search starts on the highest express lane, drops down a lane when it would overshoot, and finds any key in O(log n) steps. Two properties make it ideal here:
- **Sorted order for free.** Flushing is just walking the bottom lane from start to end, producing keys already in order for the SST file.
- **Concurrency.** Inserting a node only requires updating a few pointers with atomic compare-and-swap operations, so many threads can read while writers insert, without locks. RocksDB supports concurrent inserts only with the skip list.

Other memtable types exist for special workloads: hash-based skip lists or linked lists that bucket keys by a prefix (fast when every lookup includes a known prefix, but slow for full ordered scans), and a vector memtable for bulk loads where keys are sorted once at flush time.

Memory for the memtable comes from an **arena**: large chunks allocated up front, from which small entries are carved. Nothing is freed individually. The whole arena is released at once when the memtable is flushed, which avoids allocator overhead and fragmentation.

### The knobs
- `write_buffer_size` (64 MB by default): how big one memtable grows before it is frozen.
- `max_write_buffer_number` (2 by default): the maximum number of memtables, active plus immutable. If the limit is reached because flushes are slow, writes stall.
- `db_write_buffer_size`: a cap on total memtable memory across all column families (a **column family** is a separate keyspace in one RocksDB instance with its own memtables and SST files, a bit like a table).

## The write-ahead log
The memtable is RAM. Between a write and the next flush, which could be minutes and tens of megabytes later, that write exists nowhere on disk **except in the WAL**. If the process crashes or the machine loses power, RocksDB rebuilds the lost memtables by replaying the WAL.

### The WAL's format
RocksDB inherits its log format from LevelDB. The file is divided into **32 KB blocks**. Each record has a small header (a 4-byte checksum, a 2-byte length and a 1-byte type) followed by the payload, which is a batch of writes with their sequence number. A record that doesn't fit in the rest of a block is split into fragments tagged FIRST, MIDDLE and LAST, while one that fits is tagged FULL. The fixed block structure means a reader that hits a corrupted region can skip to the next 32 KB boundary and resynchronise rather than losing everything after it.

### Durability choices
The WAL makes data durable only as far as you ask it to:

%%matrix What survives each kind of failure under different WAL settings. Faster settings give up protection against power loss or any crash.
Setting | Process crash | Power loss | Write cost
WAL, sync on every write | yes | yes | partial, an fsync per group
WAL, no sync (default) | yes | no, last seconds lost | yes, cheap
WAL, periodic fsync | yes | partial, last interval | yes, cheap
disableWAL | no, memtables lost | no | yes, cheapest
%%

- `sync=true` on a write: the call returns only after the WAL is fsynced. Group commit amortises this across concurrent writers.
- `sync=false` (the default): the WAL write reaches the OS page cache. A **process** crash loses nothing, because the kernel still holds the data and will write it. A **power** failure can lose the last few seconds.
- `disableWAL`: no log at all. Useful for bulk loads you can redo from source, or caches. Every unflushed write is lost on any crash.

On recovery, RocksDB reads the WAL files newer than the last flush and replays them. A **recovery mode** decides what to do with damage: the default **point-in-time recovery** replays up to the first corrupted record and stops there, giving a consistent prefix of history. Stricter modes refuse to open on any corruption; looser ones skip bad records.

### When WAL files are deleted
A WAL file can be deleted once **every** memtable whose writes it contains has been flushed. With several column families sharing one WAL, a rarely written column family can hold a tiny memtable that never fills, pinning old WAL files forever. `max_total_wal_size` solves this: when the total size of live WAL files exceeds it, RocksDB forces a flush of the column families holding the oldest data, so those WAL files can go.

### Flush triggers, in summary
A memtable is flushed when:
- it reaches `write_buffer_size`;
- total memtable memory across column families reaches `db_write_buffer_size`;
- total WAL size reaches `max_total_wal_size`;
- a user asks for it (manual flush, or before a backup or shutdown).

## The SSTable
The flush turns a memtable into an **SST file** (sorted string table). It is written once, sequentially, and never modified. RocksDB's default "block-based table" layout looks like this:

%%tree Inside a block-based SST file. A reader starts at the fixed-size footer and works backwards to find the index and filter.
SST file
  Data block 1 | about 4 KB of sorted key-values, compressed
  Data block 2
  Data block N
  Meta blocks
    *Filter block | Bloom filter for all keys
    Properties block | entry count, key range, sizes
    Range deletion block | range tombstones
  Metaindex block | where each meta block is
  Index block | last key and offset of each data block
  Footer | fixed size, magic number, offsets
%%

- **Data blocks** (4 KB by default before compression) hold sorted key-value entries. Keys within a block are **prefix-compressed**: each key stores only the bytes that differ from the previous key, with periodic **restart points** where a full key is stored, so a binary search can jump in.
- The **index block** has one entry per data block: a separator key at least as large as the block's last key, and a handle (offset and size). A lookup binary-searches the index, then reads one data block.
- The **filter block** holds a Bloom filter so lookups for absent keys can skip the file (lesson 07.11).
- The **footer** is a fixed size at the end of the file and ends with a magic number (lesson 07.04's checklist again). A reader opens the file by reading the footer, then the metaindex and index blocks it points to.
- Every block carries a checksum, verified when read.

Because the file is immutable it can be compressed in whole blocks (LZ4, Snappy or Zstandard), cached without invalidation, copied by backups while the database runs, and shared between checkpoints by hard links.

## Why the WAL is still needed
Now the main question can be answered precisely. Flushing an SST file on every write would make every write a full file creation: write the data, index, filter and footer, fsync, update the MANIFEST. That would destroy the whole point of batching tens of megabytes into one sequential write. The WAL is the cheap way to cover the gap: an append of a few hundred bytes, fsynced in groups, that records each write until its memtable lands safely in an SST.

%%timeline A crash between flushes. The memtable's 40 MB is lost from RAM but fully rebuilt from the WAL on restart.
10:00:00 | Memtable flushed to L0, new WAL 000123 started
10:00 to 10:07 | 40 MB of writes into memtable and WAL 000123
10:07:12 | *Power loss: memtable gone from RAM
10:08:30 | Restart: MANIFEST lists SSTs up to 10:00
10:08:31 | Replay WAL 000123: memtable rebuilt, 40 MB
10:08:33 | Accepting writes again, nothing acknowledged was lost
%%

! Example: crash between flushes
! A node accepts 40 MB of writes into its 64 MB memtable, with `sync=true` for each write group, then loses power. On restart there are no new SST files since the last flush, but the WAL holds all 40 MB. RocksDB replays it at several hundred MB/s into a fresh memtable, which takes well under a second, and carries on. Every acknowledged write is present.
! With `sync=false`, writes from roughly the last few seconds (whatever the OS hadn't yet written) could be missing, though everything before that survives. With `disableWAL`, all 40 MB would be gone, and the database would come back as it was at 10:00.

! Example: how big the WAL gets and how long recovery takes
! A service writes 20 MB/s with 64 MB memtables and `max_write_buffer_number = 2`. At most about two memtables' worth, 128 MB, is unflushed at any time, so live WAL is normally around 64 to 128 MB and a crash means replaying at most that, a second or two.
! Add a second column family that receives only 10 KB/s. Its memtable would take almost two hours to fill, and until it flushes, every WAL file written in that time must be kept: about 130 GB of WAL at 20 MB/s. Setting `max_total_wal_size` to, say, 1 GB forces that small memtable to flush whenever the WAL total reaches 1 GB, keeping disk use and recovery time bounded.

## Common mistakes
- **Assuming the default WAL is power-safe.** Without sync, it protects against process crashes, not power loss.
- **Disabling the WAL for speed** on data you can't regenerate.
- **Huge memtables to reduce compaction**, which also mean long WAL replays after a crash and bigger stalls when a flush is slow.
- **Forgetting cold column families** pin old WAL files.

## In an interview
If you mention an LSM-based store, say how it stays durable: "Every write goes to a write-ahead log and the memtable before it is acknowledged; the WAL is fsynced in groups; on a crash the memtable is rebuilt by replaying the WAL; WAL segments are deleted once their memtable is flushed into an immutable SSTable." That one sentence shows you understand the three pieces and why each exists.

> Takeaway: the memtable makes writes fast and sorted in RAM, the SSTable makes them compact, indexed and immutable on disk, and the WAL makes them durable in between. Remove the WAL and every write since the last flush is lost on a crash; skip its fsync and power loss can take the last few seconds.

## Where a read has to look
An LSM tree never overwrites data in place, so several versions of the same key can exist at once: one in the memtable, an older one in an L0 file, an even older one down in L3. The rule that makes reads correct is simple: **the newest version wins**. So a point lookup (`Get(key)`) searches from newest to oldest and stops at the first match:
1. The **active memtable**.
2. Any **immutable memtables** waiting to flush, newest first.
3. Every **L0 file**, newest first. L0 files are flushed memtables and their key ranges overlap, so the key could be in any of them.
4. **At most one file per level** from L1 down. Within a level, files cover non-overlapping key ranges, so the engine looks up which single file's range contains the key (a binary search over file boundaries held in memory) and checks only that one.

If the first match is a value, that's the answer. If it's a **tombstone** (a deletion marker), the answer is "not found", and the search stops there, because anything older is dead.

### Why missing keys are the worst case
A key that exists, especially a recently written one, is often found early, in the memtable or a shallow level. The search stops. A key that **doesn't exist** has no match anywhere, so the search can't stop early: it must check the memtables, every L0 file, and one file in every level below. With 4 L0 files and 6 levels, that's 10 files. If each check meant a disk read, a lookup for a missing key would cost 10 random reads, which on an SSD is about 1 ms and on a hard disk about 100 ms. That's **read amplification** at its worst (lesson 07.12).

And missing-key lookups are common: "does this username exist?", "have I seen this event ID before?" (deduplication), "is this URL already crawled?", or an insert that must first check the key is new. This lesson is about the two structures that make those lookups cheap: an index inside each file, and a Bloom filter in front of it.

%%flow A point read checks newest to oldest and stops at the first hit. Bloom filters let it skip most files without any disk read.
Get(k) -> Memtables -(miss)-> L0 files -(Bloom: no)-> L1 skip -(Bloom: no)-> L2 skip -(Bloom: maybe)-> *L3: read 1 block -> Found
%%

## Inside one SST file: the index block
Suppose the engine has decided to look in one SST file. The file may be 64 MB or larger, so it must not scan it. As lesson 07.10 showed, the file is made of **data blocks** of about 4 KB, plus an **index block** with one entry per data block: a separator key that is at least the last key in that block, and a **block handle** (offset and size).

A lookup binary-searches the index for the first entry whose separator is greater than or equal to the target key. That entry names the one data block that could contain the key. The engine reads that block (from the **block cache** if it's there, otherwise from disk), then searches within it, using the block's restart points to binary-search the prefix-compressed keys. One block read per file, at most.

How big is the index? A 64 MB file with 4 KB blocks has about 16,000 blocks; at about 30 bytes per index entry that's around 500 KB, under 1% of the file. For very large files, RocksDB can **partition** the index: split it into pieces with a small top-level index over them, so only the top level must stay in memory and the rest is cached on demand like data blocks.

## Bloom filters
The index tells you which block **might** hold the key, but it can't tell you the key is absent without reading that block. That's still one disk read per file for a missing key. A **Bloom filter** removes most of those reads.

### How a Bloom filter works
A Bloom filter is a bit array of m bits, all starting at 0, plus k hash functions.
- **Add a key**: hash it k times, giving k bit positions, and set those bits to 1.
- **Check a key**: hash it the same k ways. If **any** of those bits is 0, the key was **definitely never added**. If all are 1, the key is **probably** present: those bits might all have been set by other keys.

So a Bloom filter can produce **false positives** (says "maybe" for an absent key) but **never false negatives** (it never says "no" for a key that is there). That asymmetry is exactly what a read path needs: a "no" lets you skip the file safely; a "maybe" costs one wasted block read at worst.

%%seq Checking one SST file for a key. The filter answers from memory; only a "maybe" leads to an index lookup and a block read.
Read path > Bloom filter : test k bits
Bloom filter -> Read path : a bit is 0: absent
Read path > Read path : skip file, no I/O
Read path > Bloom filter : next file: test
Bloom filter -> Read path : all 1: maybe
Read path > Index block : binary search
Index block -> Read path : block 2,417
Read path > Block cache : get block 2,417
Block cache -> Read path : miss: 4 KB disk read
%%

### The numbers
The false-positive rate depends on how many bits you spend per key. With the best choice of k (about 0.7 × bits per key), the trade-off is:

%%bars Bloom filter false-positive rate by bits per key. Each extra 4.8 bits or so cuts the false-positive rate about tenfold.
4.8 bits per key | 10 | 10% false positives
*9.6 bits per key | 1 | 1% false positives
14.4 bits per key | 0.1 | 0.1% false positives
%%

The usual default is about 10 bits per key, giving roughly a 1% false-positive rate. At that setting, a filter costs **1.25 bytes per key**. A database with 1 billion keys spends about 1.25 GB of memory on filters, which is affordable on a server and saves vast numbers of disk reads.

RocksDB offers variants:
- **Full filter** (the default today): one filter per SST file, covering all its keys, checked before reading any data block. The older **block-based filter** kept one small filter per data block, which meant reading the index first and checking more filters.
- **Partitioned filters**: like partitioned indexes, the filter is split into pieces with a top-level index so huge files don't need the whole filter in memory.
- **Ribbon filter**: a newer structure that saves about **30% of the memory** for the same false-positive rate, at the cost of more CPU to build. Good for the large, rarely rewritten bottom levels.
- **Prefix Bloom filters**: hash only a key prefix (say, the user ID part of `user42:order:...`), so a filter can answer "are there any keys with this prefix?", which helps prefix scans.

### What filters can't do
A normal Bloom filter answers "is this exact key here?". It can't help with a **range scan** ("all keys from A to B"), because a range has no single key to hash. Range scans instead open an iterator on every memtable and every relevant file in every level, and merge them, like a k-way merge sort, taking the newest version of each key. That's why LSM range scans are more expensive than B-tree range scans, and why compaction (fewer, larger sorted runs) matters so much for scan-heavy workloads.

## Caching: keeping it in memory
Filters and indexes only help if checking them doesn't itself need a disk read. RocksDB's **block cache** (an LRU cache of uncompressed blocks in memory) holds data blocks, and can be told to hold index and filter blocks too, with high priority, or to **pin** the index and filter blocks of L0 files (which are checked on almost every read). The OS page cache holds compressed file pages on top of that. A well-tuned engine has every filter and index block in memory, so a lookup's only possible disk read is the final data block.

! Example: the cost of a missing-key lookup, with and without filters
! An LSM tree has 4 L0 files and levels L1 to L6, so a missing key means checking 10 files. Without filters, each check is an index lookup (in cache) plus one 4 KB data block read (usually not in cache for random keys): about 10 block reads, roughly 10 × 100 µs = 1 ms on an NVMe SSD.
! With 10-bit filters, each file's filter is checked in memory in well under a microsecond, and only about 1% of files give a false "maybe". Expected block reads: 10 × 0.01 = 0.1, so about 10 µs of disk time on average. That's roughly 100 times fewer disk reads, for 1.25 bytes of memory per key.

! Example: checking usernames
! A signup form checks whether a name is taken, and 95% of checks are for names that don't exist. The username table lives in an LSM store where a lookup touches 6 files. Without filters, every missing-name check reads 6 blocks: at 20,000 checks a second that's 114,000 random reads a second, beyond what a single SSD comfortably sustains alongside other work.
! With 10 bits per key, each file is skipped 99% of the time, so a missing-name check averages about 0.06 block reads: about 1,140 random reads a second for the missing names, plus at most a few block reads each for the 1,000 names a second that do exist. The filters turned an I/O-bound endpoint into a memory-bound one.

%%compare The same lookup for a key that doesn't exist, with and without Bloom filters, in a tree with 10 files to check.
[No filters]
Memtable -(miss)-> 10 files -(1 block each)-> Not found
- About 10 random block reads
- About 1 ms on NVMe
- Misses cost the most
[*10-bit Bloom filters]
Memtable -(miss)-> 10 filters in RAM -(about 0.1 reads)-> Not found
+ About 0.1 block reads on average
+ Costs 1.25 bytes of RAM per key
- No help for range scans
%%

## Tuning levers and pitfalls
- **Bits per key.** More bits, fewer false positives, more memory. Many deployments use 10; some skip filters on the last level (`optimize_filters_for_hits`) when nearly every lookup is for a key that exists, because the last level holds about 90% of the data and its filter is the most expensive.
- **Filters not in memory.** If filter blocks are evicted from the cache, checking them costs a disk read, which defeats the purpose. Pin or prioritise them.
- **Too many L0 files.** Every L0 file must be checked; a compaction backlog raises read cost directly (and triggers write stalls, lesson 07.09).
- **Hot keys with many versions.** A frequently updated key may be found quickly, but range scans must skip all its older versions and tombstones until compaction removes them. Scans through regions full of tombstones are a classic Cassandra performance problem.

## In an interview
If the design uses an LSM store for lookups, especially existence checks, say: "Reads go newest to oldest and stop at the first hit, so missing keys are the expensive case; each SST file has a Bloom filter in memory, about 10 bits per key for a 1% false-positive rate, so most files are skipped without I/O, and an index block narrows each remaining file to one 4 KB block." It's a crisp answer that shows you understand both the data structure and the cost model.

> Takeaway: an LSM read searches memtables, every L0 file and one file per lower level, newest to oldest, stopping at the first hit, so a missing key must search everywhere. A Bloom filter per file, about 10 bits per key in memory, says "definitely not here" for 99% of files that lack the key, and the index block narrows each remaining file to a single block read.

?? A lookup must return the newest version, so it checks the memtables, then every overlapping L0 file, then one file per level, from newest to oldest, stopping at the first match. ## Three amplifications
To compare storage engines honestly you need a common currency. The standard one is **amplification**: how much more work the engine does than the application asked for. There are three kinds:
- **Read amplification**: the amount of work done per logical read, usually counted as disk reads (or blocks read) per lookup. You can measure it for a cold cache (the worst case) or with a realistic cache (the average case). A B-tree point lookup with the upper levels cached has a read amplification of about 1. An LSM lookup that checks 10 files without filters has about 10.
- **Write amplification**: the bytes physically written to storage divided by the bytes the application wrote. If you insert 1 GB and the disk ends up writing 20 GB (log, flush, every compaction rewrite), write amplification is 20.
- **Space amplification**: the size of the database on disk divided by the size of the live data in it. Dead versions, tombstones, half-empty pages and temporary compaction space all push it above 1.

These three trade off against each other. An engine can be very good at two of them, but making all three minimal at once appears impossible; this is sometimes called the **RUM conjecture** (Read, Update, Memory). It explains why no engine beats another on everything, and why the right question is always "which costs can this workload afford?"

%%matrix Where each design sits on the three amplifications. Every option is good at two at most.
Design | Read amp | Write amp | Space amp
B-tree | yes, low | no, page rewrites | partial, half-full pages
LSM leveled | partial, about 1 file per level | no, 10 to 30x | yes, about 1.1x
LSM tiered | no, many runs to check | yes, low | no, up to 2x or more
Append log, hash index | yes, one seek | yes, low until merge | no, garbage until merge
%%

The costs also land at different times. A B-tree pays immediately: to modify a page it must first read it, and it writes the page back (eventually) on every change. An LSM tree **defers** cost to background compaction, which is why its foreground writes are fast and why it can fall behind. Space amplification comes from different places too: fragmentation and half-full pages in a B-tree, old versions and tombstones in an LSM.

## What compaction does
Compaction is the background process that merges SST files. It has three jobs:
1. **Bound read amplification** by reducing the number of files a read must check.
2. **Reclaim space** by dropping overwritten values and tombstones whose older versions have been merged away.
3. **Keep data sorted** in long runs so range scans merge few iterators.

Every compaction reads some input files, does a streaming k-way merge by key (keeping the newest version of each key), writes new output files and deletes the inputs. The **strategy** for choosing which files to merge, and when, decides the amplification trade-off. RocksDB offers two main ones.

## Leveled compaction
**Leveled compaction** (the RocksDB and LevelDB default) organises SST files into levels L1, L2, ... where:
- each level has a **target size**, about **10 times** larger than the level above (the **fan-out**);
- within a level, files have **non-overlapping** key ranges, so each level is one sorted run.

When a level exceeds its target, the engine picks a file from it and merges it with **all the files in the next level whose key ranges overlap it**. Because the next level is ten times larger, a 64 MB file typically overlaps about ten 64 MB files below. So pushing 64 MB down one level means reading and rewriting roughly 640 MB to 700 MB. Every byte gets rewritten like this once per level as it sinks.

%%timeline One flushed file's data as it sinks through leveled compaction. Each step down rewrites it together with the overlapping files of a level ten times larger.
t0 | Flush: 64 MB memtable becomes an L0 file (written once)
t0 + 5 min | L0 to L1: merged with overlapping L1 data
t0 + 1 hour | L1 to L2: merged with about 10 overlapping L2 files
t0 + 1 day | L2 to L3: rewritten again with about 10 files
t0 + 2 weeks | *L3 to L4: rewritten again; most data ends at the last level
%%

**Write amplification** adds up: about 1 for the WAL, 1 for the flush, then (in the worst case) up to about the fan-out for each level. In practice leveled compaction typically lands at **10 to 30**.

**Read amplification** is low: one file per level (plus L0 files), and Bloom filters skip most of them (lesson 07.11).

**Space amplification** is excellent, about **1.1**. The last level holds about 90% of the data, and the levels above it are at most about 11% extra in total. Even if every upper-level entry is an overwrite of something in the last level, the dead data is bounded at roughly 10%.

## Tiered (universal) compaction
**Tiered compaction** (called **universal** in RocksDB, and size-tiered in Cassandra) does less rewriting. Instead of keeping each level as one sorted run, it lets several **sorted runs** of similar size accumulate, and merges them together only when enough have piled up. Think of it as: four 64 MB runs merge into one 256 MB run; four of those merge into one 1 GB run; and so on.

Each byte is rewritten roughly once per tier, and there are few tiers, so **write amplification is lower**, often 4 to 10.

The costs show up elsewhere:
- **Read amplification** is higher: many overlapping runs must be checked, not one per level.
- **Space amplification** is higher: duplicates of a key linger in several runs until they happen to be merged, and a big merge of the largest runs needs temporary space equal to their size. A full compaction can briefly need **double** the data size on disk. RocksDB's `max_size_amplification_percent` (200 by default) triggers a full merge when the extra space exceeds that percentage.

%%compare The two main compaction strategies. Leveled rewrites more to keep reads and space tight; tiered rewrites less and pays in reads and space.
[Leveled]
L0 -(merge with overlap)-> L1 -> L2 (10x) -> L3 (10x)
+ Read amp: one run per level
+ Space amp about 1.1x
- Write amp often 10 to 30x
[Tiered / universal]
4 runs of 64 MB -(merge)-> 1 run of 256 MB -> 1 GB
+ Write amp often 4 to 10x
- More runs to check per read
- Duplicates linger, up to 2x+ space
%%

%%widget amp Rule-of-thumb amplification for leveled and tiered compaction. Adjust the settings to see how the three costs move against each other; real numbers depend on workload and tuning.
%%

| | Leveled | Tiered / universal |
| Write amplification | High (10 to 30x) | Low (4 to 10x) |
| Read amplification | Low | Higher |
| Space amplification | Low (about 1.1x) | Higher, can reach 2x or more |
| Temporary space | Small per compaction | Up to the size of the data |
| Good for | Read-heavy, space-sensitive, mixed | Write-heavy ingest, bulk loads |

### Other strategies worth knowing
- **Time-window compaction** (Cassandra's TWCS): for time-series data with a TTL, group data by time window and never merge across windows. When a whole window expires, delete its files outright with no compaction at all.
- **FIFO compaction**: keep files in order and simply delete the oldest when a size limit is reached. Perfect for caches and logs where old data is worthless.
- **Hybrids**: many systems use tiered compaction for the upper levels (to absorb writes cheaply) and leveled at the bottom (to keep reads and space tight).

## Why write amplification matters
Write amplification looks abstract until you price it:
- **Disk bandwidth.** Every rewrite competes with foreground reads and writes. If you ingest 100 MB/s at 20x, the disk must sustain 2 GB/s of compaction writes plus the reads to feed them.
- **SSD endurance.** Flash wears out. Drives are rated in **TBW** (terabytes written) or **DWPD** (drive writes per day). Application-level write amplification multiplies with the drive's own internal write amplification (lesson 07.01).
- **CPU.** Each rewrite decompresses, merges and recompresses data.

! Example: an SSD's lifetime
! An app writes 50 GB a day. With leveled compaction at about 20x write amplification, the SSD absorbs about 1 TB a day of writes. A drive rated for 1,000 TB written lasts about 1,000 days, under 3 years, before it reaches its rated endurance.
! Switching to universal compaction at about 5x cuts the daily writes to 250 GB, so the same drive lasts over 10 years of writes. The price: reads check more sorted runs (more Bloom filter checks and more false-positive block reads), and the disk must keep up to about half its capacity free for full merges, so you buy roughly twice the raw capacity for the same data.

! Example: adding up leveled write amplification
! Take a database of 1 TB with a 256 MB L1 and fan-out 10: L1 256 MB, L2 2.5 GB, L3 25 GB, L4 250 GB, L5 2.5 TB (only partly full). A newly written byte is written to the WAL (1), flushed to L0 (1), merged into L1 (about 1 to 2, since L0 and L1 are similar sizes), then into L2, L3, L4 and L5.
! If each of those four moves rewrites it with, on average, about 5 times its size of overlapping data below (half the fan-out, since overlap varies), that's about 4 × 5 = 20. Total: about 1 + 1 + 2 + 20 = 24. Matches the "10 to 30" rule of thumb, and shows how a larger fan-out (fewer levels but more overlap each) or a smaller one (more levels) moves the number.

%%bars Daily bytes written to the SSD for 50 GB of application writes under each strategy.
Application writes | 50 | 50 GB
Tiered, 5x | 250 | 250 GB
*Leveled, 20x | 1000 | 1 TB
%%

## Common mistakes
- **Comparing engines on one axis.** "LSM has lower write amplification than a B-tree" is often true, but not always: a leveled LSM can rewrite each byte 20 times. The comparison is "fewer, larger, sequential writes" versus "small random page writes".
- **Ignoring temporary space.** Tiered compaction can need as much free space as the data itself during a full merge.
- **Not budgeting compaction bandwidth.** Ingest that the disk can absorb once may be far more than it can absorb twenty times.
- **Using size-tiered for read-heavy, update-heavy data.** Many runs, many duplicates, slow reads and wasted space.

## In an interview
Define the three amplifications in one breath each, then say which you'd sacrifice: "This is an append-mostly metrics store with TTL, so I'd use time-window or tiered compaction to keep write amplification low; reads mostly hit recent windows so extra runs don't hurt much." Or: "This is a read-heavy user profile store, so leveled compaction: reads check about one file per level and space overhead is about 10%, at the cost of rewriting data 10 to 30 times." Mention SSD endurance if write volume is large.

> Takeaway: compaction trades read, write and space amplification against each other, and no strategy wins all three. Leveled keeps one sorted run per level, so reads and space stay tight but every byte is rewritten once per level (10 to 30x); tiered merges similar-sized runs only when several pile up, so it rewrites much less but leaves more runs to search and more duplicate data on disk.

?? ## Two kinds of storage for two kinds of data
Most applications store two very different kinds of data side by side:
- **Structured records**: users, orders, comments, the caption and owner of a photo. Each is small (bytes to kilobytes), changes often, and is looked up, filtered, joined and updated in many ways.
- **Blobs** (binary large objects): the photo itself, a video, a PDF, a backup, a log archive, a Parquet file. Each is large (kilobytes to terabytes), written once, never edited in part, and read whole or by byte range.

A **database** is built for the first kind: indexes, queries, transactions, in-place updates. **Object storage** (S3, Google Cloud Storage, Azure Blob Storage, or self-hosted systems like Ceph and MinIO) is built for the second: a flat namespace of keys, each mapped to an immutable object, with enormous capacity, very high durability and a low price per byte. Big systems use both, and the standard pattern is to put the bytes in the object store and the facts about them in the database.

%%matrix What each kind of store is good at. They are almost exact complements.
Property | Relational database | Object storage
Query by any field, joins | yes | no, key or prefix only
Transactions across records | yes | no, one object at a time
Update part of a value | yes | no, replace whole object
Cost per GB stored | no, high | yes, low
Capacity | partial, one cluster | yes, effectively unlimited
Large sequential reads | partial | yes, parallel range GETs
Serve directly to browsers or a CDN | no | yes, signed URLs
%%

## Why not put blobs in the database?
It works, and for small, few blobs it can be fine. At scale it hurts in several ways at once:
- **Cost.** Database storage is fast SSD, usually replicated two or three times, plus backups. Object storage is dense hard disks with erasure coding. The price difference per gigabyte is often five to ten times.
- **Memory pollution.** A database's buffer pool is meant to hold hot index and row pages. Reading a 5 MB image through it evicts thousands of useful pages.
- **Replication and backup.** Every blob write goes through the write-ahead log and to every replica. A burst of uploads can create replication lag for the whole database, and nightly backups grow with every photo ever uploaded.
- **Serving.** To show an image, the app server must read the blob from the database and stream it through itself. With object storage, a client or CDN fetches it directly, and the app server never touches the bytes.
- **Limits and operations.** Databases have practical limits on value size (PostgreSQL caps a single field at 1 GB; drivers and protocols often have lower limits), and very large tables are slow to migrate, vacuum and restore.

Object storage has the reverse problems. It can't answer "all photos by user 42 taken in March, newest first" without listing keys and reading each object. It has no transactions across objects and no partial updates. That's why the two are combined rather than chosen between.

*Object storage, standard | 23 | about $23
Object storage, infrequent access | 12.5 | about $12.50
Block SSD volume | 80 | about $80
Managed database storage x 3 replicas | 345 | about $345
%%

## Case study: Facebook's Haystack
Haystack, Facebook's photo store described in 2010, is the classic example of why blobs need their own system, and of what a blob store looks like inside.

### The problem
Photos were first stored on NFS-mounted network filers, **one file per photo**. At the scale involved (about 260 billion images and 20 PB, with 1 billion new photos a week, roughly 60 TB, and peaks of over 1 million images served per second), the filesystem's own **metadata** became the bottleneck. To read one photo, the server had to read the directory entry to find the file's inode, read the inode to find the data's location, then read the data: **3 or more disk operations**, and the metadata for billions of files didn't fit in memory to cache. Most of the disk time was spent finding photos, not reading them.

### The fix: pack photos into big files, index them in RAM
Haystack packs many photos, called **needles**, into large append-only files of about **100 GB** called **physical volumes**. Each needle on disk carries a header (a random **cookie** to prevent guessing URLs, the photo's key, flags, size), the data, and a checksum. Each storage machine keeps an in-memory map from photo key to `(volume, offset, size)`: about **10 bytes of memory per photo** in its optimised form. A read becomes one memory lookup and **one disk operation**.

%%compare Reading one photo before and after Haystack. Moving per-photo metadata into a small in-memory index turns several disk operations into one.
[NFS, one file per photo]
Read -(directory entry)-> Inode -(block map)-> Data
- 3 or more disk operations per photo
- Billions of inodes do not fit in RAM
- Most disk time spent on metadata
[*Haystack, packed volumes]
Read -(RAM index, about 10 bytes)-> Volume offset -> Data
+ 1 disk operation per photo
+ Index for millions of photos fits in RAM
+ Large append-only files, sequential writes
%%

Three components work together:
- **Directory**: maps each **logical volume** to the physical volumes (on different machines) that replicate it, decides which logical volume a new upload goes to, marks full volumes read-only, and builds the photo URL, which encodes the volume and photo IDs so the read path needs no further lookup.
- **Cache**: an internal cache in front of the stores for popular, recently uploaded photos (which receive most reads), complementing the external CDN.
- **Store**: the machines holding physical volumes and their in-memory indexes.

Deletes simply set a "deleted" flag in the in-memory index and on the needle. Space is reclaimed later by **compaction**: copy live needles to a new volume file, skipping deleted ones, then swap. That mattered: about 25% of photos were deleted each year. If this sounds familiar, it should: it's Bitcask's design (lesson 07.08) specialised for photos.

## The cloud object store view
S3 is the same idea at a much larger scale: hundreds of microservices over a storage fleet of millions of hard disks, designed for eleven nines (99.999999999%) of durability. Because every customer's data is spread over huge numbers of drives, a single customer's burst can be served by over a million disks at once, a level of parallel throughput no database cluster offers for blobs. Lessons 07.14 to 07.20 look inside a system like this.

## The usual split, done properly
- **Object store**: the bytes. Images, video, documents, backups, logs, Parquet files.
- **Database**: the metadata. Owner, object key, size, content type, checksum, created time, permissions, processing status, and any searchable attributes. Indexed for the queries the product needs.

The tricky part is keeping the two consistent, since there is no transaction spanning both. The standard ordering:
1. **Write the bytes first** (to a new, unique key, never overwriting an existing one).
2. **Then commit the metadata row** pointing at that key.
3. **Garbage-collect orphans**: objects with no metadata row (because step 2 failed or the user abandoned the upload) are found by a periodic sweep, or by a lifecycle rule on a staging prefix, and deleted.

With this order, a metadata row never points at missing bytes; the worst case is unreferenced bytes, which cost a little storage until the sweep removes them.

%%seq A direct-to-storage upload with a pre-signed URL. The app server never handles the image bytes; the metadata row is written only after the object exists.
Client > App server : want to upload
App server > App server : pick key, sign URL
App server -> Client : pre-signed PUT URL
Client > Object store : PUT bytes + checksum
Object store -> Client : 200 OK
Client > App server : done: key, checksum
App server > Database : INSERT photo row
App server -> Client : photo created
%%

A **pre-signed URL** is a URL with an embedded signature that grants permission for one specific operation (PUT this key, before this time). It lets the client upload straight to the object store without credentials and without the bytes passing through your servers. Reads work the same way, often through a CDN that pulls from the object store.

! Example: a photo-sharing app
! Each upload goes to object storage at `photos/{user}/{uuid}.jpg`. A row in PostgreSQL stores the user, caption, upload time, the key, the size and a SHA-256. The feed query hits PostgreSQL by user and time, an indexed query returning 50 rows of about 200 bytes each. The client then fetches each image through a CDN that pulls from object storage on a miss.
! Sizing: 10 million uploads a day at 2 MB average is 20 TB a day of bytes, about 600 TB a month, which object storage absorbs at tens of thousands of dollars a month. The metadata is 10 million rows a day at about 500 bytes each, 5 GB a day, under 2 TB a year: a comfortably sized database. Putting the 600 TB a month in PostgreSQL instead would be operationally and financially absurd.

! Example: when an upload half-fails
! A user's upload succeeds to the object store, but the app server crashes before inserting the metadata row. The photo doesn't appear in their feed, so they upload again, which creates a new key and a new row. The first object is now an orphan. A nightly job lists keys created more than 24 hours ago under `photos/` and checks them against the database (or a lifecycle rule expires a staging prefix after a day), deleting about 0.1% of objects as orphans. No user ever saw a broken image, because no row ever pointed at missing bytes.

## When small blobs can stay in the database
Not every blob needs an object store. Avatars of a few kilobytes, small thumbnails or short documents, in modest numbers, can live in the database (or even inline in the row) to keep one system and one transaction. Benchmarks of embedded databases suggest blobs under roughly 100 KB can even be read faster from the database than from separate files. The decision flips when blobs are large, numerous, or need to be served directly to clients.

## Common mistakes
- **Overwriting objects in place** under the same key, so the metadata and bytes can disagree during the update. Use a new key per version.
- **Metadata first, bytes second.** A crash leaves a row pointing at nothing, and users see broken images.
- **No orphan cleanup.** Storage bills grow with failed uploads forever.
- **Proxying every download through app servers**, which wastes bandwidth and CPU; serve via signed URLs and a CDN.
- **Listing the object store to answer product queries.** Listing is slow and paginated; the database is the index.

## In an interview
This pattern comes up in nearly every design with media: "Uploads go directly to object storage via a pre-signed URL; once the object exists, we write a metadata row with key, size, checksum and owner to the database; reads come through a CDN; a background job removes orphaned objects." Mention why: cost per byte, durability, and that the database stays small and fast. If asked about consistency between the two, describe the bytes-first ordering and garbage collection.

> Takeaway: put large immutable blobs in object storage and the small, queryable facts about them in a database. Write the bytes first under a new key, then commit the metadata row, and garbage-collect orphans; each system then does cheaply and quickly the job the other does badly.

?? Images are large, written once and read whole, which object storage serves cheaply (dense disks, erasure coding, several times cheaper per GB), very durably and at huge parallel scale, directly to clients or a CDN through signed URLs. Databases are expensive per byte and suffer when filled with blobs: the buffer pool is polluted, the WAL, replicas and backups balloon, and every download has to stream through app servers. But databases are excellent at indexing, querying and transactionally updating small records such as owner, key, size, checksum and timestamps, which object storage cannot query. Storing metadata in the database (written after the bytes, with orphaned objects garbage-collected) and the bytes in object storage lets each do what it is good at.

## What has to happen behind a PUT
From the outside, Amazon S3 looks almost trivial: `PUT bucket/key` with some bytes, `GET bucket/key` to read them back, `LIST` to see what's there. Behind that small API is one of the largest storage systems ever built: hundreds of microservices, a fleet of millions of hard disks, and a design target of **eleven nines of durability** (99.999999999%, meaning that if you store 10 million objects you'd expect to lose one about every 10,000 years).

You don't need the internals of any one provider to design an object store in an interview, but you do need the shape, because every large object store (S3, Google Cloud Storage, Azure Blob Storage, Ceph, MinIO) has the same three concerns separated into different subsystems:
1. **Speaking the protocol**: HTTP, authentication, routing.
2. **Knowing where things are**: the mapping from bucket and key to the location of the bytes, plus versions and listing.
3. **Storing bytes durably**: disks, redundancy, repair.

## The parts of S3
At a high level, S3 is organised like this:

%%layers The main layers behind S3. Each is owned by separate teams and runs its own fleet behind an API contract.
Front-end fleet | REST API, authentication, request routing
*Namespace (index) service | bucket + key to version and data location
Storage fleet | millions of hard disks holding the bytes
ShardStore on each disk | per-disk storage engine, written in Rust
Background services | replication, repair, tiering, lifecycle, audits
%%

- The **front-end fleet** terminates HTTP and TLS, authenticates the request (checks the signature against IAM credentials and bucket policies), applies rate limits, and routes the request to the right internal services. Front-end servers are **stateless**, so there can be many of them behind load balancers.
- The **namespace service** (often called the index or metadata service) maps `(bucket, key)` to the object's current version, size, checksum and the location of its data. It also serves `LIST`, which is a range scan over keys in sorted order (lesson 07.15 explains why that needs range partitioning).
- The **storage fleet** is the huge population of hard disks that hold the bytes. On each disk, a storage engine called **ShardStore**, rewritten in Rust, manages how data is laid out. It was developed alongside lightweight formal verification (executable reference models and automated checks of crash consistency), because a bug at this layer could corrupt data on millions of disks.
- **Background services** do the work no request waits for: repairing lost redundancy after disk failures, moving objects between storage classes, enforcing lifecycle rules, scrubbing data to detect silent corruption, and replicating across regions.

Each of these is owned by its own teams and runs as a fleet behind an API contract, which is how an organisation of that size changes one part without breaking the others.

## A PUT, step by step
Here is the path of a single-part PUT, as large object stores are generally built:
1. **Front end**: the request arrives, TLS is terminated, the signature is checked, and the bucket's policy allows the write. If the client supplied a checksum header, the front end prepares to verify it (lesson 07.20).
2. **Placement**: the system chooses where the bytes will go. For durability, the object is **erasure-coded**: split into k data shards plus m parity shards (lesson 07.19), so any k of the k + m shards can rebuild it. The shards are placed on disks in different **failure domains**: different machines, racks and, for standard storage classes, different Availability Zones (separate data centres in one region).
3. **Write the bytes**: the shards are streamed to their storage nodes, which write them to disk. The checksum of the received data is computed and compared with the client's.
4. **Wait for durability**: once enough shards are durably stored to meet the durability target, the bytes are safe.
5. **Commit the metadata**: only now is a record written in the namespace service: key, version ID, size, checksum, and the locations of the shards.
6. **Acknowledge**: the front end returns `200 OK`. From this moment a GET or LIST can find the object.

%%seq A PUT writes the bytes first and makes them visible only when the metadata commits. Before that commit, nobody can find the new data.
Client > Front end : PUT key + checksum
Front end > Front end : auth, authorise
Front end > Storage nodes : write k + m shards
Storage nodes -> Front end : durable, CRC ok
Front end > Namespace : commit shard map
Namespace -> Front end : committed
Front end -> Client : 200 OK
%%

The ordering is the important idea, and it's the same rule as lesson 07.13: **bytes first, metadata last**. Until the metadata commit, the new bytes are invisible, so a crash halfway leaves unreferenced shards (cleaned up later), never a key pointing to missing data. And because an overwrite writes new shards and then swaps the metadata, a reader sees either the complete old object or the complete new one.

### Multipart uploads
Large objects are uploaded in **parts**: the client starts a multipart upload, uploads parts (5 MB to 5 GB each, up to 10,000 parts) in parallel and in any order, retrying only failed ones, then sends a "complete" request listing the parts. Each part is stored like a small object; "complete" is a single metadata commit that stitches them into one object. This is how clients reach gigabytes per second for one upload, and why a 1 TB upload can survive a flaky network.

### A GET
A GET is the reverse: front end authenticates, the namespace returns the current version's shard locations, the front end reads k shards (it can read from whichever disks respond fastest, and with spare shards it doesn't have to wait for a slow one), reassembles the bytes, and streams them back with the stored checksum available for the client to verify.

## Heat: the real scaling problem
The central operational idea in a fleet of hard disks is **heat**: the request load on a given disk at a given time. Lesson 07.01 explained the constraint: a hard drive does only about 120 random I/Os per second, and that number hasn't improved for decades, while capacity keeps rising (drives of 26 TB at the time this design was discussed, with 200 TB forecast). So the I/O available **per terabyte** keeps falling.

%%bars Random I/Os per second available per terabyte as drives grow, assuming about 120 IOPS per drive regardless of size.
4 TB drive | 30 | 30 IOPS per TB
12 TB drive | 10 | 10 IOPS per TB
*26 TB drive | 4.6 | about 4.6 IOPS per TB
200 TB drive | 0.6 | about 0.6 IOPS per TB
%%

If one customer's data sat on a few disks, a burst of reads from that customer would overwhelm those disks while thousands of others sat idle. S3 attacks this in three ways:
- **Spread every customer's objects over many disks.** Placement deliberately scatters shards so that one customer's working set lives on a very large number of drives. A single customer's burst can then be served by over a million disks in parallel.
- **Aggregate many workloads.** Individual workloads are extremely bursty, but millions of uncorrelated bursty workloads, added together, produce a smooth total. The fleet is sized for the aggregate, not for each customer's peak.
- **Use redundancy for reads.** With erasure coding, there are more shards than needed to read an object. If one disk is hot, the read can use the others and skip it.

! Example: why spreading matters
! A customer stores 50 TB of images and occasionally triggers a batch job that reads 2 million of them in 10 minutes: about 3,300 reads a second. If those 50 TB sat on two 26 TB drives, the drives could deliver about 240 random reads a second: the job would take over two hours and other customers on those drives would starve.
! Spread across shards on, say, 100,000 drives, the same burst is about 0.03 extra reads a second per drive, invisible in the noise. And because each object can be rebuilt from any k of its k + m shards, the few reads that land on a temporarily busy disk can simply use a different shard.

## Strong consistency and the witness
For its first 14 years, S3 was **eventually consistent** for some operations: after an overwrite or delete, a GET might briefly return the old data, and a LIST might not yet show a new key. In December 2020 S3 became **strongly consistent** for all operations, including LIST: once a PUT returns 200, every subsequent read sees it.

The old behaviour came from the **metadata cache**. To keep reads fast and available, metadata lookups are served from a caching layer in front of the persistent metadata store. A write could update the store through one path while a read was answered by a cache node still holding the previous entry.

The fix added a component called a **witness**. Every change to an object's metadata notifies the witness, which keeps a small amount of in-memory state about recent writes. Before a cache serves a metadata entry, it checks with the witness: "is my entry current?" If the witness says it's stale, the cache reads through to the persistent tier. The witness acts as a **read barrier**: a cheap, fast check that makes the cache safe without making every read go to the persistent store. Because its state is small and in memory, it is fast and easy to replace if a node fails.

%%seq The witness acts as a read barrier: the cache serves a value only if the witness confirms it is current; otherwise it reads through to the persistent metadata store.
Writer > Store : PUT k (v2)
Store > Witness : k changed
Reader > Cache : GET k
Cache > Witness : is v1 current?
Witness -> Cache : no, stale
Cache > Store : read k
Store -> Cache : v2
Cache -> Reader : v2
%%

The team verified the new protocol with formal proofs and model checking, which took more effort than writing the code, because a subtle ordering bug would break the guarantee silently. The change shipped at no extra cost to customers and with no announced performance or availability trade-off.

! Example: upload then list
! Before December 2020, a data pipeline could PUT `reports/2020-11.csv` and immediately LIST `reports/` without seeing it, because LIST read a stale cache entry. Pipelines worked around it by sleeping, retrying, or writing a separate manifest file listing what they'd uploaded (or by using a consistency layer such as a DynamoDB table). With the witness, the LIST is guaranteed to include the new key, and those workarounds became unnecessary.

## Pitfalls when using an object store like S3
- **Treating it like a filesystem.** There are no directories, only keys with slashes; renaming a "folder" means copying and deleting every object under it.
- **Hot prefixes.** Request rates scale per key range: S3 supports at least 3,500 writes and 5,500 reads per second per partitioned prefix, and splits busy ranges automatically, but a sudden burst on one narrow prefix can be throttled (HTTP 503 Slow Down) until it does. Lesson 07.16 explains the splitting.
- **Small objects.** Each request has fixed overhead and cost; billions of 1 KB objects are expensive. Pack them (lesson 07.05).
- **Ignoring checksums.** Supply one on upload and verify on download (lesson 07.20).

## In an interview
If asked to design an object store, separate the three concerns and name the commit point: "Stateless front ends for HTTP and auth; a partitioned metadata service mapping bucket and key to version and shard locations, which also serves LIST; a storage layer that erasure-codes objects across failure domains. A PUT writes the shards first and commits metadata last; the object exists from that commit." Then mention heat (spread data so bursts hit many disks) and repair (background services restore lost shards).

> Takeaway: S3 separates the HTTP front end, the namespace metadata and a disk fleet running a per-disk engine, with background services for repair and tiering. A PUT writes erasure-coded shards across failure domains first and commits metadata last, which is the moment the object exists; spreading data over huge numbers of disks tames heat, and the witness keeps cached metadata from serving stale answers.

?? The front end is a stateless fleet that terminates HTTP and TLS, authenticates and authorises the request, and routes it, choosing where the new data will go. The storage nodes (a fleet of hard disks, each managed by the ShardStore engine) durably store the object's erasure-coded shards, spread across disks, racks and Availability Zones as separate failure domains, and verify checksums. The metadata (namespace) service records the bucket and key, version, size, checksum and the locations of the shards, and also serves LIST; the metadata commit happens only after the shards are durable, and once it commits the object becomes visible to reads and the client gets 200 OK.

## The question: which server owns this key?
Once data outgrows one machine, you split it into **partitions** (also called shards) and spread them across servers. Every request then starts with the same question: given this key, which server owns it? The answer has to be quick to compute, has to spread load evenly, and has to cope when servers are added, removed or fail.

There are two main families of answers:
- **Range partitioning**: divide the key space into contiguous **sorted ranges**, and give each range to one server. Keys `a` to `f` on server 1, `f` to `m` on server 2, and so on.
- **Hash partitioning**, usually via **consistent hashing**: hash each key to a number and assign numbers to servers. Neighbouring keys land on unrelated servers.

The choice decides what queries are cheap. This lesson's focus is the one that matters most for object storage: **listing objects by prefix**, such as "show me everything under `invoices/2024/`".

%%compare The same five keys under each scheme. Ranges keep a prefix together; hashing scatters it across servers.
[Range partitioning]
invoices/01, /02, /03, /04, /05 -(sorted, one range)-> Server A
+ One server answers a prefix listing
+ Results already in order
- New keys at the end all hit one range
[Consistent hashing]
invoices/01 -(hash)-> Server C, /02 -> A, /03 -> D
+ Load spread evenly by the hash
+ Few keys move when servers change
- A prefix listing must ask every server
%%

## Range partitioning
In range partitioning, keys are kept in **sorted order** and the sorted sequence is cut into ranges. A small **partition map** records each range's boundaries and its owner. To route a request, look up which range contains the key: a binary search over a few thousand boundaries, fast enough to do on every request from a cached copy of the map.

%%tree A range-partitioned object table. Each range is a contiguous slice of the sorted keys; listing a prefix touches only the ranges that overlap it.
Object table, sorted by account, container, object
  Range 1: "" to acme/b | Server 4
  Range 2: acme/b to acme/invoices/2024/06 | Server 9
  *Range 3: acme/invoices/2024/06 to acme/m | Server 2
  Range 4: acme/m to globex/ | Server 7
  Range 5: globex/ to end | Server 4
%%

What range partitioning makes easy:
- **Prefix listing and range scans.** All keys starting with `invoices/2024/` sit next to each other in sorted order, so they live in one range or a few adjacent ones. A listing reads them in order, from one or two servers, and can stop after the first 1,000.
- **Locality for a tenant.** If the key starts with the account name, one customer's data is stored together in a small set of ranges. That makes it easy to measure, throttle and isolate a noisy customer.
- **Flexible rebalancing.** A range that grows too big or too busy can be **split** at any key into two, and cold neighbours can be **merged**. The partition map changes; the keys themselves don't. Lesson 07.16 looks at a partition manager doing this.

What it makes hard:
- **Sequential keys create hot spots.** If keys increase steadily (timestamps, auto-increment IDs, `log-2024-06-01-00001`), every new write lands in the **last range**, on one server, while the others sit idle. Splitting doesn't help much: the new "last range" is still the only one getting writes.
- **Skew needs active management.** Ranges are uneven by nature, so something must watch load and split or move ranges continuously.

Range partitioning is used by Bigtable and HBase (tablets and regions), Spanner and CockroachDB (splits and ranges), MongoDB's ranged shard keys, and the metadata layers of object stores including S3 and Windows Azure Storage.

## Hash partitioning and consistent hashing
### The naive version: hash mod N
The simplest hash scheme is `server = hash(key) % N`. It spreads keys evenly. Its fatal flaw appears when N changes. Going from 4 servers to 5 changes the result of `% N` for about **80%** of keys (in general, a fraction N/(N+1) of them), so nearly all data must move.

### The ring
**Consistent hashing** fixes that. Imagine the hash space (say 0 to 2^32 − 1) bent into a circle. Each server is hashed to one or more points on the circle. Each key is hashed to a point too, and belongs to the **first server clockwise** from it.
- **Adding a server** only takes over the keys on the arc between it and its predecessor; nothing else moves. Going from 4 to 5 servers moves about 1/5, 20% of keys, all of them to the new server.
- **Removing a server** moves only its keys, to the next server clockwise.

%%bars Share of keys that must move when a fifth server joins four, by scheme.
hash(key) mod N | 80 | about 80%
*Consistent hashing ring | 20 | about 20%, the minimum
%%

### Virtual nodes
With one point per server, arcs are uneven, and a failed server's entire load lands on the single next server, doubling its traffic. The fix is **virtual nodes**: each physical server is placed at many points (for example 100 to 256 tokens). Then each server owns many small arcs scattered around the ring. When one fails, its load spreads over all the others; when one joins, it takes small slices from many. It also lets a bigger machine take more virtual nodes.

### Hash slots
A common variant fixes the number of partitions up front. **Redis Cluster** uses 16,384 **hash slots**: `slot = CRC16(key) mod 16384`, and each node owns a set of slots. Adding a node means moving some slots, not rehashing keys. Cassandra, DynamoDB, Riak and many caches and CDNs use hashing in one of these forms.

### The cost: order is gone
A good hash function deliberately destroys any relationship between neighbouring keys. `invoices/2024/0001` and `invoices/2024/0002` are adjacent in sorted order but land on unrelated servers. So a query like "list everything under `invoices/2024/`" has no idea where to look and must **scatter** to every server and **gather** the results, then merge-sort them to return the first page in order.

%%seq A prefix listing on a hash-partitioned store must ask every node and merge the results, even if most have nothing to return.
Client > Router : LIST invoices/2024/
Router > Node 1 : scan prefix
Router > Node 2 : scan prefix
Router > Node 3 : scan prefix
Node 1 -> Router : 340 keys
Node 2 -> Router : 410 keys
Node 3 -> Router : 395 keys
Router > Router : merge, keep 1,000
Router -> Client : page + token
%%

With 3 nodes that's tolerable. With 1,000 nodes, every listing costs 1,000 requests, the slowest node sets the latency, and each node must scan its own data for the prefix (which, without a sorted local index, means a full scan).

## Range partitioning in Windows Azure Storage
Windows Azure Storage (WAS) is a good example of choosing ranges deliberately. Blobs, tables and queues are all stored in huge **Object Tables** whose primary key is `(AccountName, PartitionName, ObjectName)`. Each table is split into **RangePartitions**: contiguous ranges of rows from a low key to a high key, each served by exactly one partition server.

The reasons given for ranges over hashing:
- **Performance isolation**: a customer's objects are stored together in a set of RangePartitions, so the system can see and throttle an abusive account.
- **Efficient enumeration**: listing a container is a scan of adjacent rows.

The stated downside is exactly the sequential-key problem. If customers use steadily increasing keys, all traffic for them hits the last range. Customers who need high write rates are expected to spread their keys themselves, for example by prefixing a hash or a bucket number to the PartitionName.

## Getting both: hash then range
Many systems combine the two, and this is often the best answer. Hash a **partition key** to choose the server, and keep data **sorted within** the partition by a second key. DynamoDB's partition key plus sort key and Cassandra's partition key plus clustering columns both work this way. "All orders for customer 42, sorted by date" is one partition, read in order from one server. What you can't do is a range scan **across** partition keys.

%%matrix How the schemes compare on the properties that usually decide the choice.
Property | Range partitioning | Consistent hashing | Hash, then sort within
Prefix listing across keys | yes | no, ask every node | no
Range scan within an entity | yes | no | yes
Even load for sequential keys | no, last range hot | yes | yes
Data moved when nodes change | partial, split or move ranges | yes, about 1/N | yes, about 1/N
Needs active load management | yes | partial | partial
%%

! Example: listing a folder
! A bucket has 50 million objects and a user lists `invoices/2024/`, which matches 120,000 keys; the API returns 1,000 per page. With range partitioning, the 120,000 matching keys sit in one or two adjacent ranges. The first page is a single sorted scan of 1,000 rows on one server, a few milliseconds; the next page resumes from the last key returned.
! With hashing across 200 nodes, the matching keys are spread about 600 per node. To return the first 1,000 in order, the router must ask all 200 nodes for their first 1,000 matches (any node might hold the smallest keys), merge 200 sorted streams, and discard most results. Every page repeats that work. Latency is set by the slowest of 200 nodes, and the listing generates 200 times as many requests.

! Example: a hot range from timestamped keys
! A logging service writes 30,000 objects a second with keys like `logs/2024-06-01T10:15:03.123-...`. Under range partitioning, every new key sorts after all existing ones, so all 30,000 writes a second hit the last range on one server, which can handle about 3,000. Splitting the hot range creates a new last range, which immediately becomes the hot one again.
! The fix is to break the ordering on purpose: prefix each key with a short hash, `logs/7f/2024-06-01T...`, giving 256 prefixes. Writes now spread across 256 ranges, about 120 a second each. The price: listing "all logs from 10:15" now needs 256 prefix scans. That is the range-versus-hash trade-off made explicit.

## In an interview
When you choose a partitioning scheme, say what query it serves. "Object metadata is range-partitioned by bucket and key so LIST by prefix is a sequential scan of one or a few ranges; we split hot ranges automatically, and we advise clients not to use purely sequential key prefixes." Or for a key-value cache: "consistent hashing with virtual nodes: no range queries needed, even load, and only about 1/N of keys move when we add a node." Mentioning the hybrid (hash partition key, sorted within) often impresses.

> Takeaway: range partitioning keeps keys sorted, so prefix listings and range scans read one or a few adjacent ranges in order and a tenant's data stays together, but sequential keys pile onto the last range and ranges must be split and moved actively. Hashing spreads load evenly and moves only about 1/N of keys when servers change, but destroys key order, so a prefix listing must ask every node and merge.

?? With range partitioning, keys are stored in sorted order and each server owns a contiguous key range, so all keys sharing a prefix are adjacent and live in one range or a few neighbouring ones; a listing locates the first range from the partition map and reads keys in order from one or two servers, stopping after a page. Hashing deliberately scatters neighbouring keys to unrelated servers, so the system cannot know where keys with a given prefix live: it must send the listing to every node, have each find its matches, and merge-sort the results to return the first page in order, which costs requests on every node and is as slow as the slowest node.

## Why ranges need a manager
Lesson 07.15 showed that range partitioning keeps keys sorted, which makes prefix listing cheap, but leaves load uneven: some ranges grow huge, some get hot, some go cold. Someone has to watch every range and keep the assignment of ranges to servers healthy. That someone is the **partition manager**, and the table it maintains (which range lives on which server) is the **partition map**.

This lesson uses Windows Azure Storage (WAS) as the worked example, because its design is described in unusual detail and because the same pattern appears in Bigtable (its master and tablets), HBase (HMaster and regions), CockroachDB and Spanner (ranges and splits), and inside S3's automatic prefix scaling.

## The setting: a storage stamp
WAS groups hardware into **storage stamps**. A stamp is a cluster of 10 to 20 racks with 18 disk-heavy storage nodes per rack: about 2 PB of raw capacity in the first generation and up to 30 PB in the next. Stamps are kept around **70% utilised**, never above 80%, so there is room to absorb a rack failure and to give compaction and garbage collection space to work. A separate **Location Service** assigns each customer account to a stamp and updates DNS so the account's hostname points at it.

Inside a stamp there are three layers:

%%layers The three layers inside one WAS storage stamp. The partition layer, the subject of this lesson, sits between stateless front ends and an append-only stream layer.
Front-end servers | stateless: authenticate, route using cached partition map
*Partition layer | objects, RangePartitions, transactions, consistency
Stream layer | append-only extents, replicated three ways (lesson 07.18)
%%

The front ends receive requests, authenticate them, look up which partition server owns the requested key in a cached copy of the partition map, and forward the request. The stream layer stores bytes. The partition layer turns bytes into objects (blobs, table rows, queue messages), orders transactions, and guarantees strong consistency for each object.

## The partition layer's parts
- **Partition Map Table**: records every RangePartition of every Object Table and which partition server is serving it. Front ends cache it to route requests; if they route to the wrong server (because the map changed), that server rejects the request and the front end refreshes.
- **Partition Manager (PM)**: splits Object Tables into RangePartitions, assigns each to **exactly one** partition server with no overlaps, and keeps load balanced. Several PM instances run for availability; the one holding the **leader lock** in the Lock Service is active, and the others wait to take over.
- **Partition Servers (PS)**: serve all requests for their assigned RangePartitions, about ten each on average. A PS may serve a partition only while it holds a **lease** for it from the Lock Service, which guarantees one owner at a time and therefore strong consistency: two servers can never both accept writes for the same key.
- **Lock Service**: a Paxos-based service (Paxos being a consensus algorithm that lets a group of machines agree despite failures) used for PM leader election and for PS leases.

### How a RangePartition is stored
Each RangePartition is stored **LSM-style** (lessons 07.09 to 07.11) on top of the stream layer, as a handful of streams:

%%tree One RangePartition's persistent state. Everything is an append-only stream in the shared stream layer, so any partition server can load it.
RangePartition B | keys from acme/m to globex/
  Metadata stream | the root: names the other streams, last checkpoint
  *Commit log stream | recent changes since last checkpoint
  Row data stream | checkpoints of rows plus their index
  Blob data stream | blob bytes, for blob tables only
  In memory on the PS
    Memory table | recent updates, like a memtable
    Index cache, row data cache
    Bloom filters | one per checkpoint
%%

Writes go to the commit log stream and an in-memory **memory table**; periodically the memory table is checkpointed into the row data stream, and checkpoints are merged in the background. Reads check the memory table, then checkpoints, using Bloom filters to skip checkpoints that can't hold the key. It is the LSM tree from earlier lessons, built on a distributed append-only log instead of local files.

The crucial property: **all of a partition's state lives in the shared stream layer, not on the partition server's local disk**. A PS is just the current owner and cache. That is what makes everything in the next section cheap.

## How the PM reacts to load
The PM continuously collects load statistics for every RangePartition: transactions per second, average pending transactions, throttling rate, CPU usage, network usage, request latency and data size. Every **15 seconds** it sorts partitions by these measures and acts on a few of the worst. It has three tools.

### 1. Load balance: move a range
If a partition server is overloaded but no single range on it is too hot to handle on its own, the PM moves one or more busy RangePartitions to a lightly loaded PS. The sequence:
1. The PM tells the old PS to unload the partition. The PS writes a checkpoint (flushing its memory table), so the commit log to replay is short, and stops serving.
2. The PM assigns the partition to the new PS and updates the Partition Map Table.
3. The new PS acquires the lease, reads the metadata stream, replays the short commit log, and starts serving.

No data is copied. The new owner reads the same streams the old one wrote. The pause is typically well under a second for the moving partition.

### 2. Split: one hot range becomes two
If a single RangePartition is too big or too busy for any one server, it must be **split**. The PM decides **that** a split should happen; the PS that owns the partition decides **where**, because it has the detailed traffic data:
- A **size split** picks a key that roughly halves the data.
- A **load split** uses **Adaptive Range Profiling**, which tracks the distribution of requests across the key range, to pick the key that splits the **traffic** roughly in half. That can be very different from the middle of the data, if a small slice of keys is hot.

Then, for partition B splitting into C and D:
1. The PS checkpoints B and briefly stops serving it.
2. It uses a stream-layer operation called **MultiModify** to create new streams for C and D that **point at the same extents, in the same order, as B's streams**. Extents are immutable (lesson 07.18), so sharing them is safe. Each new partition simply ignores keys outside its range until compaction rewrites its data.
3. It starts serving C and D, and tells the PM.
4. The PM updates the Partition Map Table and typically moves one of the halves to another PS.

%%seq A split is metadata work only: both halves keep pointing at the same immutable extents, so no blob data is copied.
PM > PS-7 : split B
PS-7 > PS-7 : pick key from traffic
PS-7 > PS-7 : checkpoint B, pause
PS-7 > Stream layer : MultiModify C, D
PS-7 -> PM : serving C and D
PM > Map table : C: PS-7, D: PS-12
PM > PS-7 : unload D
PM > PS-12 : load and serve D
%%

### 3. Merge: cold neighbours become one
Splits alone would leave the system with ever more, ever smaller partitions, each with its own memory and per-partition overhead. So the PM also **merges** adjacent cold RangePartitions: it moves them onto the same PS, which then combines them into one, again with a metadata operation over existing streams. The goal is to keep the total number of RangePartitions between low and high watermarks, typically around **ten times the number of partition servers** in the stamp.

%%state The life of a RangePartition under the partition manager. Most transitions change only metadata, because the data stays in the shared stream layer.
[*] -> *Served
Served -> Moving : server overloaded
Moving -> Served : loaded
Served -> Splitting : too hot or big
Splitting -> Served : split done
Served -> Merging : cold neighbours
Merging -> Served : merged
%%

## What happens when a partition server fails
If a PS crashes, its leases expire. The PM notices (through the Lock Service and heartbeats), and reassigns each of the dead server's RangePartitions to live partition servers, spreading them out. Each new owner reads the partition's metadata stream, replays the commit log since the last checkpoint and starts serving. Again no data is copied; recovery time is dominated by commit log replay, which checkpointing keeps short. The lease protects correctness: even if the "dead" server was only partitioned from the network and is still running, its lease has expired, so it must stop serving before anyone else starts.

! Example: a viral container
! One customer's container suddenly receives 20,000 requests a second, all inside RangePartition B on PS-7, which can handle about 8,000. Requests start queueing and some are throttled. On the next 15-second pass, the PM sees B at the top of its list by pending transactions and throttling rate.
! PS-7 profiles B's traffic and finds most requests fall in a narrow slice of keys. It picks a split key that divides the traffic, not the data, creating C and D with about 10,000 requests a second each. The PM moves D to idle PS-12. If C is still too hot, the next pass splits it again. Within seconds to a minute the load is spread over several servers, and not one byte of blob data was copied.

%%timeline The viral container, second by second, as the partition manager splits and moves the hot range.
0 s | Traffic on range B jumps to 20,000 per second, PS-7 saturated
12 s | *PM pass: B tops the list, split ordered
13 s | PS-7 checkpoints B, splits into C and D via shared extents
14 s | PM moves D to PS-12, map table updated
15 s | Front ends refresh map, D's traffic goes to PS-12
27 s | Next PM pass: C still hot, split again into C1 and C2
%%

! Example: why moving is cheap here and expensive elsewhere
! RangePartition B holds 400 GB of data. In a design where each server stores its partitions on local disk, moving B to another server means copying 400 GB over the network: at 1 GB/s that's nearly 7 minutes, during which the source is busier than ever. In WAS, B's 400 GB lives in the stream layer, so the move costs a checkpoint, a lease handover and a commit log replay of perhaps tens of megabytes: about a second. This separation of compute (partition servers) from storage (streams) is the same idea behind many modern cloud databases.

## Pitfalls
- **Splitting on data size only.** A range can be small and still hot; split on load too.
- **Splitting sequential hot spots.** If keys are increasing timestamps, the new last range is hot again immediately. Splitting buys time; the real fix is a key design that spreads writes.
- **No merging.** Endless splits leave millions of tiny partitions, bloating the map and the per-partition overhead.
- **Stale routing.** Front ends must cope with a moved partition by retrying and refreshing the map, not by failing the request.
- **Two owners.** Without leases from a consensus service, a slow or partitioned server can keep accepting writes after its partition moved, breaking consistency.

## In an interview
If your design range-partitions anything (object metadata, a sorted key-value store), mention the manager: "A partition manager tracks per-range load and size, splits hot ranges at a key chosen from the traffic, moves ranges off overloaded servers and merges cold ones; servers hold leases so each range has exactly one owner, and front ends cache the partition map." If storage is separated from compute, add that splits and moves are metadata-only, which is why they can react in seconds.

> Takeaway: a partition manager owns the key-range-to-server map and keeps it balanced by moving, splitting and merging ranges, using leases so each range has exactly one owner. Keeping the data in a shared append-only stream layer makes all three operations cheap, metadata-only changes that take seconds instead of hours of copying.

?? It first decides whether moving is enough: if the range's server is overloaded but the range itself could be handled by one server, it moves the range to a lightly loaded partition server (checkpoint, release lease, new owner loads the streams and replays a short commit log). If the range itself is too hot or too big, it orders a split: the owning server profiles the traffic, picks a split key that divides the load (not just the data), checkpoints, and creates two new partitions whose streams point at the same immutable extents, so no data is copied; the manager then updates the partition map table, moves one half to another server, and front ends route to the new owners. It repeats on later passes if a half is still hot, and merges cold neighbouring ranges to keep the partition count under control.

## Bytes and metadata are different workloads
Every object in an object store has two parts:
- The **bytes**: the photo, the video, the backup. Large (kilobytes to terabytes), immutable once written, read sequentially or by byte range, and the overwhelming majority of total storage.
- The **metadata**: the bucket and key, version ID, size, content type, checksum, owner, access control, creation time, storage class, user-defined tags, and, crucially, **where the bytes live** (which disks or extents hold its shards). Tiny (a few hundred bytes to a few kilobytes), changed often (tags, ACLs, versions, lifecycle state), and accessed by key lookup and sorted listing.

These two workloads want opposite things from hardware and from data structures. Putting them in one structure makes each worse. Every large object store therefore keeps object metadata in a separate, indexed, partitioned **metadata store**, and the bytes in a capacity-optimised **data store**. This lesson explains why, and what the metadata store looks like.

%%matrix The two halves of an object store have almost nothing in common as workloads.
Property | Object bytes | Object metadata
Typical size | KB to TB | 200 bytes to a few KB
Share of total storage | about 99.99% | tiny
Mutable after write | no | yes, tags, ACLs, versions
Access pattern | sequential, ranges | point lookup, sorted scan
Needs sorted index | no | yes, for LIST
Best hardware | dense hard disks | SSD or RAM
%%

## Evidence from real systems
### Haystack: metadata was the bottleneck
Facebook's photo store (lesson 07.13) is the clearest demonstration. On NFS filers, every photo was a file, and the filesystem mixed metadata (directory entries, inodes) with data on the same disks. Reading one photo took **3 or more disk operations** just to resolve its location before reading any bytes, and the metadata for billions of files couldn't be cached in RAM.

Haystack separated them. The metadata needed to locate a photo was cut to about **10 bytes** and kept in memory on each storage machine, while the bytes sat in 100 GB volume files. One disk operation per read. Separately, the **Directory** held the mapping from logical volumes to machines, a metadata store that never touched photo bytes.

### S3 and Windows Azure Storage: separate services
S3 runs a **namespace service** (bucket and key to version and data location) as a separate system from its **storage fleet**, each built and operated by different teams (lesson 07.14). Windows Azure Storage does the same: its **partition layer** holds objects and their index, partitioned by key range, on top of a **stream layer** that only knows about bytes in extents (lessons 07.16 and 07.18).

%%flow A GET goes through metadata first to find the bytes; a LIST is answered entirely from metadata and never touches the bytes.
[GET] Client -(bucket + key)-> *Metadata store -(shard locations)-> Data store -(range reads)-> Client
[LIST] Client -(prefix, page token)-> *Metadata store -(sorted keys, sizes)-> Client
%%

## What the metadata store holds
A simplified schema for an object store with versioning:

| Table | Key | Main fields |
| buckets | bucket name | owner, region, versioning on/off, policies, created |
| objects | bucket, object key, version (newest first) | size, checksum, content type, ACL, tags, storage class, is-delete-marker, data locations |
| uploads | bucket, key, upload ID | parts uploaded so far, for multipart uploads in progress |

The primary key of the objects table is the important design decision: `(bucket, key, version descending)`. Sorting by bucket then key means all of a bucket's objects are adjacent and in key order, so listing a prefix is a range scan. Sorting versions newest first means "get the current version" is "read the first row for this key".

### Listing and fake folders
Object stores have no real directories. A "folder" view comes from LIST with a **delimiter**. `LIST prefix=photos/2024/ delimiter=/` asks for keys starting with `photos/2024/`, but groups any key that has another `/` after the prefix into a **common prefix** (`photos/2024/06/`) instead of listing each one. The metadata store implements this by scanning in key order and, on finding a common prefix, **seeking past** all keys that share it. That's only efficient because keys are sorted; it's one reason metadata is range-partitioned (lesson 07.15).

%%tree How a flat, sorted key space looks as folders when listed with delimiter "/". The folders exist only as common prefixes of keys.
bucket: acme-media
  photos/2024/05/ | common prefix, 12,000 keys under it
  photos/2024/06/ | common prefix, 9,500 keys
  *photos/2024/cover.jpg | an actual object
  photos/2024/index.json | an actual object
%%

### Versions and delete markers
With versioning on, an overwrite adds a new version row instead of replacing the old one. A DELETE without a version ID adds a **delete marker**: a version row that says "the current state is deleted". GET without a version returns 404 because the newest version is a delete marker, but older versions remain and can be restored. Lifecycle rules later expire non-current versions. All of this is metadata-only: no bytes move until garbage collection deletes data no version references.

## Why the separation pays off
- **Each half scales on its own terms.** Metadata needs fast random lookups and range scans, so it lives in a partitioned, indexed store on SSD or in memory, sized by object **count**. Bytes need cheap capacity, so they live on dense hard disks with erasure coding, sized by **volume**. A workload of billions of tiny objects stresses the first; a workload of a few huge video files stresses the second.
- **Listing and querying are cheap.** LIST, HEAD (metadata only), checking existence, and filtering by tags or dates never read the data store at all.
- **Atomic visibility.** An object becomes visible at the instant its metadata row commits. The bytes can be written first, at leisure, across many disks, because nobody can find them until the commit. Crashes before the commit leave unreferenced bytes, never half-visible objects.
- **Moving data is invisible to users.** Repair after a disk failure, moving to a colder storage class, re-erasure-coding or compaction can relocate bytes and then update the location pointer in the metadata. The user's key never changes.
- **Garbage collection becomes a join.** Bytes not referenced by any metadata row are garbage, found by comparing the data store's inventory against the metadata.

! Example: overwrite with versioning
! A user uploads a new `logo.png` to a versioned bucket. The data store writes the new bytes to fresh disks: they're invisible. Then the metadata store, in one transaction on the row range for `(acme, logo.png)`, adds version 7 pointing at them, which becomes the newest. A GET that started a moment earlier already read version 6's locations and streams version 6 to the end, because its bytes are untouched. A GET that starts after the commit gets version 7. No reader ever sees a mix.

%%seq Overwriting an object in a versioned bucket. The bytes are written first and stay invisible until the metadata commit makes version 7 current.
Client > Front end : PUT logo.png
Front end > Data store : write new shards
Data store -> Front end : durable at L7
Front end > Metadata store : add v7 -> L7
Metadata store -> Front end : committed
Front end -> Client : 200 OK, v7
Reader > Metadata store : GET logo.png
Metadata store -> Reader : v7 -> L7
%%

## Sizing the metadata store
The metadata is small per object but there are a lot of objects, so it needs its own capacity plan.

! Example: how big is the metadata?
! A region stores 100 billion objects. Each metadata row (key averaging 80 bytes, version ID, size, checksum, timestamps, ACL reference, tags, and a list of shard locations for 14 erasure-coded shards) is about 600 bytes. Total: 100 billion × 600 bytes = **60 TB of metadata**, before replication.
! That's far too much for one machine but very manageable as a partitioned store: at 1 TB of SSD per partition server, 60 to 200 servers with replication, each serving a range of buckets and keys. Meanwhile the bytes, at an average object size of 1 MB, total 100 PB. The metadata is 0.06% of the data by size but drives the request rate: every GET, PUT, HEAD and LIST hits it, often hundreds of thousands to millions of times a second across the region.

%%bars Storage for 100 billion objects of 1 MB average: the metadata is tiny by volume but is touched by every request.
Object bytes | 100000 | about 100 PB
*Object metadata | 60 | about 60 TB
%%

What metadata stores are built on varies: range-partitioned key-value stores with consensus (like WAS's partition layer), sharded relational databases keyed by bucket, or transactional key-value stores such as FoundationDB, which several large systems use for metadata. Ceph's object gateway keeps a per-bucket index in its object store, sharded because a single huge bucket index becomes a hot spot. Some simpler self-hosted object stores, such as MinIO, skip a central metadata database and store each object's metadata in a small file beside its data; that's simpler to operate but makes large prefix listings slower, since they have to walk many per-object files instead of scanning a sorted index.

## Pitfalls
- **Hot buckets.** Billions of objects in one bucket under one prefix concentrate metadata load on a few ranges; the store must split ranges by load (lesson 07.16).
- **Sequential keys.** Timestamp-first keys send every new PUT to the last range of the bucket.
- **Losing metadata loses data.** Bytes with no metadata row are unreachable garbage. The metadata store needs at least the durability of the data store: synchronous replication, consensus, backups.
- **Unbounded listing.** LIST must be paginated with continuation tokens (the last key returned), so one request never scans millions of rows.
- **Forgetting GC.** Without a sweep, failed uploads and expired versions leave bytes on disk forever.

## In an interview
When you design file or media storage, draw two boxes: "a metadata service, range-partitioned by bucket and key, holding size, checksum, version and shard locations, which serves GET lookups, HEAD and LIST; and a data service that stores immutable erasure-coded shards on dense disks." Say the metadata commit is the visibility point, size the metadata by object count, and mention garbage collection of unreferenced bytes. That shows you understand why the split exists, not just that it does.

> Takeaway: keep small, mutable, queryable metadata in an indexed, range-partitioned store sized by object count, and large immutable bytes in a capacity store sized by volume. Committing the metadata row is what makes an object exist, which lets bytes be written, moved and repaired behind a stable key.

?? Metadata is small, changes often (versions, tags, ACLs) and must be looked up by key and listed in sorted order, while bytes are huge, immutable and need cheap capacity, so each wants different hardware (SSD or RAM versus dense disks) and different structures (a sorted, partitioned index versus erasure-coded blobs). Separating them lets each scale on its own terms (metadata by object count, bytes by volume), lets LIST, HEAD and existence checks run without touching data, makes the metadata commit the single atomic moment an object becomes visible (bytes can be written first, safely invisible), lets repair and tiering move bytes by just updating a pointer, and makes garbage collection a matter of finding bytes no metadata row references. Haystack showed the cost of mixing them: three or more disk operations per photo on NFS versus one once metadata moved into memory.

## Why put a log at the very bottom?
Lessons 07.16 and 07.17 described the partition layer of Windows Azure Storage (WAS): the part that knows about objects, keys, ranges and transactions. Underneath it sits a layer that knows nothing about any of that. It stores **streams of bytes that can only be appended to**. Every blob, table row and queue message in the system, and every commit log and checkpoint of the partition layer, ends up as appends to these streams.

This is a deliberate and widespread design. Google's GFS and its successor Colossus, HDFS, Kafka's partitions, and the storage layer under Amazon Aurora all make the lowest layer append-only. This lesson walks through how the WAS stream layer works and then answers the real question: why is append-only the right contract for the bottom of a storage system?

## The building blocks
%%tree The stream layer's data model. A stream is a list of extents; an extent is a sequence of blocks; only the last extent of a stream accepts appends.
Stream //acct/partition-B/commitlog | named, ordered list of extent pointers
  Extent 17 | sealed, immutable, about 1 GB
    Block | up to 4 MB, own checksum
    Block
  Extent 18 | sealed, immutable
  *Extent 19 | unsealed: appends go here
    Block
    Block | newest append
%%

- A **block** is the unit of reading and writing: up to 4 MB, appended atomically, with its own **checksum** that is verified on every read. Clients read whole blocks, so every byte read is checked.
- An **extent** is a sequence of appended blocks and is the **unit of replication**. Each extent is stored as a file on several extent nodes. The partition layer targets extents of about **1 GB**.
- A **stream** is a named, ordered list of pointers to extents. It looks to its user like one big append-only file, readable at any offset. Crucially, the **same extent can appear in more than one stream**, which is what lets the partition layer split a RangePartition by creating two streams that point at the same extents (lesson 07.16).
- Only the **last extent** of a stream can be appended to. When it reaches its target size, it is **sealed** at a block boundary and becomes **immutable forever**; a new extent is added to the stream for further appends.

Two kinds of server run the layer:
- The **Stream Manager (SM)** is a small Paxos cluster (Paxos being a consensus algorithm that keeps a group of replicas in agreement despite failures). It tracks the stream namespace, which extents make up each stream, and where every extent's replicas live. It is **off the critical path** of appends: clients ask it where to write only when a new extent starts. In the background it re-replicates extents that lost a replica, garbage-collects extents no stream references, and schedules erasure coding of sealed extents. Its entire state fits in about **32 GB of memory** for up to 50 million extents and 100,000 streams per stamp.
- **Extent Nodes (ENs)** store extent replicas as files on their local disks: the blocks, their checksums, and an index from offset to block. An EN knows nothing about streams, only about the extents it holds.

## How an append works
When a new extent is created, the SM assigns it **three replicas** on extent nodes in different **fault domains** (racks with independent power and network) and **upgrade domains** (groups of servers that are rebooted together during software rollouts), so no single rack failure or upgrade takes out two copies. One replica is the **primary**; the other two are **secondaries**. This assignment doesn't change while the extent is unsealed, so writers can talk to the replicas directly, without consulting the SM per append.

The append path:
1. The client (a partition server) sends the block to the extent's **primary** EN.
2. The primary chooses the **offset** at which the block goes, which orders concurrent appends from different clients.
3. It forwards the block and offset to both secondaries.
4. Each replica writes the append durably.
5. The primary acknowledges the client **only after all three replicas** have the append on disk.

%%seq An append from a partition server (PS) is acknowledged only when all three replicas have it durably on disk, at the offset chosen by the primary.
PS > Primary : append block
Primary > Primary : pick offset X, journal
Primary > Secondary 1 : append at X
Primary > Secondary 2 : append at X
Secondary 1 -> Primary : durable at X
Secondary 2 -> Primary : durable at X
Primary -> PS : success, offset X
%%

Because each EN has many extents on many disks, appends from many streams interleave on the same disk, which would turn sequential writes into random ones. WAS solves that with a dedicated **journal drive** on each EN: every append is first written sequentially to the journal (and acknowledged as soon as it's there), then lazily copied to the extent's data disk. This cut average append latency from about **30 ms to 6 ms**. It is the same trick as an LSM's write-ahead log: make the durable write sequential, organise the data later.

## Sealing: how replicas agree after a failure
What if a replica fails or is unreachable during an append? The writer doesn't try to patch things up in place. It asks the SM to **seal** the extent:
1. The SM asks the reachable replicas for their current **commit length**: how many bytes they have durably.
2. It seals the extent at the **smallest** commit length among them. Any append beyond that point was never acknowledged to a client (acknowledgement requires all three), so dropping it loses nothing that was promised.
3. If a replica that was unreachable comes back later, the SM forces it to synchronise to **exactly** the sealed length, truncating or copying as needed.

After sealing, every replica of the extent is **bitwise identical**. The writer simply continues in a new extent with a fresh set of replicas, and the SM re-replicates the sealed extent to a healthy node in the background.

%%timeline A replica fails during appends. Sealing at the smallest commit length makes every copy identical without any complex repair protocol.
t0 | Appends to extent 9 on EN-A (primary), EN-B, EN-C
t1 | EN-C stops responding mid-append
t1 + 50 ms | Writer asks SM to seal extent 9
t1 + 80 ms | *SM reads lengths: A = 812 MB, B = 812 MB, seals at 812 MB
t1 + 90 ms | Writer opens extent 10 on EN-A, EN-D, EN-E and carries on
Later | EN-C returns, is synced to exactly 812 MB; SM adds a third copy elsewhere
%%

The partition layer is designed to cope with this simply: it only ever reads data at offsets for which it received an acknowledgement, so it can never read a "phantom" append that only some replicas had.

%%state The life of an extent. Data is only ever appended while unsealed; after sealing it never changes, which enables everything that follows.
[*] -> *Unsealed
Unsealed -> Sealed : reached 1 GB, or a replica failed
Sealed -> ErasureCoded : background, to cut storage cost
Sealed -> Unreferenced : no stream points at it
ErasureCoded -> Unreferenced : no stream points at it
Unreferenced -> GarbageCollected : SM deletes replicas
%%

## Reads
Reads can go to any replica of a sealed extent, since they're all identical. WAS sends reads with a **deadline**: if an extent node can't serve the read in time (because its disk is busy), it says so immediately, and the client tries another replica rather than waiting. For erasure-coded extents (lesson 07.19), the client can read more fragments than it needs and use the first ones to arrive. These tricks cut tail latency without any coordination.

## Why make the lowest layer append-only?
- **Replication becomes simple.** For a mutable block, replicas can disagree about content at any offset after a failure, and reconciling that needs a heavy protocol. For an append-only extent, there is exactly **one** thing replicas can disagree about: its **length**. Sealing at an agreed length settles it completely. No conflict resolution, no merging, no version vectors.
- **Immutability for everything above.** Sealed extents never change, so they can be erasure-coded, geo-replicated, cached, scrubbed, and **shared between streams** safely and without locks. That sharing is what makes partition splits and merges metadata-only operations that take seconds (lesson 07.16).
- **Sequential I/O.** Appends are disk-friendly, especially with the journal drive. Higher layers keep their own LSM-style indexes to find rows, so they never need in-place updates.
- **Simple crash recovery for the layer above.** A partition server that crashes leaves behind streams whose contents are exactly the acknowledged appends. Its replacement reads the metadata stream, replays the commit log from the last checkpoint and serves.
- **Clean separation of concerns.** The stream layer worries only about durability and placement; the partition layer worries only about objects and consistency. Each can be reasoned about and tested on its own.

%%compare Replicating mutable blocks versus append-only extents. With appends, the only possible disagreement after a failure is how long the extent is.
[Mutable blocks]
Write offset X -(replicate)-> 3 copies, any byte can change
- Replicas can differ anywhere after a crash
- Repair needs per-block versions or quorum reads
- Data can't be safely shared or cached
[*Append-only extents]
Append at end -(replicate)-> 3 copies, then seal
+ Only the length can differ
+ Seal at the smallest acked length
+ Sealed data shared, cached, erasure-coded
%%

## What it costs
The usual bill for logs. Deleted and overwritten data stays in sealed extents until the partition layer compacts: it reads the live data out of old extents, writes it to new ones, updates its streams, and the SM then garbage-collects extents that no stream references. That costs I/O and requires spare capacity, one reason stamps are kept below 80% full.

! Example: a partition server crash
! PS-3 dies while appending to extent 9 of RangePartition B's commit log stream. The SM asks the extent nodes holding extent 9 for their lengths: 812.0 MB, 812.0 MB and 811.9 MB (the last never received the final append). It seals at 811.9 MB. The final append was never acknowledged to anyone, because acknowledgement needed all three replicas, so nothing promised is lost.
! The PM assigns B to PS-8. PS-8 reads B's metadata stream to find the last checkpoint and the commit log position after it, replays about 30 MB of commit log into its memory table, and starts serving B, typically within a couple of seconds. No data was copied and no replica is in an ambiguous state.

! Example: the cost of garbage
! A RangePartition's blob stream holds 2 TB across 2,000 sealed 1 GB extents. Over a month, customers delete or overwrite 30% of the blobs. Those bytes stay in their extents, so 600 GB is dead. The partition layer's garbage collection reads the 1.4 TB of live data from extents that are mostly dead, rewrites it to new extents, and updates its index; the SM then deletes about 600 old extents. That's 1.4 TB read and 1.4 TB written (more with replication) to reclaim 600 GB, the log-structured trade-off from lesson 07.07, now at datacentre scale.

## In an interview
If you're designing a storage system from the bottom up, propose an append-only replicated log as the lowest layer: "Data is appended to extents replicated three ways across fault domains, acknowledged when all replicas have it; on any failure, the extent is sealed at the smallest committed length and writing continues in a new extent; sealed extents are immutable, so we can erasure-code, share and garbage-collect them freely." Then name the cost: compaction and garbage collection of dead data.

> Takeaway: an append-only lowest layer means replicas can only disagree about length, so sealing at the shortest acknowledged length keeps every copy identical without a complex repair protocol. Sealed data is immutable, so everything above it can move, share, erasure-code and recover it cheaply; the price is garbage collection of dead data.

?? With append-only extents, the only thing replicas can disagree about after a failure is how long the extent is, so the system can reconcile them simply: seal the extent at the smallest committed length (anything beyond it was never acknowledged, since acknowledgement requires all replicas) and force every replica to exactly that length, making them bitwise identical, then continue appending in a new extent. Sealed data never changes, so it can be erasure-coded, geo-replicated, cached, scrubbed and shared between streams without locks, which is what makes partition splits and merges metadata-only and recovery a matter of replaying logs. Appends are also sequential and disk-friendly. The cost is that deleted and overwritten data remains until higher layers compact live data and the stream manager garbage-collects unreferenced extents.

## The problem: disks die, constantly
**Durability** is the probability that data you stored is still there, intact, when you come back for it. It's different from **availability**, which is whether you can reach the data right now. An outage hurts availability; losing the bytes hurts durability, and is usually far worse.

At scale, hardware failure is not an event, it's a rate. Hard drives in large fleets fail at an **annual failure rate** (AFR) of roughly 1 to 2%. With 100,000 drives that's 1,000 to 2,000 dead drives a year, three to six every day. Add whole servers, power supplies, racks, network switches, and occasionally an entire data centre, and a storage system has to treat losing a disk as routine.

The only defence is **redundancy**: store more than one copy's worth of information, spread so that no single failure removes too much of it, and **repair** quickly after each failure so redundancy is restored before the next one. There are two ways to add redundancy: **replication** and **erasure coding**. Large systems use both.

## Replication
**Replication** keeps N full copies of the data on different disks, ideally in different **failure domains** (different servers, racks, power feeds, Availability Zones). Three copies (3x) is the classic choice:
- **Tolerates 2 failures**: the data survives as long as any one copy remains.
- **Storage overhead 3x**: 1 TB of data needs 3 TB of disk, which is 200% extra.
- **Reads are cheap**: read any single copy, so the busiest copy can be avoided.
- **Writes are simple**: send the same bytes to three places.
- **Repair is cheap**: when a disk dies, copy each lost piece from one surviving replica. Lose 1 TB, read 1 TB.

## Erasure coding
**Erasure coding** splits data into **k data shards** and computes **m parity shards** from them, for k + m shards in total, stored on k + m different disks. The code is designed so that **any k of the k + m shards** are enough to rebuild the original. The usual family is **Reed-Solomon** codes.

### The simplest case: one XOR parity
Take two data shards, A and B, and compute parity P = A XOR B (bitwise exclusive-or). Store A, B and P on three disks. Lose A? Compute A = P XOR B. Lose B? B = P XOR A. Any 2 of the 3 rebuild the data, at 1.5x storage, tolerating 1 failure. This is RAID 5.

Reed-Solomon generalises it to any k and m. The maths is linear algebra over bytes (a finite field with 256 elements): arrange the data as a matrix, multiply by a coding matrix to produce the parity rows. To recover, take the rows of the coding matrix for the shards that survive, invert that square matrix, and multiply it by the surviving shards. The data comes back exactly.

%%compare The same 1 TB stored with 3x replication and with 10 + 4 erasure coding. Erasure coding stores less and tolerates more failures.
[3x replication]
1 TB -(copy x3)-> 3 disks, full copies
+ Survives any 2 failures
+ Read and repair from one copy
- 3 TB stored, 200% overhead
[*10 + 4 erasure coding]
1 TB -(split 10, add 4 parity)-> 14 disks
+ Survives any 4 failures
+ 1.4 TB stored, 40% overhead
- Repair reads 10 shards per lost shard
%%

### Real-world parameters
- **Backblaze** splits each file into **17 data shards plus 3 parity shards**, 20 pieces on 20 different storage servers, and can rebuild from any 17. Overhead is 20/17, about **1.18x**, tolerating 3 failures. Backblaze open-sourced its Java Reed-Solomon library under the MIT licence; it handles up to 256 shards in total and encoded about 149 MB/s on a single CPU thread of a storage server.
- **Facebook's warm photo storage (f4)** used Reed-Solomon with 10 data and 4 parity shards, 1.4x within a region, with an XOR of two regions' data held in a third for disaster recovery, for an effective 2.1x across regions, down from about 3.6x in the hot photo store it replaced (three replicas, each on RAID-6).
- **Windows Azure Storage** uses Reed-Solomon in production and later a refinement called **Local Reconstruction Codes** (LRC), which adds small local parity groups so the most common repair (one lost fragment) reads only a handful of fragments rather than all k. Its configurations land at about **1.3x to 1.5x**.
- **S3** uses erasure coding across Availability Zones for its standard storage classes, which is how it reaches eleven nines without storing several full copies in each zone.

%%bars Raw storage needed per byte of data under common redundancy schemes.
3x replication | 3 | 3.0x, 2 failures
10 + 4 Reed-Solomon | 1.4 | 1.4x, 4 failures
*17 + 3 Reed-Solomon | 1.18 | 1.18x, 3 failures
12 + 2 + 2 LRC | 1.33 | 1.33x
%%

## The hidden cost: repair
Erasure coding looks strictly better until a disk fails. With 3x replication, rebuilding one lost piece means **reading one copy** of it. With k + m erasure coding, rebuilding one lost shard means **reading k other shards** (from k different machines) and recomputing. For 10 + 4, a lost shard costs reading 10 shards' worth of data across the network.

So a dead disk generates roughly **k times more repair traffic** than it would with replication. In a large fleet with several disk deaths a day, that repair traffic is a significant, permanent load on disks and network. Repair speed also matters for durability: the longer a stripe runs with missing shards, the more exposed it is to further failures.

Erasure coding has other costs too:
- **Small reads touch many disks.** Reading a whole object means reading k shards. For small objects this multiplies I/O operations, so systems erasure-code large extents (often a gigabyte) rather than individual small objects.
- **Writes need encoding** and a write to k + m disks. Updating part of a stripe means recomputing parity, which is why erasure coding pairs naturally with **immutable, sealed** data.
- **CPU** for encoding and decoding, though modern libraries use SIMD instructions and run at gigabytes per second.

%%matrix Replication versus erasure coding on the properties that decide which to use where.
Property | 3x replication | 10 + 4 erasure coding
Storage overhead | no, 3.0x | yes, 1.4x
Failures tolerated | partial, 2 | yes, 4
Healthy read | yes, any one copy | partial, read 10 shards
Repair one lost unit | yes, read 1 unit | no, read 10 units
Small or changing data | yes | no
Large sealed data | partial, costly | yes
%%

## Replicate first, erasure-code later
Because each scheme suits a different stage of data's life, large systems use **both**. Windows Azure Storage is a clear example:
- **Intra-stamp replication** happens in the stream layer, synchronously, on the write path. Every append goes to three replicas in different fault domains before it is acknowledged (lesson 07.18). An acknowledged write therefore survives disk, node and rack failures, and the contract is at least three durable copies, even through a cluster-wide power failure.
- **Erasure coding** happens in the background on **sealed** extents. Each sealed blob extent is split at block boundaries into N fragments plus M Reed-Solomon parity fragments, spread across fault domains, and then the extra full replicas are dropped. Storage cost falls from 3x to about 1.3x to 1.5x.
- **Inter-stamp (geo) replication** happens in the partition layer, asynchronously, copying objects to a second stamp in another region for disaster recovery. It is committed on the secondary within about 30 seconds on average.

%%timeline The life of a blob extent's redundancy in a replicate-then-erasure-code design.
0 s | Appended: three full replicas across fault domains, acknowledged
About 30 s | Async geo-replication copies the objects to another region
Minutes to hours | Extent reaches 1 GB and is sealed, immutable
Later, background | *Erasure-coded into N + M fragments; extra replicas deleted, 3x becomes about 1.3x
On disk failure | Lost fragments rebuilt from surviving ones elsewhere
%%

The reasoning: while data is being written, replication is simpler, faster and allows reads from any copy; recently written data is also the most likely to be read or deleted soon. Once it's sealed and cooling, the storage cost dominates, and erasure coding's repair cost is paid rarely.

Erasure coding even helps reads in one way. WAS sends reads with a deadline; for erasure-coded data on a busy disk, it can read all N + M fragments and use the first N to arrive, so one slow disk never sets the latency.

## Thinking about durability numbers
Durability figures like "eleven nines" come from models of failure and repair rates. A rough version shows what matters.

! Example: why repair speed matters as much as copies
! Suppose drives have a 2% AFR, so the chance a given drive fails on a given day is about 0.02 / 365 ≈ 0.000055. With 3x replication and a 1-day repair window, after one copy is lost, data is lost only if both other copies' drives fail before repair: about 0.000055 × 0.000055 ≈ 3 × 10^-9 per incident.
! Halve the repair time to 12 hours and that drops about fourfold, because both remaining failures must fall in a shorter window. Lengthen it to a week (a slow rebuild of a 20 TB disk) and the risk rises about 50-fold. Erasure coding with 4 parity shards needs 5 failures in the window, which pushes the risk down by many more orders of magnitude, provided repairs keep up. Real models also include correlated failures (a rack losing power, a bad batch of drives), which is why shards must be spread across failure domains: independence is the assumption every one of these numbers depends on.

! Example: 1 PB of backups
! Stored with 3x replication, 1 PB of data needs 3 PB of raw disk. With 10 + 4 erasure coding it needs 1.4 PB, saving 1.6 PB, about 80 drives of 20 TB, and survives four simultaneous failures instead of two.
! When a 20 TB disk dies, though, rebuilding its shards reads about 200 TB from other disks (10 shards read per shard rebuilt) instead of 20 TB. Spread over 500 other disks in the cluster, that's 400 GB each, which the cluster can absorb in a few hours of background bandwidth, but it's a real, recurring cost that has to be budgeted, and it's why codes like LRC exist.

## Common mistakes
- **Counting copies but ignoring placement.** Three replicas in one rack survive a disk failure but not a rack failure.
- **Erasure-coding small, hot or mutable data.** Every read touches k disks and every update recomputes parity.
- **Ignoring repair bandwidth.** A design that tolerates 4 failures but takes a week to repair one can be less durable than expected.
- **Confusing durability with backups.** Redundancy protects against hardware failure, not against a bug or a user deleting data; that needs versioning and backups.

## In an interview
Quote the numbers: "3x replication costs 200% extra and tolerates 2 failures with cheap repair; 10 + 4 Reed-Solomon costs 40% extra and tolerates 4, but repairing one shard reads 10. So I'd replicate on the write path and erasure-code sealed, colder data in the background, with shards spread across racks or zones." Mention that repair speed and independent failure domains drive real durability.

> Takeaway: replication is simple, cheap to read and cheap to repair but costs 3x; erasure coding gives equal or better durability at about 1.2x to 1.5x but makes repairs about k times more expensive and small reads touch many disks. Large systems replicate data while it is written and erasure-code it once sealed, spreading every copy or shard across failure domains.

## The question
Data travelling from your application to a storage service passes through many hands: your process's memory, a TLS library, the kernel, a network card, switches, a load balancer, a proxy, the storage service's front end, its memory, its storage engine, a disk controller, the disk itself, and later the whole chain in reverse. Several of those steps already check for errors. TCP has a checksum. Ethernet has a CRC. Disks have error-correcting codes. So why do serious storage systems insist on **another** checksum, computed by the sender and verified by the final receiver?

The answer is one of the most important principles in systems design, and it applies far beyond storage.

## The end-to-end argument
The **end-to-end argument**, first set out in 1981, says roughly this: a function such as "make sure the data arrived intact" can be implemented **completely and correctly only with the knowledge and help of the application at the endpoints**. Providing it inside the communication system alone is not enough. A partial version inside the system can still be worth having, but only as a **performance enhancement**.

The standard illustration is **careful file transfer** from host A to host B. List what can go wrong:
1. The file on A's disk may now read back incorrectly because of a disk fault.
2. The file system, the transfer program or the network software may make a mistake while buffering or copying the data, on A or B.
3. The processor or its memory may have a transient error during copying, on A or B.
4. The network may drop, change, lose or duplicate packets.
5. Either host may crash partway through.

Now suppose you make the network perfectly reliable. That fixes threat 4 and nothing else. Threats 1, 2, 3 and 5 all happen **outside** the network, inside the hosts, where no network check can see them. The only check that covers everything is end to end: A computes a checksum of the file as it should be; after B has written it, B reads it back, computes the same checksum and compares. If they differ, retry.

Does that make network reliability useless? No. If the network corrupted 10% of packets, the end-to-end check would fail constantly and every transfer would be retried many times. Lower-level checks are worth having **because they catch errors early and make end-to-end retries rare**. They just can't be the guarantee.

%%layers The path of an upload and which checks cover each part. Every layer's check covers only its own segment; only an end-to-end checksum covers all of them.
*Sender application | computes the end-to-end checksum
Client memory, TLS, kernel | covered by ECC memory at best
TCP between client and proxy | 16-bit TCP checksum, this hop only
Proxy or load balancer memory | not covered by any network check
TCP between proxy and server | a new checksum, this hop only
Server memory and storage engine | not covered by TCP
Disk controller and media | sector ECC, misdirected writes not caught
*Receiver verification | recomputes and compares the checksum
%%

## A real failure: the byte-swapping gateway
A well-known incident shows exactly how per-hop checks fail. A network made of local networks joined by **gateways** used a checksum on every hop. One gateway developed a transient hardware fault in the code that copied data between its buffers: about once in every million bytes, it **swapped a pair of bytes**. Every hop's checksum passed, because each hop computed its checksum on the data **after** the gateway had already corrupted it. The corruption happened inside the gateway's memory, between two checked segments.

Operating system source files transferred through that gateway were silently corrupted. Nobody noticed until much later, and the owners had to repair them by hand, comparing against old printed listings. An end-to-end checksum, computed by the sender and checked by the receiver, would have caught every one of those transfers immediately.

%%compare Per-hop checksums versus an end-to-end checksum, for corruption that happens inside an intermediate device.
[Per-hop checks only]
Sender -(checked)-> Gateway memory -(checked)-> Receiver
- Corruption inside the gateway passes
- Each hop checksums already-bad data
- Silent damage discovered much later
[*End-to-end checksum]
Sender computes C -> any path -> Receiver recomputes C
+ Catches corruption anywhere on the path
+ Detects bugs in proxies and software
+ Failed check triggers a clean retry
%%

## Why TCP's checksum is not enough
- **It covers one hop of one connection.** TCP checksums protect data between two TCP endpoints. Load balancers, TLS terminators and proxies end one TCP connection and start another, and in between the data sits in their memory, unprotected. So do your application's buffers, the storage server's memory, the storage engine's code and the disk firmware. That's the gateway problem, in modern form.
- **It is weak.** The TCP checksum is a 16-bit ones'-complement sum. It misses whole classes of errors, including reordered 16-bit words (exactly the gateway's byte-pair swaps, if aligned) and a fraction of random errors. Measurements on real networks have found that somewhere between about one in tens of millions and one in billions of packets carry errors that the TCP checksum fails to catch, usually introduced by faulty hardware on hosts or routers rather than on the wire. Ethernet's CRC is stronger, but it is recomputed at each link, so it too only covers one hop.
- **Scale makes rare errors routine.** Hard drives specify an unrecoverable read error rate of about 1 per 10^15 bits (enterprise) to 10^14 (consumer): roughly one bad read per 125 TB or 12.5 TB. Memory without ECC suffers bit flips; even CPUs occasionally compute wrong results silently. Disks sometimes write data to the wrong place (**misdirected writes**) or report a write that never happened (**lost writes**), and the disk's own error correction can't detect either, because the sector it reads back is internally consistent, just wrong. At exabyte scale, events that are one-in-a-trillion happen every day.

%%bars Expected unrecoverable disk read errors per day at the enterprise rate of 1 per 10^15 bits, by daily read volume. At large scale, rare errors become routine.
10 TB read per day | 0.08 | about 1 every 12 days
1 PB read per day | 8 | about 8 a day
*1 EB read per day | 8000 | about 8,000 a day
%%

## How storage systems apply it
The principle, made practical:
- **Compute the checksum as early as possible**, ideally in the client application before the data leaves it, from the data as the application knows it.
- **Carry it with the data** through every layer.
- **Verify it at every place the data lands**: when the service receives it, before acknowledging; when it's written to disk; on every read; and in periodic background **scrubbing** of data at rest, to find silent corruption before a user does and while other redundant copies or shards can still repair it.
- **Store it alongside the data**, so a reader can verify what it got.

Inside storage systems you've met in this module: every block in the WAS stream layer carries its own checksum, verified on every read (lesson 07.18); RocksDB checksums every SST block and WAL record (lesson 07.10); Bitcask stores a CRC per record (lesson 07.08). Filesystems such as ZFS store each block's checksum in the **parent** block pointer rather than next to the block itself, so a misdirected or lost write (which leaves an old but internally valid block in place) is caught, because the parent's checksum doesn't match.

## How S3 does it
S3 exposes end-to-end integrity to clients directly:
- **Algorithms**: CRC64NVME (the default), CRC32, CRC32C, SHA-1, SHA-256, MD5, XXHash64, XXHash3, XXHash128 and SHA-512. The client sends its value in an `x-amz-checksum-...` header. S3 computes its own checksum on the bytes it received and **validates it before storing** the object, then stores the checksum with it. A mismatch rejects the PUT.
- **Automatic**: AWS SDKs compute a checksum by default if you don't supply one; if neither client nor SDK does, S3 computes CRC64NVME itself on arrival (which protects storage but not the path from your application).
- **Trailing checksums**: for streaming uploads, where the checksum isn't known until all the data has been read, it can be sent as a **trailer** after the body, announced with `x-amz-trailer`. The client computes as it streams, with no second pass over the data.
- **Multipart uploads** have two kinds. A **full-object** checksum covers every byte from first to last (CRC64NVME, CRC32, CRC32C), because CRCs of parts can be mathematically combined. A **composite** checksum is a checksum of the per-part checksums (for SHA and MD5 families, which can't be combined).
- **Downloads**: `GetObject` and `HeadObject` return the stored checksum when checksum mode is enabled, so the client can verify what it received.
- **ETags are not checksums.** The ETag is the MD5 of the data only for single-part uploads that are plaintext or SSE-S3 encrypted. For multipart uploads it's an MD5 of the parts' MD5s with "-N" appended. Don't rely on it for integrity.
- **At rest**: S3 Batch Operations' **Compute checksum** recalculates checksums for billions of stored objects without downloading them and produces an integrity report.

%%seq End-to-end integrity with S3: the client checksums before sending, S3 validates before storing, and the client verifies again on download.
Client > Client : compute CRC64NVME of the file
Client > S3 : PUT + x-amz-checksum-crc64nvme
S3 > S3 : recompute on received bytes, compare
S3 -> Client : 200 OK (or 400 on mismatch)
Client > S3 : GET with checksum mode enabled
S3 -> Client : bytes + stored checksum
Client > Client : recompute and compare
%%

! Example: a corrupting proxy
! A company's upload service sits behind a TLS-terminating proxy with a faulty memory module that flips about one bit per 10^12 bytes. The service uploads 50 TB a day, so about 50 objects a day are silently corrupted inside the proxy. TCP checksums pass on both hops, because each hop checksums the already-damaged data.
! The client computes SHA-256 before upload and sends it as `x-amz-checksum-sha256`. For those 50 objects, S3's computed value doesn't match, the PUT is rejected with an error, and the client retries cleanly, probably through a different proxy. Without the end-to-end checksum, 50 corrupt objects a day would be stored with an "OK", and discovered months later when someone opened a damaged backup.

! Example: choosing the algorithm
! A backup tool uploads 5 GB files. Computing SHA-256 runs at roughly 0.5 to 2 GB/s per core; CRC64 or CRC32C with hardware instructions runs at 10 GB/s or more. For pure corruption detection, a CRC is ample (accidental corruption doesn't conspire to match a 64-bit CRC), costs well under a second per file, and supports full-object checksums for multipart uploads. Use SHA-256 when you also need protection against deliberate tampering, or a content-addressed key.

## Common mistakes
- **Trusting TCP or TLS.** TLS does authenticate data in transit, but only between its two endpoints; a TLS-terminating proxy decrypts into plain memory, and corruption there is invisible.
- **Checksumming after the data has been through several layers.** Compute it where the data originates.
- **Using the ETag as a checksum** for multipart uploads.
- **Verifying on write but never at rest.** Bit rot is found by scrubbing, not by the next customer who reads the file.
- **Detecting but not repairing.** A checksum failure on read should trigger a read from another replica or shard and a repair of the bad copy.

## In an interview
Whenever data integrity matters (backups, financial records, media pipelines), say: "The client computes a checksum (CRC32C or CRC64 for speed, SHA-256 if we need tamper-evidence) and sends it with the upload; the service verifies before acknowledging, stores the checksum with the data, verifies on every read and scrubs at rest; downloads verify too." If asked why, give the end-to-end argument in one sentence: lower-layer checks only cover their own segment, and corruption happens in memory, proxies, software and disks between them.

> Takeaway: only the endpoints can confirm that what was stored is what was sent. Checksum at the source, carry the checksum with the data, verify before acknowledging, on every read and at rest, and treat lower-layer checks like TCP's as a way to catch errors sooner, not as the guarantee.

Corruption also happens where TCP can't see it: in the sender's and receiver's memory, in proxies, load balancers and TLS terminators that end one connection and start another, in application and storage-engine bugs, and on disks (bad reads, misdirected or lost writes), as in the classic case of a gateway that swapped bytes in its own memory while every hop's checksum passed. Only a checksum computed by the original sender from the data as it should be, carried with the data and verified by the final receiver (and rechecked on reads and at rest), confirms the data is intact end to end; lower-layer checks are still useful because they catch errors early and make end-to-end retries rare.
