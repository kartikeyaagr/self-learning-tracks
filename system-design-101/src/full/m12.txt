@12.01
## The query every location product needs
"Restaurants within 2 km of me." "Drivers near this rider." "Friends who are in this city right now." Every one of these is a **proximity query**: given a point (the user's latitude and longitude) and a radius, return the objects whose positions fall inside the circle, usually sorted by distance and cut to the top few dozen.

Each stored object is just a row with two numbers: **latitude** (degrees north or south of the equator, from −90 to 90) and **longitude** (degrees east or west of the prime meridian, from −180 to 180). The naive SQL is a **bounding box**, a rectangle that contains the circle:

`SELECT * FROM places WHERE lat BETWEEN 40.70 AND 40.74 AND lng BETWEEN -74.02 AND -73.98`

You then compute the true distance for each returned row (with the **haversine formula**, the great-circle distance on a sphere) and keep those under 2 km. The box is a cheap over-approximation; the exact distance check trims its corners.

To make the numbers concrete, imagine a Yelp-like system: about **100 million daily users** and **10 million businesses**. Nearby search is the main read path, so it might run at 5,000 to 20,000 queries per second at peak. Each query must return in tens of milliseconds. The question for this lesson is why the obvious way to index that table does not work, and what does.

## How a B-tree index answers a range query
Start from first principles. A **B-tree index** (lesson 03.04 covers it in detail) keeps keys in **sorted order** across a balanced tree of disk pages. To answer `lat BETWEEN 40.70 AND 40.74`, the database descends the tree to the first key at or above 40.70 (about 3 or 4 page reads for millions of rows), then walks the leaf pages in order until the key passes 40.74. The cost is roughly **log N to find the start, plus the number of matching entries**.

That is excellent when the range is selective. The problem is that "selective in one dimension" is not the same as "selective in two".

### A latitude index returns a strip around the planet
Every place between 40.70 and 40.74 degrees north matches the latitude condition, wherever it is on Earth. That band runs right round the planet at New York's latitude: through central Spain, southern Italy, Turkey, Central Asia, northern China and the western United States. The index gives you a **horizontal strip about 4.4 km tall that circles the whole globe**.

### A longitude index returns a strip from pole to pole
The longitude condition alone matches a **vertical strip** about 3.4 km wide at New York's latitude, from the Arctic to Antarctica, passing New York, the Caribbean near Haiti, and Colombia and Peru.

### Combining them still reads too much
The database has three plans, and none is good:
1. **Use one index, filter the other condition row by row.** It reads the whole latitude strip and discards everything outside the longitude range.
2. **Intersect the two indexes** (a bitmap AND, which Postgres can do). It reads both strips in full, builds two sets of row IDs, and intersects them. You still touch both strips.
3. **Full table scan.** Read all 10 million rows.

### A composite index does not save you
A **composite index** on `(lat, lng)` sounds like a 2D index, but it is not. A B-tree sorts by the first column and uses the second only to **break ties**. Latitudes are real numbers, so ties almost never happen. The index is effectively sorted by latitude alone; within the range 40.70 to 40.74 the longitudes are scattered in no useful order, and the database must still scan the whole strip and check each longitude.

The underlying reason is simple: **a one-dimensional sort order cannot preserve two-dimensional closeness**. Two places on the same latitude, one in New York and one in Madrid, sit right next to each other in the index. Two places 50 m apart, one just north and one just south of 40.70, can be far apart.

%%compare What each index shape reads for one 4 km box. The B-tree strategies read whole strips; a spatial index reads only the neighbourhood.
[Latitude B-tree]
lat 40.70 to 40.74 -> Strip around the globe -> Filter lng row by row
- Reads about 100,000 entries
- Grows with the whole world's data
[Composite (lat, lng)]

[*Spatial index]

%%

! Example: what the bad query costs
! 10 million businesses spread around the world. A 0.04 degree latitude band (about 4.4 km tall) contains maybe 100,000 businesses worldwide; a 0.04 degree longitude band maybe 50,000. The box where they cross holds about 300.
! Plan 1 reads 100,000 index entries and, if the table is not covered by the index, up to 100,000 random heap page reads at roughly 0.1 ms each on SSD: about 10 seconds of I/O in the worst case for 300 results. Even fully cached, scanning 100,000 entries costs tens of milliseconds of CPU per query.
! At 10,000 queries per second, that is a billion index entries scanned per second. With a spatial index, each query descends to a handful of leaf nodes or cells covering the box and reads about 300 matches plus a few hundred near misses that the exact distance filter removes: under 1,000 entries, a reduction of about 100 times.

## The fixes: make space searchable
There are two families of solution. Both rest on the same insight: you need a structure where things that are close in 2D are close in the index.

### Family 1: spatial trees
These understand two dimensions directly by recursively dividing space or grouping objects.

**Quadtrees.** Take a square region (the whole map). If it holds more than some threshold of points (say 100), split it into four equal quadrants: north-west, north-east, south-west, south-east. Repeat for any quadrant that is still over the threshold. Each leaf holds at most 100 points. The tree **adapts to density**: Manhattan becomes a deep tree with tiny leaves a few hundred metres across, while the middle of the Pacific stays one huge leaf at depth 2. A query descends only into quadrants that overlap the search circle, then scans the points in the overlapping leaves.

%%tree A point quadtree over a city: dense areas split deeper, sparse ones stay as large leaves. Leaves hold at most 100 points.
World
  NW quadrant | 40 points, leaf
  NE quadrant | split
    NE.NW | 80 points, leaf
    *NE.NE: Manhattan | split again
      NE.NE.NW | 95 points
      *NE.NE.NE | 100 points, query lands here
      NE.NE.SW | 70 points
      NE.NE.SE | 60 points
    NE.SW | 30 points, leaf
    NE.SE | 12 points, leaf
  SW quadrant | 3 points, leaf
  SE quadrant | 0 points, leaf
%%

Quadtrees are easy to build in memory and are a common answer in interviews for a "nearby businesses" service: build the tree from the business table at start-up, keep it in RAM on each search server, and rebuild or patch it as businesses are added. With 10 million businesses at about 100 per leaf, you get about 100,000 leaves; at roughly 1 KB per node plus 24 bytes per point (ID plus coordinates), the whole tree fits in well under 1 GB.

**R-trees.** An R-tree wraps each object in its **minimum bounding rectangle** (MBR), the smallest axis-aligned box that contains it, then groups nearby rectangles into larger rectangles, level by level, up to a root. Like a B-tree, it is balanced and its nodes are sized to disk pages (a node may hold 50 to 200 children). A query checks which children's rectangles overlap the search area and descends only into those. Rectangles at the same level may **overlap**, so a query sometimes descends several branches. PostGIS implements R-trees on Postgres's **GiST** (Generalised Search Tree) framework, and the **R\*-tree** variant it draws on chooses splits that keep overlap and total area low. R-trees handle shapes, not just points: polygons, lines, delivery zones.

**k-d trees and BKD trees.** A **k-d tree** splits space alternately by x and y at the median point. Elasticsearch and Lucene store geo fields in **block k-d (BKD) trees**: k-d trees packed into disk-page-sized blocks and built in bulk per index segment. Because Lucene segments are write-once, the tree is built in a batch when a segment is flushed or merged, which is efficient for search engines that index in batches but means updates arrive with a short delay.

### Family 2: encoded cells
These take a different route: convert each 2D position into a **1D key** in such a way that nearby points usually get nearby keys, then store the key in an **ordinary** B-tree or sorted set.
- **Geohash** (lesson 12.02): interleave the bits of latitude and longitude and write them in base 32. Nearby points usually share a prefix, so a prefix scan finds them.
- **S2 and H3** (lesson 12.03): Google's sphere-based square cells and Uber's hexagonal cells, each with a 64-bit cell ID.
- **Redis GEO** (lesson 12.04): stores a 52-bit geohash as the score in a sorted set.

The general recipe is the same in every case.

%%flow Encoded cells: convert the point to a cell key, scan nearby keys with an ordinary index, then filter by exact distance.
Rider at 40.72, -73.99 -(encode)-> *Cell dr5ru -(scan cell plus neighbours)-> Candidates -(exact distance under 2 km)-> Results
%%

1. **Encode** the query point into its cell at a resolution roughly matching the search radius.
2. **Expand** to the neighbouring cells, because the circle may cross a cell boundary (lesson 12.02 explains why this is essential).
3. **Scan** each cell's key range in the ordinary index to get candidates.
4. **Filter** candidates by exact distance and sort.

Cells give up a little precision (the candidate set is always a superset of the answer) in exchange for using infrastructure every database already has.

## The space-filling curve idea underneath
Why do encoded cells work at all, when a sort by latitude does not? The trick is a **space-filling curve**: a path that visits every cell of a grid exactly once and tends to stay in one neighbourhood before moving on. Geohash uses the **Z-order curve** (the path traced by interleaving bits); S2 uses the **Hilbert curve**, which never jumps across the grid between consecutive cells. Sorting by position along such a curve means that a small square region usually corresponds to a few **contiguous ranges** of keys.

"Usually" is the important word. Any mapping from 2D to 1D must break some neighbourhoods: there will always be places where two adjacent cells are far apart on the curve. That is why every cell-based search queries several cells and post-filters. The fix is not perfection; it is making the breaks predictable and cheap to work around.

## Choosing between them
| Situation | Typical choice | Why |
| Static listings: businesses, hotels, stores | Search engine with geo (Elasticsearch BKD) or PostGIS R-tree | Combines distance with text, category and rating filters; writes are rare |
| Polygons: zones, roads, parcels | PostGIS R-tree, S2 coverings | Needs containment and intersection, not just distance |
| Moving points: drivers, couriers, phones | In-memory cells (Redis GEO, H3 keyed maps) | Hundreds of thousands of cheap overwrites per second |
| Analytics and pricing by area | H3 or S2 cell IDs as a column | Group by a cell ID like any other key |
| A quick feature on an existing SQL database | Geohash column with a B-tree | No new infrastructure |

%%matrix How the main spatial index types compare on the properties that drive the choice.
Index | Points | Polygons | Cheap updates | Adapts to density | Ordinary B-tree
Quadtree | yes | partial, via bounding boxes | partial, split or merge leaves | yes | no
R-tree (PostGIS) | yes | yes | partial, rebalancing cost | yes | no
BKD (Elasticsearch) | yes | yes | no, batch built per segment | yes | no
Geohash column | yes | partial, as cell coverings | yes, recompute key | no, fixed cells | yes
S2 or H3 cell IDs | yes | yes, as coverings | yes, recompute key | partial, mixed resolutions | yes
%%

The rule of thumb: **spatial trees for geometry and mostly static data, encoded cells for points that move constantly**. Writes to an R-tree may split and rebalance nodes; writes to a cell index just recompute a key, which is a few arithmetic operations.

! Example: designing "nearby businesses" at 10,000 queries per second
! Data: 10 million businesses at about 1 KB of metadata each is 10 GB, but the spatial index needs only ID, latitude and longitude: 24 bytes each, about 240 MB. That fits in RAM on every server.
! Choice: an in-memory quadtree (or a geohash table) replicated on 10 search servers, each handling 1,000 queries per second. Businesses change rarely (a few thousand edits a day), so rebuild the index nightly and apply edits as small patches, accepting minutes of staleness for new listings.
! Query: the rider's point is located in its leaf (about 25 node visits), neighbouring leaves overlapping the 2 km circle are added (typically 4 to 9 leaves of up to 100 points each), 600 candidates are distance-filtered to 300, the top 20 by a distance and rating score are returned, and the full business records are fetched from a cache by ID. Total server time: about 1 to 3 ms.

## What goes wrong
- **Radius too large for the structure.** A 50 km query in a city expands to hundreds of cells or leaves and tens of thousands of candidates. Cap the radius, or switch to a coarser resolution and rank by relevance instead of returning everything.
- **Empty areas.** In rural areas, a 2 km radius may return nothing. Products usually expand the radius step by step (2 km, 5 km, 20 km) until they have enough results, which means your index must handle wide queries efficiently too. A quadtree does this naturally because sparse areas have large leaves.
- **Distance on a flat map.** Treating degrees as a flat grid is wrong away from the equator: at 60 degrees north, a degree of longitude is half as long as at the equator. Always do the final filter with haversine (or a projection appropriate to the area).
- **Forgetting the boundary.** Querying only the single cell or leaf that contains the point misses neighbours just across its edge. Always include neighbouring cells.

## In an interview
When asked to design proximity search, say the problem out loud: "A B-tree is one-dimensional, so lat and lng indexes return strips, not neighbourhoods." Then ask the questions that pick the structure: Are the objects static or moving? Points or shapes? How many queries and updates per second? Do results need text or category filters? A static business directory points to a geo-enabled search engine or an in-memory quadtree; a fleet of moving drivers points to an in-memory cell index (lesson 12.04). Finish with the exact distance filter, because it shows you know cells are approximations.

> Takeaway: a B-tree orders one dimension, so separate or composite lat/lng indexes return huge strips, not small neighbourhoods. Use a spatial tree (quadtree, R-tree, BKD) for shapes and mostly static data, or encode positions into cells (geohash, S2, H3) along a space-filling curve for moving points, query the cell and its neighbours, then filter candidates by exact distance.

## The idea in one sentence
A **geohash** turns a latitude and longitude into a short string, such as `dr5ru`, so that points in the same small area share the same leading characters. Once you have that, "everything near here" becomes "every key that starts with these characters", and an ordinary B-tree index or sorted set can answer it with a range scan. Lesson 12.01 explained why plain latitude and longitude indexes fail; geohash is the simplest fix that works on any database.

Geohash was published by Gustavo Niemeyer in 2008. The underlying idea, interleaving the bits of two coordinates to get a single number (a **Z-order curve** or **Morton code**), was described by G. M. Morton in 1966.

## How a geohash is built
The construction is repeated halving. You keep two intervals, one for longitude (starting at −180 to 180) and one for latitude (starting at −90 to 90), and you write down a bit each time you halve one of them.
1. Take the **longitude** interval and split it at its midpoint. If the point is in the upper half, write **1** and keep the upper half; otherwise write **0** and keep the lower half.
2. Do the same for the **latitude** interval.
3. Keep alternating: longitude, latitude, longitude, latitude. The bits are therefore **interleaved**: even positions come from longitude, odd positions from latitude.
4. Group the bits into fives and write each group as one **base-32** character from the alphabet `0123456789bcdefghjkmnpqrstuvwxyz` (digits and letters, leaving out a, i, l and o so the strings are hard to misread).

Each character carries 5 bits, so each extra character divides the current cell into 32 smaller cells. Because 5 is odd, the cells alternate in shape: odd-length geohashes give cells that are square in degrees (the first character splits the world 8 ways in longitude and 4 in latitude, giving 45° × 45° cells), and even-length ones give cells twice as wide as they are tall.

%%tree Encoding 42.605 N, 5.603 W: each halving keeps one half and writes one bit. The first five bits, 01101, become the character e.
Whole world
  Bit 1, lng: west half | 0, keep -180 to 0
    Bit 2, lat: north half | 1, keep 0 to 90
      Bit 3, lng: east of -90 | 1, keep -90 to 0
        Bit 4, lat: below 45 | 0, keep 0 to 45
          *Bit 5, lng: east of -45 | 1, so 01101 = e
            Bits 6 to 10: 11111 | z
              Bits 11 to 25 | s, 4, 2
%%

! Example: encoding a point by hand
! Take 42.605 N, 5.603 W. Longitude −5.603 is below the midpoint 0 of −180 to 180, so bit 1 is 0 and the interval becomes −180 to 0. Latitude 42.605 is above the midpoint 0 of −90 to 90, so bit 2 is 1 (interval 0 to 90). Longitude is above −90, bit 3 is 1 (−90 to 0). Latitude is below 45, bit 4 is 0 (0 to 45). Longitude is above −45, bit 5 is 1 (−45 to 0).
! The first five bits are 01101, which is 13, and character number 13 of the alphabet (counting from 0) is `e`. Continuing gives 11111 (31, `z`), 11000 (24, `s`), 00100 (4, `4`) and 00010 (2, `2`). The geohash is `ezs42`, a cell about 4.9 km across that contains the point.
! Decoding runs the same process backwards: each bit tells you which half to keep, and the final pair of intervals is the cell. Its centre is the decoded position, and half the cell size is the error.

## Why prefixes mean nearness
Points in the same cell share every character that identified that cell. All points in the 4.9 km cell `dr5ru` (which covers Midtown Manhattan, including the Empire State Building at `dr5ru6j`) have geohashes beginning `dr5ru`. "Everything in this cell" is therefore a **prefix query**:
- In SQL: `WHERE geohash LIKE 'dr5ru%'`, which a B-tree can serve as the range `geohash >= 'dr5ru' AND geohash < 'dr5rv'`.
- In a key-value store with ordered keys (Cassandra clustering columns, DynamoDB sort keys, Bigtable row keys), a range scan between the same two bounds.
- In Redis, the bits are kept as a 52-bit integer score in a sorted set and the range is a score range (lesson 12.04).

That is the whole trick: a 2D square becomes a 1D range, and a 1D range is what every database is good at.

## The catch: close points need not share a prefix
The relationship only works one way. **Points that share a long prefix are close. Points that are close do not necessarily share a long prefix.** The reason is cell boundaries.

Look at what the 3 × 3 block of cells around `dr5ru` is called:

%%matrix The eight neighbours of dr5ru. The row to the north starts dr72, not dr5r: those cells are adjacent on the map but far away in key order.
Row | West | Middle | East
North | dr725 | dr72h | dr72j
Middle | dr5rg | **dr5ru**, query | dr5rv
South | dr5re | dr5rs | dr5rt
%%

The cells to the east and west share four characters with `dr5ru`. The cells to the north share only three, because a boundary at a coarser level (between the 4-character cells `dr5r` and `dr72`) runs along the top edge of `dr5ru`. A rider standing 10 m south of that line is in `dr5ru`; a driver 10 m north of it is in `dr72h`. A query for `dr5ru%` misses the driver completely.

How bad can it get? It depends on how coarse the boundary is:
- **Ordinary cell edges** at the query precision: neighbours differ in the last character or two.
- **Edges of coarse cells**: neighbours can differ from the second or third character on, as with `dr5r` and `dr72`.
- **The equator and the prime meridian**: these are the very first halvings, so points a metre apart can share **no prefix at all**. A point just north of the equator at longitude 10 E encodes as `s0p058`; one just south encodes as `kpzpgx`. At 51.5 N, the Greenwich meridian gives `u10hbp` just east of it and `gcpuzz` just west.
- **The 180° meridian**: the far east and far west of the longitude range are physically adjacent across the Pacific but sit at opposite ends of the key space.
- **The poles**: all longitudes converge, cells become slivers, and "neighbour" stops meaning much.

There is also a distortion issue: because longitude degrees shrink towards the poles, a geohash cell of a given length covers less ground east to west at high latitudes. A precision that suits Lagos is too fine for Oslo by a factor of two.

%%compare Querying one cell versus the cell and its eight neighbours, for a rider 10 m from a cell edge.
[Single prefix]
Rider in dr5ru -> Scan dr5ru% -> Driver in dr72h missed
- Misses anything across the nearest edge
- Results depend on where the cell lines fall
[*Cell plus 8 neighbours]
Rider in dr5ru -> Scan 9 prefixes -> Haversine filter
+ The 3 by 3 block always covers the circle
+ Correct regardless of cell lines
- Up to 9 range scans and more candidates
%%

## The fix: query the neighbours too
The standard solution is the **3 × 3 search**:
1. Choose a precision whose cell size is **at least the search radius** in both directions, so the circle can extend at most one cell beyond the query cell in any direction.
2. Compute the query point's cell at that precision.
3. Compute its **8 neighbouring cells**. Libraries provide `neighbours(hash)`; it works by decoding the cell, stepping one cell width or height in each direction, and re-encoding (with wrap-around at 180°).
4. Run **9 prefix scans** (they can be issued in parallel, or merged into fewer ranges where neighbours happen to be contiguous in key order).
5. **Post-filter** every candidate by exact distance (haversine), because nine cells cover far more area than the circle, then sort by distance.

Why does a 3 × 3 block always cover the circle? If each cell is at least r wide and r tall, then a circle of radius r centred anywhere inside the middle cell cannot reach past the outer edges of its neighbours. The worst case is a point in a corner of the query cell; even then the circle stays inside the block.

How much extra area do you scan? Nine cells of side r cover 9r², while the circle covers πr², about 3.14r². Roughly a third of the candidates survive the distance filter. If cells are bigger than r, the ratio gets worse: with cells of side 2r, nine cells cover 36r², and fewer than 9% of candidates survive.

! Example: choosing precision for "within 1 km"
! Precision 6 cells are about 1.2 km × 0.6 km near the equator. Their height (0.6 km) is smaller than the 1 km radius, so the circle can reach two cells away north or south and a 3 × 3 block may not cover it. Precision 5 cells (4.9 km × 4.9 km) are larger than the radius in both directions, so 9 of them always do.
! So: compute the point's precision-5 cell and its 8 neighbours and scan 9 prefixes. The block is about 14.7 km × 14.7 km, roughly 216 km², while the circle is 3.14 km². Only about 1.5% of the area is useful.
! In a dense city with 100 places per km², that is about 21,600 candidates for about 314 results. Better: use precision 6 and a larger block of cells, about 3 wide and 5 tall (15 cells, roughly 3.6 km × 3.0 km, 11 km²), which yields about 1,100 candidates. More scans, far fewer rows. Many implementations pick the block size automatically from the radius and the cell dimensions at that latitude.

## Storing geohashes in practice
A few patterns appear in real systems:
- **One column, many precisions.** Store a 12-character geohash (precise to a few centimetres) and query any prefix length. Some systems also store the 5- and 6-character prefixes as separate indexed columns so they can use equality (`geohash6 IN (...)`) instead of `LIKE`.
- **Partition keys.** In a distributed store, use a coarse prefix (say 4 characters) as the partition key and the full geohash as the sort key. All places in one roughly 40 km cell live on one partition, and a radius query touches one to four partitions. Watch for **hot partitions**: the cell over central Tokyo holds vastly more rows than one over Siberia.
- **Search engines.** Solr and Elasticsearch supported geohash prefix filters and grid aggregations; the same neighbour-list idea applies there, even though modern versions use BKD trees for point queries.
- **Map clustering.** Grouping points by a geohash prefix is a quick way to draw "142 restaurants here" bubbles at low zoom: group by the first 4 or 5 characters and count.

## Pitfalls
- **Querying only the containing cell.** The most common bug. It passes tests when test points are in the middle of cells and fails in production at the edges.
- **Precision chosen in degrees, not metres.** A precision that works at the equator is wrong at high latitude because cells are narrower. Compute block size from the real cell dimensions at the query latitude.
- **Skipping the distance filter.** The 3 × 3 block is a square up to three times the radius on each side. Without the haversine filter you return places 3 km away for a 1 km query.
- **Using string comparison on mixed lengths.** `dr5ru` sorts before `dr5ru6j`, which is what you want for prefix ranges, but make sure the upper bound of the range is the next prefix (`dr5rv`), not `dr5ru` plus a high character, or you will miss rows.
- **Expecting good behaviour near the poles or the antimeridian.** If your product serves Fiji, the Aleutians or polar research stations, handle wrap-around explicitly or use S2 (lesson 12.03).

## In an interview
Geohash is often the right first answer for "find nearby X" because it needs no special infrastructure. Explain the encoding briefly (interleaved bits, base 32), say that a prefix is a cell and a cell is a range scan, and then volunteer the boundary problem before the interviewer asks. Describe the 3 × 3 neighbour search and the exact distance filter, and mention how you would choose the precision from the radius. If the interviewer pushes on uneven density (Manhattan versus a desert), mention that a fixed grid does not adapt, and that a quadtree (lesson 12.01) or mixed-resolution cells do.

> Takeaway: a geohash interleaves longitude and latitude bits and writes them in base 32, so points in the same cell share a prefix and a 2D area becomes a 1D range scan. Points near a cell edge (or the equator, prime meridian, antimeridian and poles) can have very different prefixes, so always query the cell plus its 8 neighbours at a precision where cells are at least the radius, then filter by exact distance.

## Why another grid?
A **discrete global grid** is a way of cutting the whole surface of the Earth into cells, each with an ID, usually at many resolutions nested inside one another. Geohash (lesson 12.02) is one. It works, but it has three structural weaknesses:
- **Distortion.** Its cells are rectangles in latitude and longitude. Because lines of longitude converge towards the poles, a geohash cell at 60 degrees north is half as wide on the ground as one at the equator, and near the poles cells become thin slivers.
- **Seams.** The equator, the prime meridian and the 180° meridian are boundaries of the very first bits, so neighbours across them have unrelated keys.
- **Two neighbour distances.** Square-ish cells have edge neighbours and corner neighbours at different distances, which makes "the cells around me" a lopsided idea.

Two production systems fix these problems in different ways: Google's **S2**, which uses squares projected onto a sphere, and Uber's **H3**, which uses hexagons. This lesson explains how each is built, why hexagons behave better for some jobs, and how to choose.

## S2: a cube wrapped around the sphere
S2 models the Earth as a **sphere** rather than a flat map, so there is one coordinate system for the whole planet with **no seams or singularities**. It is built in four steps.
1. **Enclose the sphere in a cube.** Each point on the sphere is projected outward onto one of the cube's **six faces**. A face covers about one sixth of the Earth.
2. **Correct the distortion.** A straight projection would make cells near the face corners much smaller than cells near the face centres. S2 applies a quadratic transform to the face coordinates so that cells at the same level are within a factor of about 2 of each other in area everywhere on Earth.
3. **Subdivide each face like a quadtree.** Level 0 is the face. Each cell splits into four children, down to **level 30**. A level-30 leaf cell is under a square centimetre.
4. **Number the cells along a Hilbert curve.** A **Hilbert curve** is a space-filling curve that visits every cell of a grid once, always stepping to an adjacent cell. Because consecutive cells on the curve are physically adjacent, a compact region usually maps to a few contiguous ranges of IDs.

%%tree The S2 hierarchy: six cube faces, each a quadtree down to level 30. Truncating a cell ID gives its parent.
Earth: 6 cube faces
  Face 2 | level 0, about 85 million km2
    Child 0 to 3 | level 1, 4 per parent
      ... | each level splits into 4
        Level 10 cell | about 80 km2
          *Level 13 cell | about 1.3 km2, neighbourhood
            Level 16 cell | about 20,000 m2, a block
              Level 30 leaf | under 1 cm2
%%

### The 64-bit cell ID
Every S2 cell has a 64-bit ID laid out as: **3 bits** for the face, then **2 bits per level** saying which of the four children was chosen, then a single **1 bit** marking where the meaningful bits end, then zeros. A level-30 cell uses all 3 + 60 bits plus the marker. To get a cell's parent at any level, you clear the low bits and move the marker up, which is a couple of bit operations. So the hierarchy is **exact**: every child lies entirely inside its parent.

### The region coverer
S2's most useful tool is the **region coverer**: give it any region (a circle, a polygon, a country) and limits such as "at most 8 cells, levels 10 to 16", and it returns a set of cells that covers the region, using big cells for the interior and small cells along the edges. Because every cell is a contiguous range of leaf IDs, a covering is a short list of **ID ranges**, and "find points in this region" becomes a handful of range scans on an ordinary sorted index. Coverings can also be **interior** (cells entirely inside the region), which lets you skip exact tests for points that land in them (lesson 12.05).

S2 is the cell system under MongoDB's `2dsphere` index, and it is used widely in Google's mapping products. Because it works on the sphere, it handles polygons that cross the antimeridian or contain a pole, which flat latitude and longitude grids mangle.

! Example: an S2 covering for a 2 km radius search
! The rider is at 40.72 N, 73.99 W. The circle has an area of about 12.6 km². Ask the coverer for at most 8 cells between levels 12 and 15. It might return 2 level-12 cells (each about 5 km²) for the middle and 6 level-13 cells (about 1.3 km² each) around the edges: about 18 km² in total, roughly 5 km² more than the circle.
! Each cell becomes one range of leaf IDs, so the query is 8 range scans on a B-tree column holding each point's level-30 cell ID. Adjacent cells along the Hilbert curve often merge into a single range, so it might be 5 scans in practice. Every candidate is then checked against the exact 2 km distance.

## H3: hexagons on an icosahedron
Uber built **H3** to support **dynamic pricing** (measuring supply and demand per hexagon in each city), **dispatch** decisions, and city-wide analysis and visualisation of trips. Its construction is different from S2's:
1. **Project the Earth onto an icosahedron**, a solid with **20 triangular faces**. The icosahedron is the Platonic solid closest to a sphere, so distortion is low.
2. **Tile the faces with hexagons.** At the coarsest resolution there are **122 base cells**.
3. **Accept 12 pentagons.** A sphere cannot be tiled with hexagons alone (a consequence of Euler's formula for polyhedra), so there are exactly **12 pentagons** at every resolution, one centred on each icosahedron vertex. Uber orients the icosahedron (following Buckminster Fuller's Dymaxion map) so all 12 land in the ocean, where almost nobody calls a ride.
4. **Refine in steps of 7.** There are **16 resolutions**, 0 to 15. Each finer resolution has hexagons of about **one seventh** the area of the previous. This is called **aperture 7**.

A hexagon cannot be split exactly into 7 smaller hexagons, so each resolution's grid is rotated slightly relative to the previous one, and the 7 children are only **approximately** contained in their parent: bits of the children stick out past the parent's edge and bits of the parent are covered by children of a neighbour. Indexing at a **single resolution** is exact; rolling up to a parent introduces a small, bounded error. If your analytics sum resolution-9 counts into resolution-7 parents, a few events near hexagon edges are attributed to a neighbouring parent.

### The H3 index and its operations
Each cell is a **64-bit integer**: a few mode and reserved bits, 4 bits for the resolution, 7 bits for the base cell, then fifteen 3-bit digits (each 0 to 6) naming which child was taken at each level. Key operations:
- `latLngToCell(lat, lng, res)`: point to cell ID (formerly `geoToH3`).
- `gridDisk(cell, k)`: all cells within **grid distance** k (formerly `kRing`). k = 1 gives the cell plus its 6 neighbours (7 cells); in general a disk has 1 + 3k(k + 1) cells, so k = 2 gives 19.
- `cellToParent` and `cellToChildren`: move between resolutions.
- `polygonToCells`: all cells whose centres lie inside a polygon.
- `compactCells`: replace each complete set of 7 children with their parent, so a region is stored as a mix of resolutions. A city-sized region of about 10,000 fine hexagons typically compacts to under a thousand cells, because its interior is replaced by a few large parents.

## Why hexagons: one neighbour distance
In any grid, the **neighbours** of a cell are the cells it touches. The question that matters is how far away their centres are.
- A **square** with side d has **8 neighbours**: 4 share an edge, with centres at distance **d**, and 4 touch only at a corner, with centres at **d√2 ≈ 1.41d**. That is **two** different distances.
- A **triangle** has neighbours sharing an edge and neighbours sharing only a vertex, at **three** different distances.
- A **hexagon** has exactly **6 neighbours**, and every one shares a full edge. By symmetry, all six centres are the **same distance** away (√3 times the edge length). There is no such thing as a corner-only neighbour.

The deep reason is geometric: hexagons are the regular polygon with the most sides that still tiles the plane, which makes them the closest a tile can get to a circle. A hexagonal tiling is also the only regular tiling in which neighbouring cells never meet at a single point.

%%compare Neighbour structure of the two grid shapes. Hexagon neighbours are all equivalent; square neighbours come in two kinds.
[Square grid]
Centre -(4 edge neighbours)-> distance d
Centre -(4 corner neighbours)-> distance 1.41 d
- Two distances, two kinds of neighbour
- Rings look like squares, not circles
[*Hexagon grid]
Centre -(6 edge neighbours)-> distance d
+ One distance, one kind of neighbour
+ Rings approximate circles
- Children only approximately nest
%%

### Why it matters in practice
- **Rings approximate circles.** `gridDisk(cell, 2)` is a near-circular blob of 19 cells. With squares, the cells within two steps are either a diamond (if you only count edge neighbours) or a square whose corners are 41% further away than its sides (if you count diagonals). Radius searches, catchment areas and "how far can a driver reach in 5 minutes" all treat every direction alike on hexagons.
- **Smoothing is uniform.** Surge pricing blends each cell's demand with its neighbours' to avoid sharp price cliffs between adjacent cells. On hexagons, one weight serves all six neighbours. On squares you must decide whether diagonals count and how much less, and any choice creates directional artefacts.
- **Movement is cleaner.** A car crossing a city leaves a trail of cells. On a square grid it can pass exactly through a corner, touching two diagonal cells in an ambiguous way. On hexagons, movement always crosses an edge into exactly one neighbour, which **minimises quantisation error** in flow and trajectory analysis.
- **Simpler maths.** Gradients, diffusion and flow calculations on a grid need coefficients per neighbour type. One type means one coefficient.

! Example: how circular is a ring?
! Take resolution-8 hexagons (about 0.74 km² each, centres about 0.8 km apart). `gridDisk(cell, 2)` gives 19 cells, about 14 km². The equivalent circle (14 km² area) has a radius of about 2.1 km, and every cell centre in the disk is within about 1.6 km of the centre, roughly evenly in all directions.
! Now a square grid with 0.86 km sides (the same area per cell). "Within 2 steps including diagonals" is a 5 × 5 block, 25 cells and 18.5 km². Its edge centres are 1.7 km away but its corner centres are 2.4 km away, so a "2-step" search reaches 41% further towards the diagonals than along the axes.

## The trade-off of hexagons
Hexagons are not free. H3 cells are **not laid along a space-filling curve** in a way that keeps neighbours close in ID order, so you cannot answer "everything near here" with a single range scan. Instead you **compute the exact set of cell IDs** you need (a `gridDisk`) and query `WHERE h3_cell IN (...)` or do several key lookups. For a disk of 19 cells, that is 19 lookups, which is fine in memory or in a key-value store and acceptable in SQL. The approximate parent-child nesting, as described above, also makes hierarchical roll-ups slightly lossy.

S2 makes the opposite trade: squares with an exact hierarchy and Hilbert ordering, so regions become ID ranges, but with two neighbour distances and cells whose shapes vary somewhat across a face.

%%matrix Geohash, S2 and H3 on the properties that usually decide the choice.
Grid | Cell shape | Low distortion | Exact hierarchy | Range-scan friendly | Uniform neighbours
Geohash | lat and lng rectangles | no, high near poles | yes, prefixes | yes, Z-order | no, two distances
S2 | squares on cube faces | yes, low | yes, ID truncation | yes, Hilbert curve | no, two distances
H3 | hexagons plus 12 pentagons | yes, low | partial, approximate | no, compute cell sets | yes, one distance
%%

## Choosing a grid
- **Geohash** when you want something simple on an existing database, mostly for point search in populated latitudes, and you are happy to handle neighbours and edges yourself.
- **S2** when you need **region coverings** and polygons, global correctness (antimeridian, poles), and range scans on a sorted store. Good for storage indexes and "which documents fall in this region" queries.
- **H3** when the job is **aggregation and analysis by area**: demand and supply per cell, heat maps, pricing, ETAs, movement between cells, machine-learning features keyed by cell. The uniform neighbours make smoothing and spatial statistics behave.

Many systems use more than one: H3 for analytics and pricing, a geohash or S2-backed index for storage queries, and an R-tree in PostGIS for polygons.

! Example: surge pricing in H3
! Uber buckets ride requests and available drivers by resolution-8 hexagon (about 0.74 km² each). Every minute, for each hexagon, it computes a demand-to-supply ratio: 42 requests and 14 drivers gives a ratio of 3.0.
! Raw ratios are noisy, and a price that jumps between adjacent blocks invites drivers to game the boundary. So each cell's ratio is smoothed with its `gridDisk(cell, 1)` neighbours: for example 50% weight on the cell itself and the remaining 50% split equally across the 6 neighbours, about 8.3% each. Because all six are equally far away, equal weights are correct. A square grid would need one weight for the 4 edge neighbours and a smaller one for the 4 diagonal ones, and the result would still show faint diagonal artefacts.

## Pitfalls
- **Forgetting the pentagons.** At every resolution, 12 cells have 5 neighbours, not 6. Code that assumes `gridDisk(cell, 1)` always returns 7 cells breaks near them. They are in oceans, but global datasets do touch them.
- **Mixing resolutions carelessly.** Joining a resolution-8 table to a resolution-9 table needs an explicit parent or child conversion; and for H3 that conversion is approximate.
- **Choosing too fine a resolution.** Resolution 12 cells (about 300 m²) give one or two events each per hour; statistics become noise. Pick the coarsest resolution that still resolves the effect you care about.
- **Assuming close IDs mean close cells.** That holds roughly for S2 along the Hilbert curve and not at all for H3.

## In an interview
If you propose a grid, say which and why. "Geohash for the business search, because it is a column on our existing database, with a 3 × 3 neighbour query" is a fine answer for a directory. For anything about pricing, demand, ETAs or heat maps, H3 is the natural choice, and you should be ready to explain the hexagon argument: one neighbour distance, so rings are near-circular and smoothing is uniform. If the interviewer asks about polygons or global coverage, mention S2's region coverer and exact hierarchy.

> Takeaway: S2 projects the sphere onto a cube and subdivides each face into a quadtree of square cells numbered along a Hilbert curve, giving low distortion, no seams, exact 64-bit hierarchical IDs and range-scan-friendly coverings. H3 tiles an icosahedron with hexagons (plus 12 pentagons) in 16 resolutions of aperture 7. Hexagons have a single centre-to-neighbour distance (squares have two, triangles three), so rings approximate circles and smoothing and movement behave the same in every direction, at the cost of approximate nesting and no range scans.

?? A hexagon's six neighbours all share a full edge with it, and by symmetry all six centres are the same distance away; no neighbour touches only at a corner. A square has four edge neighbours at distance d and four corner neighbours at about 1.41d, so its neighbours sit at two different distances (triangles have three). With one distance, rings of hexagons approximate circles, smoothing can give every neighbour the same weight, movement always crosses into exactly one neighbour, and spatial maths needs a single set of coefficients; squares make diagonal directions behave differently from axis directions.

## The workload
A ride-hailing app tracks its drivers continuously. Each driver's phone sends its position **every 4 seconds** while the app is online. With **1 million active drivers** at peak, that is **250,000 location writes per second**. At the same time, riders open the app and ask "which drivers are within 3 km?", and the matching service asks the same question every time it needs to dispatch a ride: thousands of queries per second, each needing an answer in a few milliseconds.

Before choosing a store, look closely at what this data is like. It has four unusual properties, and the design follows from them.
- **Write-heavy and overwrite-only.** Each update replaces the previous position for that driver. You never read an old position on the hot path; the history of a trip, if you want it, belongs somewhere else.
- **Short-lived.** A position older than about 30 seconds is useless for matching. The driver has moved 300 m or more.
- **Loss-tolerant.** If the whole store vanished, it would refill itself within one update interval, about 4 seconds, as every online driver sends a fresh position.
- **Small.** Each record is a driver ID, two coordinates and maybe a status: well under 100 bytes. A million drivers is about 100 MB.

Those properties describe a **hot, ephemeral working set**: small enough to fit in memory, changing too fast to be worth making durable. That points to an in-memory store.

## Redis GEO: what it is
Redis has a built-in geospatial index. It is not a separate data structure: it is a **sorted set** (Redis's ordered collection of unique members, each with a numeric score) with helper commands that convert coordinates to and from scores.

### Writing
`GEOADD key longitude latitude member` adds a member, or **updates** its position if it already exists. Underneath:
1. Redis normalises the coordinates. Latitude is limited to about ±85.05 degrees (the limit of the Web Mercator projection), longitude to ±180.
2. It interleaves 26 bits of longitude with 26 bits of latitude into a **52-bit geohash integer** (lesson 12.02), which gives a resolution of well under a metre.
3. It stores that integer as the member's **score** in the sorted set. A 52-bit integer fits exactly in the 53-bit mantissa of the double-precision float that sorted sets use for scores, which is why 52 was chosen.

A sorted set is implemented as a **skip list** (ordered by score, for range queries) plus a **hash table** (member to score, for direct lookups). Updating a member means finding it in the hash table, removing it from the skip list and reinserting it at its new score: **O(log N)**, a few microseconds.

### Querying
`GEOSEARCH key FROMLONLAT lng lat BYRADIUS 3 km ASC COUNT 20 WITHDIST` returns members inside a circle (or a box with `BYBOX`), sorted nearest first, with their distances. (`GEORADIUS` is the older form of the same thing.) Underneath:
1. Redis picks a geohash precision whose cells are large enough for the radius.
2. It computes the query point's cell and its **8 neighbours**, exactly the 3 × 3 trick from lesson 12.02.
3. For each of the 9 cells it does a **score range scan** on the skip list: all members whose score falls inside that cell's range.
4. It computes the exact distance to each candidate, discards those outside the circle, sorts and applies `COUNT`.

The cost is **O(N + log M)**, where M is the size of the set and N is the number of members inside the 9 cells. Everything happens in memory, so a typical query over a city takes well under a millisecond.

```
GEOADD drivers:nyc -73.9857 40.7484 driver:812          # every 4 s per driver
GEOSEARCH drivers:nyc FROMLONLAT -73.99 40.75 BYRADIUS 3 km ASC COUNT 20 WITHDIST
GEOPOS drivers:nyc driver:812                           # read one driver's position
ZREM drivers:nyc driver:812                             # remove: it is just a sorted set
```

Driver app > Location service : position (lat, lng), every 4 s
Location service > Redis : GEOADD drivers:nyc lng lat d812

## Why not Postgres for this?
PostgreSQL with PostGIS can index points in a GiST (R-tree style) index and answer radius queries well. For the static business listings of lesson 12.01, it is a strong choice. The problem here is not reading; it is the **write pattern**. Follow one `UPDATE drivers SET location = ... WHERE id = 812` through Postgres:
1. **MVCC creates a new row version.** Postgres never updates a row in place; it writes a new tuple and marks the old one dead, so concurrent readers can still see the old version (lesson 03.07 covers MVCC).
2. **The spatial index gets a new entry.** Because the indexed column changed, the update cannot use Postgres's cheap "heap-only tuple" path; the GiST index gets an insertion pointing at the new tuple, which may split nodes.
3. **The write-ahead log records it.** Each update writes WAL records for the heap and the index change, and the first change to each page after a checkpoint writes a full 8 KB page image.
4. **The commit waits for fsync** (unless you relax it), so the change is durable on disk.
5. **Replication ships the WAL** to every replica, which replays the same work.
6. **Vacuum cleans up later.** Every update leaves a dead tuple and a dead index entry that autovacuum must scan for and remove.

Multiply by 250,000 per second. Every one of those costs buys durability and history for data that is worthless 30 seconds later and refills itself in 4 seconds if lost. The table also stays tiny (a million rows) while generating gigabytes of dead versions and WAL per minute, which is the worst case for vacuum.

Redis does none of this on the hot path. An update is a hash lookup and a skip-list move in RAM. Persistence can be turned off for this dataset, or relaxed to periodic snapshots, because losing it is cheap.

%%bars Rough sustained update capacity per node for this workload. Postgres pays MVCC, WAL, index and vacuum costs on each write; Redis updates memory.
Postgres with PostGIS | 15000 | about 10,000 to 20,000 per s
Redis GEO, no pipelining | 80000 | about 80,000 per s
*Redis GEO, pipelined | 300000 | 200,000 to 400,000 per s
%%

These are order-of-magnitude figures, and both systems can be tuned, but the gap of about 10 to 30 times per node is real, and it comes from doing far less work per write.

! Example: the numbers
! 1 million drivers × 1 update per 4 s = 250,000 GEOADDs per second. Spread over 20 Redis shards by city or region, that is 12,500 per shard on average, and perhaps 40,000 on the shard holding a big city at rush hour. A Redis instance handles that comfortably, especially if the location service **pipelines** updates (sends a batch of commands in one round trip).
! Memory: a sorted set entry with a member name like `driver:1234567` costs roughly 100 bytes including the skip list and hash table overhead. One million drivers is about 100 MB in total, or 5 MB per shard. A "last seen" set doubles it. That is trivial.
! The same load on Postgres would be 250,000 row updates per second, each with WAL, a GiST insertion and a dead tuple. At about 300 bytes of WAL per update that is 75 MB per second of WAL, about 6.5 TB a day, replicated to every replica, for a table whose live size is 100 MB.

## Separate the hot path from the durable path
The design that comes out of this is two paths with different guarantees.

%%compare The same location stream, split by what each consumer needs. The hot path optimises for speed; the durable path for history.
[*Hot path: Redis GEO]
Every update -> GEOADD overwrite -> Matching reads
+ Latest position only, in memory
+ Sub-millisecond reads and writes
+ Rebuilt by the next round of updates
- No history, no durability
[Durable path: database or lake]
Sampled updates -(batched, async)-> Kafka -> Trips, analytics
+ Full trip traces, billing, disputes
+ Durable and replayable
- Seconds of delay, not used for matching
%%

The location service writes every update to Redis and also publishes it to a log such as Kafka (lesson 08.02 covers logs and queues). Downstream consumers sample or batch it into a durable store: per-trip GPS traces for fare calculation and disputes, positions every 30 seconds for analytics, and aggregates by H3 cell (lesson 12.03) for pricing. None of that sits on the latency-critical path.

## Operating it
### Shard by region
A single sorted set for the whole world would be one key on one Redis node: a hot spot that cannot scale out. Use **one key per city or per coarse cell** (for example a geohash-4 or an S2 level-6 cell), and spread the keys across a Redis Cluster. Queries stay local: a rider in Manhattan only touches the New York key. Drivers near a region boundary need care: either write them to both neighbouring keys, or have queries near a boundary search both keys and merge.

### Stale drivers
Sorted-set members **cannot expire individually**; `EXPIRE` works only on whole keys. If a driver's phone dies, their last position stays in the set forever and they keep being offered rides. Three common fixes:
- **A last-seen set.** Alongside each GEOADD, write `ZADD seen:nyc <timestamp> driver:812`. A background job runs every few seconds: `ZRANGEBYSCORE seen:nyc -inf <now − 30 s>` finds stale drivers, and it removes them from both sets.
- **Filter at query time.** Fetch a few more results than needed and drop any whose last-seen time is too old.
- **Time-bucketed keys.** Write to `drivers:nyc:<minute>` and set a 2-minute TTL on each key. Queries search the current and previous minute and keep the newest position per driver. Old buckets disappear on their own.

### Availability state
A driver on a trip, or one who has just been offered a ride, must not be offered another. Either keep separate keys for available drivers (`ZREM` from `available:nyc` when they accept a trip, `GEOADD` back when they finish), or store status in a hash and filter after the search. Keeping the available set separate makes queries return useful results directly.

%%state A driver's lifecycle as the location store sees it. Only Available drivers are in the set that matching searches.
[*] -> Offline
Offline -> *Available : app online, first GEOADD
Available -> Offered : matcher reserves driver
Offered -> Available : declined or 15 s timeout
Offered -> On trip : accepts, removed from available set
On trip -> Available : trip ends, GEOADD again
Available -> Offline : no update for 30 s, reaper removes
%%

### Reserving a driver safely
Two matching servers may find the same nearby driver at the same moment. Use an atomic reservation, for example `SET reserve:driver:812 ride:991 NX EX 15` (set only if absent, expire in 15 seconds). Whichever matcher succeeds offers the ride; the other moves to its next candidate. The TTL releases the driver automatically if the offer is never answered.

### Failure
If a Redis shard dies, its replica can take over within seconds, but even without replicas the damage is bounded: the next round of updates repopulates the set within about 4 seconds. During that window, riders in that region may see "no drivers nearby" or slightly fewer options. That is why giving up durability here is an acceptable, deliberate choice.

! Example: a shard failure walked through
! 18:02:00: the Redis node holding `drivers:chicago` crashes. Its replica is promoted 3 seconds later, but asynchronous replication lost the last few hundred milliseconds of updates, so some drivers' positions are up to a second older. Nobody notices.
! Now suppose there was no replica. The location service gets connection errors, a new empty node is assigned the key, and from 18:02:05 updates start arriving again. 25,000 Chicago drivers each update every 4 seconds, so after 1 second about a quarter are back, after 4 seconds almost all. Ride requests in those few seconds see thin supply and are retried by the matcher. No trip data is lost, because trips live in the durable path.

## Pitfalls
- **Treating Redis as the system of record.** Trip state, fares and driver accounts belong in a durable database. Redis holds only what can be rebuilt.
- **One giant key.** A worldwide `drivers` key concentrates all traffic on one node and makes every search scan a huge set. Shard by geography.
- **Forgetting staleness.** Without a reaper or time buckets, offline drivers haunt the map.
- **Too large a radius.** A 50 km `GEOSEARCH` in a dense city returns tens of thousands of members before `COUNT` trims them. Start small and widen only if needed.
- **Assuming it is exact distance by road.** GEOSEARCH gives straight-line distance. Rank the top 20 by an ETA service that knows roads and traffic.

## In an interview
State the properties of the data first (overwrite-only, short-lived, self-healing, small), because they justify everything else. Then explain how Redis GEO works underneath (52-bit geohash scores in a sorted set, 9-cell range scans), give the throughput arithmetic, and show the split between the hot path and the durable path. Mention sharding by region, stale-driver cleanup and atomic reservation. If asked "what if Redis goes down?", the honest answer is that the data rebuilds itself in seconds, which is the point.

> Takeaway: frequent driver location updates are write-heavy, overwrite-only, short-lived and self-healing. Redis GEO stores them in memory as 52-bit geohash scores in a sorted set (GEOADD is O(log N), GEOSEARCH scans the 9 surrounding cells), avoiding the per-write MVCC, WAL, index, replication and vacuum costs Postgres would pay. Shard keys by region, reap stale drivers, reserve drivers atomically, and keep trips and history in a durable store on a separate path.

?? Location updates are hundreds of thousands of overwrites per second of data that is useless after about 30 seconds and refills itself within 4 seconds if lost. Postgres would pay for each update with a new MVCC row version, a spatial index insertion, WAL writes and fsync, replication to replicas and later vacuum, all to make worthless data durable. Redis keeps the positions in memory as 52-bit geohash scores in a sorted set, so GEOADD is an O(log N) in-memory update and GEOSEARCH answers radius queries with a few range scans in under a millisecond; durability is unnecessary because the next update rebuilds the state. Trips and sampled history go to a durable database on a separate, asynchronous path.

## The problem
A **geofence** is a virtual boundary drawn on a map, almost always stored as a **polygon**: an ordered list of vertices (latitude and longitude pairs) whose edges enclose an area. Products use them everywhere:
- an **airport pickup zone**, where drivers must queue and riders get special instructions;
- a **delivery area** for a restaurant or a dark store;
- a **surge or pricing region**;
- a **tax or regulatory region** (a city that charges a congestion fee);
- a **no-fly or no-parking zone** for drones and scooters.

The core operation is: for each incoming location update, **which geofences contain this point?** Often the product cares about the change, not the state: "driver entered the JFK zone", "scooter left the permitted area".

Now the scale. Suppose there are **100,000 geofences** worldwide and **250,000 location updates per second** (the driver fleet from lesson 12.04). Testing every polygon for every update would be 25 billion point-in-polygon tests per second. Each test is itself proportional to the polygon's number of edges (lesson 12.06), and a detailed city boundary may have 2,000 vertices. That is not a system you can build. The whole lesson is about not doing that.

## The principle: filter, then refine
Almost every spatial query engine uses the same two-stage pattern, called **filter and refine**:
1. **Filter**: use a cheap, approximate test, supported by an index, to find the small set of fences that **might** contain the point.
2. **Refine**: run the exact, expensive test only on those candidates.

The cheap approximation is the **bounding box** (or minimum bounding rectangle, MBR): the smallest axis-aligned rectangle containing the polygon, stored as four numbers (min and max latitude, min and max longitude). Testing whether a point is in a box is four comparisons. A point outside the box is certainly outside the polygon. A point inside the box may or may not be inside the polygon (it could be in a corner of the box that the polygon does not reach), so it goes to the refine stage.

%%flow Filter then refine: the spatial index narrows 100,000 fences to a few bounding boxes; exact tests run only on those.
Location update -(R-tree on bounding boxes)-> *3 candidate fences -(exact point in polygon)-> 1 or 2 fences contain it -(compare with last result)-> Enter and exit events
%%

## The filter stage: an R-tree of boxes
Checking 100,000 boxes is still too many per update, so the boxes themselves go into a spatial index. The usual choice is an **R-tree** (lesson 12.01): leaves hold fence bounding boxes; each internal node holds a box that encloses all of its children's boxes. To find boxes containing a point, start at the root, descend into every child whose box contains the point, and collect the leaves you reach.

%%tree An R-tree over geofence bounding boxes. A point at JFK descends only into the boxes that contain it; whole continents are skipped.
Root: world
  Europe box | skipped
  *North America box
    West coast box | skipped
    *New York area box
      *JFK pickup zone | leaf, candidate
      LaGuardia zone | leaf, box misses
      *Queens surge zone | leaf, candidate
      Manhattan delivery | leaf, box misses
    *New York state tax region | leaf, candidate
  Asia box | skipped
%%

With a fan-out of about 100 children per node, 100,000 boxes form a tree of 3 levels. A lookup touches a handful of nodes on each level. Boxes can overlap, so occasionally the search descends two or three branches, but the work is still a few dozen box tests instead of 100,000.

### In PostGIS
PostGIS does all of this for you. Create a **GiST** index on the geometry column, and the containment functions use it automatically:

```
CREATE INDEX fences_geom_idx ON geofences USING GIST (geom);

SELECT id, name
FROM geofences
WHERE ST_Contains(geom, ST_SetSRID(ST_MakePoint(-73.78, 40.64), 4326));
```

`ST_Contains` first performs a **bounding-box comparison that uses any available spatial index** (the filter), then runs the exact geometric test on the survivors (the refine). An internal variant, `_ST_Contains`, skips the index step, which is useful only for debugging. The same applies to `ST_Intersects`, `ST_Covers` and `ST_DWithin`.

## Semantics: contains versus covers
Edge cases at the boundary are where geofencing bugs live. `ST_Contains(A, B)` is true when no point of B lies in A's **exterior** and at least one point of B's **interior** lies in A's interior. A consequence that surprises people: **a polygon does not contain a point exactly on its boundary.** If a point on the fence line should count as inside, use `ST_Covers`, which is more inclusive.

For moving vehicles with GPS noise of several metres, a point lying exactly on a line is rare. It matters for:
- **Adjacent fences sharing an edge** (two delivery areas that meet along a street): a point on the shared edge is in neither under `ST_Contains`, and in both under `ST_Covers`. Pick deliberately, and if each point must belong to exactly one zone, add a tie-break rule.
- **Unit tests**, which love points on corners.
- **Snapped coordinates**, such as addresses geocoded to a street centreline that also forms a zone boundary.

## A faster alternative: precompute cells
The R-tree approach does a little index work and a few exact tests per update. At 250,000 updates per second that is fine on a cluster, but you can do better by moving the expensive work from **per update** to **per fence**. Fences change rarely (a few edits a day); updates arrive constantly. So precompute.

1. **Cover each geofence with grid cells** at a chosen resolution, using H3, S2 or geohash (lessons 12.02 and 12.03). S2's region coverer and H3's `polygonToCells` exist for exactly this.
2. **Classify each cell** for each fence: **interior** (the whole cell is inside the fence) or **boundary** (the fence's edge passes through the cell).
3. **Build a map** from cell ID to a short list of (fence ID, interior or boundary). Cells touched by no fence are simply absent.
4. For each location update, **compute the point's cell ID** (a few arithmetic operations) and look it up in the map.

The lookup has three possible outcomes:
- **Absent**: no fence contains the point. Done.
- **Interior entries**: those fences contain the point. No geometry needed.
- **Boundary entries**: run the exact point-in-polygon test against just those fences.

| Cell type for a fence | What the update does | Cost |
| Not in the map | Nothing: no fence here | One hash lookup |
| Interior | Report the fence directly | One hash lookup |
| Boundary | Exact test on that fence | Lookup plus one O(n) test |

Most of the area of a typical fence is interior, and most of the Earth is in no fence at all, so the vast majority of updates are answered by **one hash lookup**. Only points near fence edges pay for geometry.

%%bars Approximate work per location update for 100,000 fences. Precomputed cells answer most updates with one lookup.
Test every polygon | 100000 | 100,000 polygon tests
R-tree filter then refine | 30 | about 30 box tests plus 2 exact tests
*Cell map, interior cell | 1 | 1 hash lookup
Cell map, boundary cell | 2 | 1 lookup plus 1 exact test
%%

### Choosing the resolution
The resolution trades memory against how often you hit boundary cells.
- **Coarser cells** mean fewer entries in the map, but a bigger share of each fence is boundary cells, so more updates need exact tests.
- **Finer cells** mean almost every update lands in an interior or empty cell, but the map grows with the fence's area divided by the cell area.

A common compromise is **mixed resolution**: big cells for the interior of a fence and small cells along its edge, exactly what S2's coverer and H3's compaction produce. The lookup then checks the point's cell at a few resolutions (or walks its parents).

! Example: sizing a cell map
! Say 100,000 fences averaging 5 km² each (500,000 km² in total, overlapping in places). At H3 resolution 9 (about 0.105 km² per cell) that is about 4.8 million cell entries. At around 40 bytes per entry (64-bit cell ID, fence ID and a flag in a hash map) the map is about 200 MB: small enough to keep in memory on every geofence server.
! Boundary share: a 5 km² fence of roughly circular shape has a perimeter of about 8 km. Resolution-9 cells are about 0.35 km across, so the edge passes through roughly 25 to 45 cells, out of about 48 cells in total for the fence. At this resolution half or more of the fence's cells are boundary cells.
! Go one resolution finer for the edges only (resolution 10, about 0.13 km across): keep the roughly 20 interior resolution-9 cells and split the edge band into about 175 resolution-10 cells, of which only some 60 to 100 still touch the boundary. Those thin boundary cells cover only about a tenth of the fence's area, so a point picked at random in the fence has roughly an 85% chance of landing in an interior cell and needing no polygon test at all.

## Further optimisations
- **Check only on change.** A vehicle that is still in the same interior or empty cell as last time cannot have crossed a fence boundary. Cache each vehicle's last cell and last result, and skip the work when nothing changed. At city speeds, a car stays in a resolution-9 cell for tens of seconds, which is many updates.
- **Emit enter and exit events, not states.** Compare the new set of fences with the previous set for that vehicle and publish only the differences ("entered JFK pickup zone"). Downstream consumers then process a tiny event stream instead of every update.
- **Add hysteresis.** GPS jitters by 5 to 20 m. A vehicle parked on a fence line can flip in and out every few seconds, producing a flood of events. Require the vehicle to be inside for two consecutive updates, or 20 m past the line, before declaring an entry, and similarly for exits.
- **Simplify polygons.** A city boundary traced at 2,000 vertices can often be simplified to 200 with an error of a few metres, making exact tests ten times faster. Buffer slightly outward or inward if the product prefers false positives or false negatives.
- **Shard by region.** Keep each city's fences and cell map on the servers that process that city's vehicles, routed by a coarse cell of the update.

%%state One vehicle against one fence, with hysteresis so GPS jitter on the boundary does not produce a storm of events.
[*] -> Outside
Outside -> Entering : first update inside
Entering -> Outside : next update outside
Entering -> *Inside : second update inside, emit ENTER
Inside -> Leaving : first update outside
Leaving -> Inside : next update inside
Leaving -> Outside : second update outside, emit EXIT
%%

! Example: an airport zone, both ways
! A car at JFK sends an update. **R-tree approach**: the search descends to three leaf boxes containing the point: the JFK pickup zone, the Queens surge zone and the New York state tax region. Exact tests (about 300 edges in total) confirm the car is inside all three. About 30 box comparisons and 3 polygon tests.
! **Cell approach**: the point's resolution-9 cell is looked up in the map. It is an interior cell of the tax region and of the surge zone (no test needed) and a boundary cell of the pickup zone (one exact test on its 80 edges). Compared with the vehicle's previous result (tax region and surge zone only), the set gained one fence, so after the second consecutive inside update the service emits `ENTER jfk_pickup` and the driver app shows the queue instructions.

## Keeping fences up to date
Fences do change: a new delivery area launches, an event closes some streets. Because the cell map is derived data, treat it like any other index:
- Store the polygons in a durable database (PostGIS is a good fit) as the **source of truth**.
- On a change, recompute that fence's covering and publish a diff to the geofence servers, or rebuild the whole map periodically and swap it in atomically.
- Version the map, so you can tell which fence definitions produced a given event when investigating a complaint.

## Pitfalls
- **Testing everything.** The naive loop works in a demo with 20 fences and collapses with 20,000.
- **Ignoring boundary semantics.** Decide whether "on the line" is inside, and be consistent across services.
- **No hysteresis.** Fence-entry notifications that fire five times while a driver waits at a kerb.
- **Longitude wrap and big polygons.** A fence crossing the 180° meridian, or a very large one where edges should follow great circles, behaves wrongly in planar geometry. Use geography types or S2 for those.
- **Stale cell maps.** Changing a polygon in the database but not regenerating its covering means the fast path keeps answering with the old shape.

## In an interview
Say "filter and refine" early. Describe the bounding-box R-tree (or PostGIS doing it for you) for a moderate load, then show you can go further by precomputing a cell-to-fences map so most updates are a single hash lookup, with exact tests only for boundary cells. Add the product details that show experience: emit enter and exit events, cache per-vehicle state, add hysteresis for GPS noise, and choose contains or covers deliberately for shared edges.

## The question behind every geofence
Lesson 12.05 narrowed 100,000 geofences down to two or three candidates per location update. The **refine** step still has to answer, exactly: **is this point inside this polygon?** That is the **point-in-polygon** problem. It sounds easy when you look at a drawing, because your eyes see the shape. A computer only has a list of vertices, and the polygon might be concave, have hundreds of edges and wiggle in and out like a coastline.

The standard answer is a short algorithm called **ray casting**. This lesson explains how it works, why it is correct, how to implement it so that awkward cases do not break it, and when you need the alternative, the winding number.

## The algorithm
From the point, draw a **ray**: a half-line that starts at the point and goes off to infinity in one fixed direction. By convention it goes horizontally to the right (towards positive x). Then count how many times the ray **crosses the polygon's edges**.
- **Odd** number of crossings (1, 3, 5, ...): the point is **inside**.
- **Even** number (0, 2, 4, ...): the point is **outside**.

That is all. It is also called the **even-odd rule** or the **crossing number** algorithm. It works for any **simple polygon**, meaning one whose edges do not cross each other, whether convex or concave.

## Why odd means inside
The correctness rests on one theorem and one observation.

**The Jordan curve theorem** says that a simple closed curve in the plane (and a simple polygon's boundary is one) divides the plane into exactly **two regions**: a bounded **inside** and an unbounded **outside**. Every point not on the curve is in exactly one of them, and **you cannot get from one region to the other without crossing the curve**.

**The observation**: the polygon is bounded, so if you go far enough along the ray, past the right-most vertex, you are certainly **outside**.

Now put them together. Walk along the ray **backwards**, from the far end (known to be outside) towards the point you are testing.
1. You start outside.
2. Between crossings you stay in the same region, because crossing the boundary is the only way to change region.
3. Each time you cross an edge, you switch: outside to inside, or inside to outside.
4. When you reach the test point, you have switched once per crossing. After an even number of switches you are back where you started: outside. After an odd number, you are inside.

So the **parity** of the crossing count tells you the region. The direction of the ray does not matter, and neither does the shape: a concave polygon may make the ray cross many times (in, out, in, out, in), but each crossing still flips the state exactly once.

%%state Walking back along the ray from far away: every edge crossing flips the region. Three crossings means the test point is inside.
[*] -> Outside
Outside -> Inside : cross edge at x = 6
Inside -> Outside : cross edge at x = 4
Outside -> *Inside : cross edge at x = 2, reach the point
%%

! Example: a concave U shape
! Take the U-shaped polygon with vertices (0,0), (6,0), (6,6), (4,6), (4,2), (2,2), (2,6), (0,6). It is a square block with a notch cut out of the top middle, between x = 2 and x = 4, down to y = 2.
! **Point P = (1, 4)**, in the left arm. Its ray goes right along y = 4. It crosses the notch's left wall (x = 2, from (2,2) to (2,6)), the notch's right wall (x = 4, from (4,6) to (4,2)) and the outer right wall (x = 6). The outer left wall at x = 0 is to the left of P, so it does not count. **Three crossings: odd, inside.** Correct.
! **Point Q = (3, 4)**, inside the notch, which is outside the polygon. Its ray crosses x = 4 and x = 6. **Two crossings: even, outside.** Correct, even though Q is surrounded by the polygon on three sides.
! **Point R = (3, 1)**, in the bottom bar below the notch. The ray along y = 1 crosses only the right wall at x = 6 (the notch walls only span y = 2 to 6). **One crossing: inside.**

%%timeline The ray from P = (1, 4) through the U shape, read from the point outwards. The region alternates at each crossing.
x = 1 | *Start at P, region unknown
x = 2 | Cross notch left wall
x = 4 | Cross notch right wall
x = 6 | Cross outer right wall
x = 7 and beyond | Certainly outside, so 3 crossings means P is inside
%%

## Implementing it
For each edge from (x1, y1) to (x2, y2), with the test point at (px, py) and the ray going right along y = py:
1. **Does the edge straddle the ray's line?** It can only cross the horizontal line y = py if one endpoint is above it and the other is not.
2. **Where does it cross?** Solve for the x coordinate of the edge at height py: `x = x1 + (py − y1) × (x2 − x1) / (y2 − y1)`. This is just linear interpolation along the edge.
3. **Is that crossing to the right of the point?** If x > px, the ray hits this edge: flip the `inside` flag.

```
def inside(px, py, poly):
    n = len(poly); c = False
    for i in range(n):
        x1, y1 = poly[i]; x2, y2 = poly[(i + 1) % n]
        if (y1 > py) != (y2 > py):            # straddles the ray's line
            x = x1 + (py - y1) * (x2 - x1) / (y2 - y1)
            if x > px:
                c = not c
    return c
```

The cost is **O(n)** for n edges: one comparison per edge, plus a multiply and divide for the few that straddle. A 200-vertex delivery zone takes well under a microsecond. Because the straddle test guarantees y1 ≠ y2, the division never divides by zero.

Two cheap speed-ups are common in production:
- **Bounding box first.** Check the point against the polygon's min and max coordinates before looping. Most candidates from a coarse filter fail this test.
- **Avoid the division.** Compare `(px − x1) × (y2 − y1)` with `(py − y1) × (x2 − x1)`, flipping the comparison when y2 < y1. Same answer, no floating-point division, and slightly more robust.

## The edge cases
Ray casting is short, but most buggy implementations fail on the same handful of cases.

### The ray passes exactly through a vertex
Imagine the ray hitting the exact point where two edges meet. A naive implementation counts both edges, giving two crossings where there should be one, or one where there should be none. Two situations look identical locally but need different answers:
- **Passing through**: the boundary goes from below the ray to above it via the vertex. That is a real crossing: count **once**.
- **Touching**: the boundary comes down to the ray and goes back up (a peak or a valley touching the line). That is not a crossing: count **zero** (or two, which has the same parity).

The fix is a **half-open rule**: treat every vertex that lies exactly on the ray as if it were **slightly above** it. Then an edge is counted only if one endpoint is strictly above py and the other is at or below. The condition `(y1 > py) != (y2 > py)` in the code does exactly this. A vertex shared by two edges is then counted for exactly one of them when the boundary passes through, and for neither or both when it only touches.

! Example: a ray through two vertices of a diamond
! Diamond with vertices (2,0), (4,2), (2,4), (0,2). Test point P = (−1, 2), clearly outside on the left. The ray along y = 2 runs exactly through the vertices (0,2) and (4,2).
! Apply the rule with py = 2. Edge (2,0)→(4,2): is 0 > 2? No. Is 2 > 2? No. Same side, skip. Edge (4,2)→(2,4): 2 > 2 no, 4 > 2 yes. Straddles; it meets the line at x = 4, which is right of −1: **crossing 1**. Edge (2,4)→(0,2): 4 > 2 yes, 2 > 2 no. Straddles; meets the line at x = 0 > −1: **crossing 2**. Edge (0,2)→(2,0): no, no. Skip.
! Two crossings: **outside**. Correct. A naive version counting every edge that touches y = 2 would have counted all four edges and also got an even answer here, but would fail for P = (1, 2), inside the diamond: the rule gives exactly one crossing (at x = 4), so inside.
! Now the tangent case: P = (−1, 4), level with the top vertex (2,4). Edge (4,2)→(2,4): 2 > 4 no, 4 > 4 no. Edge (2,4)→(0,2): no, no. **Zero crossings: outside.** The ray grazed the peak and correctly did not count it.

### Horizontal edges
An edge lying exactly along the ray (both endpoints at y = py) never straddles under the half-open rule, so it is skipped. Its two end vertices are handled by the neighbouring edges, which gives the right parity.

### Points exactly on the boundary
For a point lying on an edge, the answer depends on implementation details and rounding, and may be "inside" for some edges and "outside" for others. If boundary points matter (shared edges between zones, as in lesson 12.05), test for them explicitly first: check whether the point is within a small tolerance of any edge, and return "on the boundary" so the caller can apply its own rule (like `ST_Covers` versus `ST_Contains`).

### Floating point
Near an edge, rounding errors in the intersection formula can flip the answer for points a few nanometres from the line. For geofences with GPS noise of metres, this does not matter. For exact geometry (CAD, map overlays, legal boundaries), robust libraries use **exact predicates**: orientation tests computed with enough precision to always give the correct sign.

%%matrix The edge cases of ray casting and how a correct implementation handles each.
Case | Naive count | Half-open rule | What to do
Ray through a pass-through vertex | no, counts 2 | yes, counts 1 | Rule handles it
Ray grazes a peak or valley | partial, counts 2 | yes, counts 0 | Rule handles it
Horizontal edge on the ray | no, may divide by zero | yes, skipped | Rule handles it
Point exactly on an edge | maybe | maybe | Test for boundary first
Rounding near an edge | maybe | maybe | Tolerance or exact predicates
%%

## Polygons with holes and multiple parts
Real geofences are often **multipolygons**: several separate pieces (an airport with two terminals) or a shape with **holes** (a city zone that excludes a park). Ray casting extends naturally: run the same loop over **every ring** (outer boundaries and hole boundaries) and count all crossings together. A point in a hole crosses the hole's boundary once more than a point just outside the hole, so its parity comes out even: outside. No special case is needed.

## The alternative: the winding number
The **winding number** of a point with respect to a closed curve counts how many times the curve travels **around** the point, anticlockwise counting +1 and clockwise −1. The point is inside if the winding number is **non-zero** (the **nonzero rule**). It can be computed as cheaply as the crossing number: walk the edges, and for each edge that crosses the ray, add +1 if it crosses upwards and −1 if downwards. A 2001 formulation showed it needs no trigonometry and runs as fast as crossing counting.

For simple polygons, both rules always agree. They differ for **self-intersecting** polygons, where the edges cross each other.

%%compare The two inside rules on a five-pointed star drawn as one self-intersecting path. They agree on simple polygons and differ here.
[Even-odd rule]
Ray from the centre -> crosses 2 edges -> even
- Centre pentagon counts as outside
+ Matches crossing count directly
[Nonzero winding rule]
Ray from the centre -> 2 crossings, same direction -> winding 2
+ Centre pentagon counts as inside
+ Matches how most people read the shape
%%

A pentagram drawn as one continuous path has a pentagon in the middle. A ray from the centre crosses two edges, so even-odd says **outside** (a hollow star), while both crossings go the same way, so the winding number is 2 and nonzero says **inside** (a solid star). SVG's `fill-rule` exposes exactly these two options, `evenodd` and `nonzero`, and graphics libraries do the same.

For geofencing, self-intersecting polygons are almost always **data errors** (a zone someone drew with a twisted edge). The right response is usually to **validate polygons on input** (PostGIS has `ST_IsValid` and `ST_MakeValid`) rather than to pick a rule.

## Beyond a single test
Ray casting is O(n) per point. If you test **one polygon against millions of points**, preprocessing helps: split the polygon's bounding box into horizontal **slabs** at every vertex's y coordinate, sort the edges within each slab, and a point test becomes a binary search, O(log n). Or overlay a grid and mark cells that are fully inside, fully outside or on the boundary, which is the cell-covering idea from lesson 12.05 applied inside one polygon. The general lesson: do expensive work once per shape when the shape is tested many times.

Also remember that ray casting works on a **flat plane**. Latitude and longitude are not a plane, but for polygons a few kilometres across the error is negligible. For huge polygons, ones crossing the 180° meridian, or ones near the poles, use a spherical library (S2, or PostGIS geography types).

## In an interview
Give the rule (odd inside, even outside), then the reason in two sentences: the boundary separates inside from outside, the far end of the ray is outside, and each crossing flips the region. Mention the vertex problem and the half-open fix, the O(n) cost, and that you only run it on candidates after a bounding-box filter. If the interviewer asks about self-intersecting shapes, explain the winding number and the pentagram.

> Takeaway: cast a ray from the point and count edge crossings; odd means inside. By the Jordan curve theorem the boundary splits the plane into inside and outside, every crossing flips between them, and the far end of the ray is outside, so parity gives the answer. ## The requirement
A dating app shows profiles one at a time and the user swipes left or right. The product rule is strict: a profile the user has already swiped on must **never** appear again. Showing it twice looks broken, and showing someone you rejected is worse.

The scale makes this hard. An active user may swipe through **20,000 profiles** over a few months. The app has **50 million users**. Every time the recommendation service builds a batch of profiles to show (lesson 12.08), it starts with a few hundred **candidates** from nearby, then must remove every candidate this user has already seen, within a latency budget of a few milliseconds for this step.

So the question is a **set membership** question, asked hundreds of times per request: "is profile X in user U's seen set?" There are two ways to answer it: an exact set, or a probabilistic one.

## Option 1: an exact set
Store every seen profile ID per user and check membership directly. Choices:
- A **Redis set** per user (`SADD seen:u9 4471`, `SMISMEMBER seen:u9 ...` for a batch).
- A database table keyed by `(user_id, seen_id)`, which is also the durable swipe log.
- A **sorted set** scored by swipe time, if you want to expire very old swipes.

Exact sets give exact answers, with no surprises. The cost is **memory or I/O**.
- Raw size: 20,000 IDs × 8 bytes = **160 KB** for a heavy user. Redis's set encoding adds overhead: once a set exceeds a small size it becomes a hash table, at roughly 50 to 70 bytes per member in practice, so 20,000 members is over 1 MB.
- Across users: if the average user has seen 2,000 profiles, that is 50 million × 2,000 = **100 billion** seen pairs. At 8 bytes each that is 800 GB of raw IDs; with set overhead, several terabytes of RAM.
- If the sets live in a database instead, each recommendation request needs a batch lookup of 500 IDs against a large index: workable, but tens of milliseconds and heavy load at peak.

## Option 2: a Bloom filter
A **Bloom filter** (lesson 04.13 introduces it) is a compact probabilistic set. It can tell you an element is **definitely not** in the set, or **probably** in it.

### The structure
- A **bit array of m bits**, all initially 0.
- **k independent hash functions**, each mapping an element to one of the m positions.

### Operations
- **Add** an element: compute its k hashes and set those k bits to 1.
- **Test** an element: compute its k hashes and look at those bits. If **any** is 0, the element was **definitely never added**, because adding it would have set all of them. If **all** are 1, it **may** have been added, or those bits may all have been set by other elements.

%%flow A Bloom filter test: any zero bit proves "never added"; all ones only means "probably added".
Profile 4471 -(k = 7 hashes)-> Bits 12, 88, 301 ... -(any bit is 0)-> *Definitely not seen: show it
Profile 9020 -(k = 7 hashes)-> Bits 5, 140, 777 ... -(all bits are 1)-> Probably seen: skip
%%

In practice you do not need k truly independent hash functions. The standard trick computes two hashes h1 and h2 with a fast non-cryptographic function (MurmurHash, xxHash) and derives the rest as `h1 + i × h2` for i from 0 to k − 1. Standard Bloom filters **cannot delete** (clearing a bit might remove other elements too; **counting Bloom filters** store small counters instead of bits to allow it) and cannot **list** their contents.

### The maths
For n elements in m bits with k hashes, the false positive rate is approximately:

`p ≈ (1 − e^(−kn/m))^k`

The intuition: after n insertions of k bits each, the chance a given bit is still 0 is about e^(−kn/m). A false positive needs all k probed bits to be 1. The **optimal number of hashes** for a given m and n is `k = (m/n) ln 2`, which makes about half the bits 1. Turning it around, to reach a target rate p you need:

`m = −n ln p / (ln 2)²` bits, which is about **9.6 bits per element for 1%** and 14.4 bits per element for 0.1%.

! Example: sizing the filter
! Target: n = 20,000 seen profiles, false positive rate 1%.
! m = −20,000 × ln(0.01) / (ln 2)² = 20,000 × 4.605 / 0.4805 ≈ **191,700 bits ≈ 24 KB**.
! k = (191,700 / 20,000) × 0.693 ≈ 6.6, so **7 hash functions**.
! At 0.1%: about 287,500 bits (36 KB) and 10 hashes. At 0.01%: about 383,400 bits (48 KB) and 13 hashes. Each tenfold improvement in accuracy costs only about 12 KB more.
! Compare the exact set: 160 KB of raw IDs, over 1 MB as a Redis set. The Bloom filter is about 7 times smaller than the raw IDs and 40 times smaller than the Redis set.

%%bars Memory per heavy user (20,000 seen profiles). The Bloom filter is a fixed-size bit array; exact sets grow with overhead per member.
Redis set | 1200 | about 1.2 MB
Raw 8-byte IDs | 160 | 160 KB
Bloom, 0.01% | 48 | 48 KB
Bloom, 0.1% | 36 | 36 KB
*Bloom, 1% | 24 | 24 KB
%%

## The asymmetry decides the answer
The important property is not the size, it is the **direction of the errors**. A Bloom filter's errors are **one-sided**.
- **False negatives never happen.** If a profile was added, its k bits are 1 and stay 1 (nothing ever clears them). Every test of it returns "probably seen". So a profile the user has swiped is **never shown again**. The hard product rule is guaranteed.
- **False positives happen at the designed rate.** An **unseen** profile can test as "seen" because other profiles happened to set all of its bits. It is then **skipped**. The user never sees that one profile, which is a small loss (a missed potential match), but the rule "never show twice" is not broken.

Map that onto the product requirement: the requirement is about never showing seen profiles, which is exactly the guarantee a Bloom filter gives. The cost of its errors falls on the side that does not matter much: a few fresh profiles quietly disappear from the candidate pool. **So a Bloom filter fits**, provided the false positive rate is kept low and under control.

When would the false positives matter?
- **Small pools.** In a small town, or with narrow filters (age 34 to 36, within 5 km), a user may have only 300 eligible profiles in total. Losing 1% of them is 3 people, and if the user has seen 280 already, those 3 might be half of what is left. For such users, use an exact set; it is small anyway because the pool is small.
- **A filter that has overfilled.** The rate is only 1% at the designed n. Beyond that it climbs fast.

%%bars False positive rate of a filter sized for 20,000 items at 1% (24 KB, 7 hashes) as the user keeps swiping. Past the design size it degrades quickly.
10,000 swipes | 0.025 | 0.03%
*20,000 swipes | 1 | 1% as designed
30,000 swipes | 5.8 | 5.8%
40,000 swipes | 15.7 | 16%
60,000 swipes | 43.6 | 44%
%%

At 60,000 swipes, nearly half of all fresh candidates would be wrongly hidden. A user who swipes a lot would see their recommendations dry up for no visible reason. So you must handle growth.

## Practical design
### Where the filter lives
- **Per-user filter in Redis**, using the RedisBloom module (`BF.ADD`, `BF.MEXISTS` to test a batch of 500 in one call), or as a plain Redis string with `SETBIT` and `GETBIT` if you implement the hashing yourself.
- Or **loaded into the recommendation service's memory** when the user's session starts, and written back periodically. At 24 KB per active user, 2 million concurrently active users is about 48 GB, spread across the fleet.

### Handling growth
- **Scalable Bloom filters** chain several filters of growing size: when the current one reaches its design capacity, a new, larger one (with a tighter target rate) is added. Tests check all of them; adds go to the newest. The overall false positive rate stays bounded. RedisBloom's filters do this automatically when you set an expansion factor.
- **Size per user.** New users start with a small filter (say for 1,000 items, about 1.2 KB); heavy users get bigger ones. There is no reason to give 50 million users a 24 KB filter each when most will never swipe 20,000 times.
- **Rebuild periodically** from the durable swipe log, for example when a user crosses a threshold, so the filter is resized exactly.

### Source of truth
The Bloom filter is a **derived, disposable index**. The exact swipe log, a table of `(user, profile, direction, time)`, is the source of truth. It is needed anyway for matching (a match is two right swipes), analytics and moderation. If a filter is lost or corrupted, rebuild it from the log: for 20,000 rows, a few milliseconds of hashing.

### Write path ordering
When a user swipes, write the swipe log **first**, then add to the filter. If the second step fails, a background job can repair the filter from the log. The opposite order risks a filter entry with no log row, which is harmless here (a profile hidden that was not really seen), but the log-first order keeps the source of truth complete.

%%seq Building one batch of profiles with a per-user Bloom filter. Seen profiles are always removed; about 1% of unseen ones are removed too.
App > Recommender : next batch for u9
Recommender > Candidate index : 500 nearby profiles matching filters
Candidate index -> Recommender : 500 candidate IDs
Recommender > Redis : BF.MEXISTS seen:u9 (500 IDs)
Redis -> Recommender : 176 probably seen, 324 not seen
Recommender > Ranker : score the 324 fresh profiles
Ranker -> Recommender : top 20 in order
Recommender -> App : 20 profiles to show
%%

! Example: one request walked through
! User u9 has swiped 12,400 profiles. Their filter was sized for 20,000 at 1% (24 KB), so at 12,400 items its actual false positive rate is about 0.1%.
! Candidate generation returns 500 profiles near them. Of these, 175 really are profiles u9 has seen (heavy users exhaust their local pool quickly). The filter flags all 175, because there are no false negatives. Of the 325 unseen ones, at about 0.1% it is most likely to flag none or one by mistake; say 1. The remaining 324 go to the ranker. If the filter had been sized right at its limit (1%), it would have flagged about 3.
! The wrongly flagged profile is simply never shown to u9. Nobody can tell. An exact set would have avoided that tiny loss at about 7 to 40 times the memory.

### Belt and braces
If the cost of a mistake were higher (say, showing a profile the user **blocked**), you would not rely on any probabilistic structure. Blocks are few per user, so keep them in an exact set and check them separately. You can also do a final exact check of the 20 profiles actually being sent against the swipe log: 20 point lookups instead of 500.

## Common mistakes
- **Getting the direction backwards.** Some designs store "profiles not yet seen" in a Bloom filter. Then a false positive means "probably not seen" for a seen profile, and it gets shown again: the exact failure the requirement forbids. Always store the set whose false positives are harmless.
- **Fixed-size filters forever.** A filter sized at sign-up silently degrades as the user swipes. Monitor fill ratio (fraction of bits set) and resize.
- **Using it for things that must be exact.** Blocks, reports and legal removals need exact sets.
- **No source of truth.** If the only record of what a user has seen is the Bloom filter, you cannot rebuild, resize or debug it.
- **Cryptographic hashes.** SHA-256 is ten times slower than needed. Use MurmurHash or xxHash.

## In an interview
Name the trade-off clearly: exact sets cost memory proportional to swipes with overhead per entry; a Bloom filter costs about 10 bits per item at 1% and has one-sided errors. Then say the key sentence: "there are no false negatives, so a seen profile is never shown; a false positive only hides an unseen profile, which is a small, tunable loss". Size it with the formula, mention growth (scalable filters, rebuild from the swipe log), and give the small-pool exception. That shows you understand both the data structure and the product.

> Takeaway: a Bloom filter answers "definitely not seen" or "maybe seen" with no false negatives, so seen profiles are never shown again; false positives only hide some unseen profiles, a small, tunable loss. Size it with m = −n ln p / (ln 2)² and k = (m/n) ln 2 (about 24 KB and 7 hashes for 20,000 items at 1%), keep the exact swipe log as the source of truth, grow or rebuild the filter before it overfills, and use exact sets for small pools and for anything that must be exact, like blocks.

## The problem
You have a **catalogue** of millions or billions of items (videos, products, people, jobs, restaurants), one **user** with a history and a context (time, place, device), and a few tens of milliseconds to decide which 10 items to show. The best models for this are deep networks that look at hundreds of **features** (measurable properties) of the user, the item and the context, and predict something like "probability this user watches this video for more than 30 seconds".

Those models are accurate and expensive. Running one on every item in the catalogue, for every request, is impossible. This lesson explains the architecture every large recommender uses to get around that, the **multi-stage funnel**, why it is split the way it is, and how the same ideas apply to two-sided **matching**: dating, jobs and assigning drivers to riders.

## The multi-stage funnel
A modern recommender works in stages, each cheaper per item than the next and each looking at far fewer items.
1. **Candidate generation** (also called **retrieval**): narrow the huge corpus to a manageable set. On a video platform, from **billions** of videos to **hundreds or thousands**. This must be very fast per item, so it uses simple signals and precomputed structures. Several **generators** usually run in parallel, each nominating a different kind of candidate: "similar to what you watched", "popular near you", "from creators you follow", "trending now".
2. **Scoring** (or **ranking**): a precise model scores each candidate with rich features and orders them. It can afford to be expensive because it sees only hundreds of items.
3. **Re-ranking**: apply rules and constraints the model does not handle: remove items the user disliked or has already seen (lesson 12.07), boost fresh content, enforce **diversity** (not ten videos from one channel), apply **fairness** or business rules, insert ads.

%%bars Items considered at each stage of a typical video recommender for one request. Each stage cuts the set by one or more orders of magnitude.
Catalogue | 1000000000 | 1 billion videos
Candidate generation | 1000 | about 1,000
After filtering | 500 | about 500
Ranked by model | 500 | 500 scored
Re-ranked | 50 | top 50
*Shown | 10 | 10
%%

## Why split the stages: the cost arithmetic
Suppose the ranking model costs **1 ms of compute per item** (a deep network with a few hundred features; on a GPU much of this is batched, but the total compute is what matters).
- **Score the whole catalogue**: 10 million items × 1 ms = 10,000 CPU-seconds **per request**. At 10,000 requests per second, you would need 100 million cores. Impossible.
- **Retrieve, then rank**: an **approximate nearest neighbour** (ANN) search over 10 million item embeddings (lesson 09.13 covers vector search and HNSW) takes **a few milliseconds**. Then rank 500 candidates: 500 ms of compute, batched on a GPU in about 10 to 20 ms of wall-clock time. Total: about 30 ms.

The split puts compute where it pays off. It also lets each stage optimise a **different goal**:
- **Retrieval optimises recall.** Recall is the fraction of the truly good items that make it into the candidate set. If a great item is not retrieved, no ranker can show it. Precision (how many candidates are good) barely matters here; that is the ranker's job.
- **Ranking optimises precision at the top.** It must put the best 10 of the 500 in the right order. Metrics are things like **NDCG** (a score that rewards relevant items near the top) or click-through rate.
- **Different data.** Retrieval uses **embeddings** and indexes computed offline, perhaps daily. Ranking uses **fresh, request-time features**: time of day, device, what the user did 30 seconds ago, how many times this item was shown to them today.

A useful framing is that retrieval is **fast but coarse**, cutting millions of items to hundreds (a 99.99% reduction) and trading precision for efficiency; ranking is **slower but precise**, adding item, user and context features and bigger models because it sees so few items.

%%compare One big model versus the funnel. The funnel spends cheap compute on the many and expensive compute on the few.
[One stage]
Every item -(deep model)-> Sorted list
- 10,000 CPU seconds per request
- Cannot meet any latency budget
- One model must do everything
[*Retrieve then rank]
Every item -(ANN, few ms)-> 500 candidates -(deep model)-> Top 10
+ About 30 ms in total
+ Recall and precision tuned separately
+ Many generators feed one ranker
%%

## Offline versus online
The same architecture can be laid out on two axes: **offline versus online** (precomputed in batch jobs versus computed per request) and **retrieval versus ranking**.

%%matrix What happens where. Heavy learning and indexing run offline; requests only embed, look up and score.
Stage | Offline (batch jobs) | Online (per request)
Retrieval | Learn item embeddings, build ANN indexes, item-to-item maps, graphs | Embed the user and context, fetch nearest neighbours and graph neighbours
Ranking | Train ranking models, load features into a feature store | Join candidates with stored features, score with the model, return the order
%%

A **feature store** is a database of precomputed features keyed by user or item (for example "user's watch time per category over 7 days"), with a batch side used for training and a low-latency side used at serving. Using **the same feature definitions** for training and serving prevents **train-serve skew**: the model learning on one version of a feature and being served a subtly different one, which silently degrades results.

## How candidate generation works
### Embeddings and the two-tower model
An **embedding** is a vector of, say, 64 to 256 numbers that represents an item or user so that similar things have nearby vectors. The most common retrieval model is the **two-tower** model:
- An **item tower** (a neural network) turns item features into an item embedding.
- A **user tower** turns user history and context into a user embedding in the **same space**.
- Training pushes the user embedding close to the embeddings of items the user engaged with, and away from others. The score is just the **dot product** of the two vectors.

Because the towers are separate, you can embed every item **offline** and put the vectors into an ANN index. At request time you run only the user tower (one forward pass, about a millisecond) and ask the index for the nearest few hundred items.

%%flow A two-tower retrieval model: items are embedded offline into an ANN index; at request time only the user is embedded.
[Offline] Item features -(item tower)-> Item vectors -(build)-> *ANN index
[Online] User history and context -(user tower)-> User vector -(top 300 by dot product)-> *ANN index -> Candidates
%%

### Other generators
- **Item-to-item co-occurrence**: "people who watched A also watched B", computed offline from sessions and stored as a map from each item to its top 100 neighbours. Look up the user's last few items.
- **Graph walks**: build a weighted item graph from session behaviour, generate random walks, learn item embeddings from them (word2vec-style), and store nearest neighbours in an item-to-item map. Alibaba uses this approach before ranking with a deep network.
- **Search-style retrieval**: an inverted index plus a **knowledge graph** for query expansion. DoorDash expands a search for "KFC" to fried chicken and wings, so Popeyes and Bonchon are retrieved too.
- **Rules**: subscriptions, trending in the user's region, new releases.

Real examples of the funnel:
- **Facebook search** uses a two-tower model whose document embeddings are quantised into a **Faiss** ANN index; queries are embedded online, candidates retrieved by ANN plus Boolean filters, then ranked.
- **LinkedIn talent search** uses a first-level XGBoost model to retrieve and score; the top **1,000** candidates get extra features and a second-level model, and **125** results are shown.

## Ranking and re-ranking
The ranker takes each candidate, fetches its features (item statistics, user-item interaction counts, context), and predicts one or more outcomes: click, watch time, like, share, hide. Large systems predict several and combine them in a weighted **value function**, for example `0.3 × p(click) + 0.6 × expected watch minutes + 2.0 × p(share) − 5.0 × p(hide)`. The weights encode product priorities.

Re-ranking then applies list-level logic the per-item model cannot see:
- **Diversity**: at most 3 items per channel or seller, mix categories.
- **Freshness**: guarantee a slot for new uploads so they get a chance to collect data (exploration).
- **Deduplication** across generators, and the **seen filter** (lesson 12.07).
- **Policy**: remove borderline content, enforce age restrictions.

! Offline, nightly: train the two-tower model on the last 30 days of watches, embed 50 million videos (64 floats each, 256 bytes, so 12.8 GB) into an HNSW index, and refresh the feature store.
! Online, one request: three generators return 300 (two-tower ANN), 200 (subscriptions) and 100 (trending) candidates. Merge and deduplicate to 520; remove videos already watched, leaving 480. ! If the ranker alone had to score all 50 million videos at 1 ms each, one request would take about 14 hours of compute.

## Matching is two-sided
Recommendation shows items to a user. **Matching** pairs up two sides who both have preferences or constraints: riders and drivers, job seekers and employers, people on a dating app. Two things change.

### Both sides must want it
On a dating app, a good recommendation for A is someone A likes **and** who likes A. Candidate generation filters by **hard constraints** on both sides (location, each person's age range, the seen filter), and ranking predicts **mutual interest**, for example `p(A likes B) × p(B likes A)`. Re-ranking also **spreads attention**: if the most popular 5% of profiles were shown to everyone, they would be flooded and everyone else ignored, which is bad for both. Systems cap how often a profile is shown per day.

### Assignment, not just ranking
For ride-hailing, each driver can take only one rider. Ranking drivers for each rider independently and giving each rider their best one (**greedy matching**) is simple but can be globally poor. The alternative is **batched matching**: collect requests for a short window (a few seconds), then solve an **assignment problem** over all open riders and available drivers in an area, minimising the total pickup time. The classic exact method is the **Hungarian algorithm**, O(n³) for n riders and drivers, fine for the few hundred in one area per batch.

! Example: greedy versus batched assignment
! Two riders and two drivers. Pickup times: R1 to D1 is 2 minutes, R1 to D2 is 3 minutes, R2 to D1 is 3 minutes, R2 to D2 is 9 minutes.
! **Greedy**: R1 requests first and gets the nearest driver, D1 (2 min). R2 gets what is left, D2 (9 min). Total waiting: **11 minutes**, and R2 probably cancels.
! **Batched**: wait 2 seconds, then consider both riders together. Assign R1 to D2 (3 min) and R2 to D1 (3 min). Total: **6 minutes**, and nobody waits 9. R1 waits one minute longer than greedy would have given them, which is the price of a better global result.

%%seq Batched ride matching over a 2-second window. The matcher solves one assignment per area instead of one greedy choice per request.
Rider app > Matcher : request R1
Rider app > Matcher : request R2
Matcher > Location index : available drivers near R1 and R2
Location index -> Matcher : D1, D2 with positions
Matcher > ETA service : pickup times for all pairs
ETA service -> Matcher : 2 by 2 cost matrix
Matcher > Matcher : solve assignment, total 6 min
Matcher > Driver app : offer R1 to D2, R2 to D1
%%

The candidate generation for ride matching is the geospatial search from lesson 12.04 (drivers within a few kilometres), and the ranking is an **ETA model** that understands roads and traffic rather than straight-line distance. So the same retrieve-then-rank structure appears again, followed by a global assignment step.

## Common mistakes
- **One model for everything.** Skipping retrieval and scoring the catalogue does not scale; skipping ranking and showing ANN neighbours directly gives mediocre ordering.
- **Optimising the wrong stage metric.** Measuring retrieval by precision encourages tiny, safe candidate sets that starve the ranker. Measure retrieval by recall.
- **Train-serve skew.** Features computed one way in a batch job and another way at serving time. Share definitions through a feature store.
- **No exploration.** A system that only shows what it already knows works never learns about new items. Reserve slots for exploration.
- **Greedy matching in a two-sided market.** Locally best choices can be globally bad, as the ride example shows.

## In an interview
Draw the funnel first, with rough numbers at each stage, and justify it with the cost arithmetic. Name what runs offline (embeddings, ANN index, model training, feature computation) and online (user embedding, ANN lookup, feature fetch, scoring, re-ranking). Say that retrieval optimises recall and ranking optimises top-of-list precision. For matching problems, add the two-sided twist (mutual interest, attention spreading) and, for rides or deliveries, batched assignment over a short window.

> Takeaway: recommendation is a funnel. Candidate generation (fast, recall-oriented, often ANN over two-tower embeddings plus co-occurrence and rule-based generators) cuts millions of items to hundreds; ranking (slow, precise, rich fresh features) orders them; re-ranking enforces diversity, freshness and rules. The split exists because the precise model is far too expensive to run on the whole catalogue. Two-sided matching adds mutual-interest scoring and, where each side can take only one partner, a global assignment step.
